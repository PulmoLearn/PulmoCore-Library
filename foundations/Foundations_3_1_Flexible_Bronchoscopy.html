What Is Flexible Bronchoscopy?

A flexible bronchoscope is a thin, flexible tube with a miniature camera and a working channel that allows the RT and physician team to see directly inside the airways and perform procedures. It is inserted through the nose or mouth, passes through the larynx, and advances into the tracheobronchial tree.

Think Like an RT During Bronchoscopy

Bronchoscopy is not just a physician procedure that the RT watches. The scope can temporarily narrow the airway, sedation can depress ventilation, topical anesthesia can reduce protective reflexes, and sampling or therapeutic work can trigger coughing, bleeding, bronchospasm, or oxygen desaturation. The RT helps keep the procedure safe by preparing oxygen and suction, monitoring trends, recognizing deterioration early, and communicating clearly when the patient needs a pause or escalation.

Beginner anchor: Bronchoscopy has two broad purposes. Diagnostic bronchoscopy answers “What is the problem?” by visualizing and collecting samples. Therapeutic bronchoscopy answers “How can we relieve this airway problem?” by removing obstruction, opening narrowed airways, or clearing secretions.

Mallampati Airway Assessment

Before sedation, the RT and care team assess the patient's airway using the Mallampati classification. This tells you how much of the pharynx is visible when the patient opens wide and sticks out their tongue. The harder it is to see the airway, the harder emergency intubation may be if complications develop during sedation.

Safety clarification: Mallampati is a screening clue, not a complete airway assessment. A high Mallampati class should make the team look more carefully at mouth opening, neck mobility, jaw size, dentition, facial anatomy, obesity, history of difficult airway, and whether emergency airway equipment is immediately available.

Preparing the Patient for Bronchoscopy

Before inserting the scope, the airway must be numbed with topical anesthesia (lidocaine) to reduce discomfort, coughing, and the gag reflex. The method used depends on whether the patient already has an artificial airway or is awake and breathing on their own.

Preparation Is More Than Numbing the Airway

Before bronchoscopy, the team confirms consent, allergies, NPO status, baseline vital signs, oxygen needs, anticoagulant or bleeding risk, available suction, specimen containers, emergency airway equipment, and the patient’s ability to tolerate sedation. Topical lidocaine improves comfort, but preparation also protects the airway and prevents delays once the procedure starts.

Non-Intubated Patient (Awake Procedure)
  1. Lidocaine gel 2% — squeezed into nasal passages with sterile cotton swabs to numb the nares
  2. Lidocaine gargle 2% — patient gargles and swallows to numb the pharynx and throat
  3. Lidocaine nebulizer 4% — patient inhales nebulized lidocaine to numb the larynx, trachea, and proximal bronchi
Intubated Patient (Already Has Airway)
  • Lidocaine 1–2% instilled directly into the ET tube or tracheostomy before scope insertion
  • This numbs airways distal to the artificial airway
  • No nasal or throat preparation needed — airway is already secured
  • Reduces cough during scope advancement
Clinical Note — Lidocaine Toxicity: Monitor total lidocaine dose during preparation. Symptoms of toxicity include perioral tingling, dizziness, tinnitus, seizures, and cardiac effects. Your facility protocol will specify maximum safe dose (often 4–4.5 mg/kg).

Conscious Sedation Monitoring

During bronchoscopy, the patient receives conscious sedation — they are drowsy but still breathing on their own. The RT's job is continuous monitoring of five essential parameters. Any change in these values may indicate the patient is drifting toward hypoventilation or other complications.

Trend before crisis: SpO₂ can look acceptable for a short time even while ventilation is getting worse, especially if the patient is receiving supplemental oxygen. A rising ETCO₂, falling respiratory rate, shallow chest rise, snoring, or decreasing responsiveness may warn you before the saturation drops.

Recognizing and Managing Hypoventilation During Sedation

The most common complication of conscious sedation is hypoventilation — the patient's breathing becomes too slow or shallow to adequately ventilate. The RT must recognize the warning signs and act immediately.

During sedation, hypoventilation is usually a ventilation problem first. More oxygen may temporarily improve SpO₂, but it does not remove CO₂ or open an obstructed upper airway. The priority is to pause the procedure, stimulate the patient, optimize position, open the airway, and assist ventilation if needed.

Warning Signs
Respiratory rate dropping below 10, rising ETCO₂ above 50 mmHg, SpO₂ declining, patient difficult to rouse
Immediate Response
Stop the procedure, stimulate the patient, reposition airway, prepare to assist ventilation
Airway Maneuvers
Chin-lift opens the airway by lifting the soft tissues. Jaw-thrust advances the mandible to relieve obstruction. Both are hands-on interventions.

Diagnostic Bronchoscopy Sampling Techniques

Diagnostic bronchoscopy allows the team to collect samples from the airway or lungs to identify infections, evaluate tumors, and diagnose lung disease. There are five techniques, each suited to a different clinical need.

Choose the Sample Based on the Clinical Question

New students often memorize the names but miss the logic. Ask: Do we need fluid from deep alveoli, secretions from larger airways, cells from a visible lesion, tissue architecture, or a lymph node sample beyond the airway wall? The answer tells you which sampling method fits the situation.

Bronchoalveolar Lavage (BAL)

What happens: The scope is wedged into a small subsegmental bronchus, sealing it off. Saline is instilled into the airway beyond the scope tip, then suctioned back to collect cells from the alveolar level — the deepest part of the lung.

Best for: Suspected infections in immunocompromised patients, pneumocystis jiroveci (PCP), fungal infections, interstitial lung disease. Reaches the deepest sample of any bronchoscopy technique.

Key detail: The scope must be WEDGED (occluding the airway) to obtain a true BAL. If not wedged, it becomes bronchial washing instead.

Bronchial Washing

What happens: Small amounts of saline are instilled into a larger airway (segmental or lobar bronchus) and suctioned back as a sample. The scope is NOT wedged.

Best for: Initial infection screening, sputum-equivalent samples from patients who cannot produce sputum, general airway sampling when a specific segment is suspected.

Key detail: Samples larger airways. Less specific than BAL but easier to perform and useful as a first step.

Bronchial Brushing

What happens: A thin flexible brush catheter is advanced through the working channel of the scope. The brush is extended and rubbed directly across the surface of a visible lesion or suspicious area to collect cells.

Best for: Visible endobronchial lesions — collecting surface cells from a tumor or abnormality for cytology.

Key detail: Collects cells from the surface only — does not penetrate into the tissue. Best combined with biopsy for complete evaluation of a lesion.

Endobronchial Biopsy

What happens: Flexible biopsy forceps are advanced through the scope's working channel and used to pinch and remove small pieces of tissue from a visible lesion.

Best for: Getting actual tissue architecture — this is the gold standard for diagnosing endobronchial tumors and confirming malignancy type.

Key detail: Forceps only work on VISIBLE lesions. Requires intact tissue — this tells the pathologist not just what cells are present, but how they are organized. Small bleeding risk at the biopsy site.

Transbronchial Needle Aspiration (TBNA)

What happens: A needle tip is passed through the scope's working channel and pushed through the airway wall to aspirate cells from adjacent structures — lymph nodes, mediastinal masses, or tumors adjacent to central airways.

Best for: Sampling areas that cannot be directly seen — mediastinal lymph nodes (important for lung cancer staging), paratracheal masses.

Key detail: Unlike biopsy, TBNA goes THROUGH the airway wall. Modern EBUS (endobronchial ultrasound)-guided TBNA uses real-time imaging to precisely target nodes.

Specimen Identity Is a Patient-Safety Issue

Before the sample leaves the procedure area, verify the patient, specimen type, exact anatomic site, collection method, requested studies, container or preservative, and transport priority. BAL for microbiology, brushing for cytology, and biopsy for histopathology are not interchangeable. Follow facility policy for bedside labeling and chain of custody.

Therapeutic Bronchoscopy Procedures

Therapeutic bronchoscopy is used when airway obstruction or pathology needs active treatment rather than just sampling. A CT scan is usually done first to map all areas of obstruction before the procedure begins.

Match the Tool to the Obstruction

Therapeutic bronchoscopy decisions depend on what is blocking the airway. Tumor tissue may need heat or cold. A narrowed airway may need dilation or a stent. A foreign body needs removal. Thick secretions need suction and clearance. The RT supports oxygenation, suction, equipment setup, and rapid communication if bleeding, bronchospasm, or hypoxemia develops.

Case Complete — Key Takeaways

Mallampati: Class 4 = only soft palate visible = highest risk. Always prepare emergency airway plan before sedation.
Preparation: Non-intubated patients need gel + gargle + nebulizer. Intubated patients need instillation through artificial airway only.
Hypoventilation: Rising ETCO₂ + falling RR + declining SpO₂ = act immediately. Never place supine. Head up, airway maneuvers, BVM ready.
BAL: Best for deepest lung tissue sampling — immunocompromised, PCP, fungal. Scope must be wedged to perform true BAL.

Post-Procedure Recovery and Reassessment

Bronchoscopy safety does not end when the scope comes out. Continue oxygenation, ventilation, vital-sign, rhythm, consciousness, airway, breath-sound, bleeding, and pain assessment until the patient returns to an appropriate baseline. Verify gag and swallowing reflex recovery according to policy before oral intake and provide clear instructions about when eating and drinking may resume.

Airway protection
Keep the patient NPO until gag reflex and throat sensation return per policy.
Respiratory status
Trend SpO₂, RR, ETCO₂ if available, breath sounds, cough strength, and secretion clearance.
Escalate
Report persistent hypoxemia, increasing work of breathing, unilateral chest pain, diminished breath sounds, significant hemoptysis, hemodynamic change, or worsening mental status.
Document objectively
Record route, support, monitoring trends, specimens, complications, interventions, response, communication, education, and disposition readiness.

FAQ

Common Bronchoscopy Questions

Is a patient fully asleep during bronchoscopy?

Not usually. Most bronchoscopies use conscious sedation — the patient is drowsy and comfortable but still breathing independently. General anesthesia may be used for complex therapeutic procedures. The RT monitors breathing continuously because sedation can suppress the respiratory drive.

Why does Mallampati matter if the patient is already sedated?

The Mallampati score predicts how hard it will be to intubate the patient IF something goes wrong during sedation. If the patient becomes apneic or has severe hypoventilation, the team may need to secure the airway emergently. Knowing the class ahead of time means equipment and personnel are ready before any crisis develops.

What is the difference between BAL and bronchial washing?

Both use saline to collect cells from the airways, but the key difference is scope position and depth. In BAL, the scope is wedged into a small subsegmental bronchus, sealing it off and collecting cells from the deepest alveolar level. In bronchial washing, the scope is NOT wedged, and saline is instilled into larger, more proximal airways. BAL reaches deeper and is the preferred technique for immunocompromised patients with suspected deep lung infections.

Why can you only use thermal ablation with low FiO₂?

Thermal ablation uses electrocautery or laser energy to generate heat inside the airway. At high oxygen concentrations (high FiO₂), the airway becomes an oxygen-enriched environment where any spark or ignition can cause a fire or explosion. This is not a theoretical risk — airway fires during laser bronchoscopy are a documented serious complication. The team reduces FiO₂ to the minimum safe level before activating thermal energy.

What happens after a diagnostic bronchoscopy?

Patients are monitored in recovery for 1–2 hours while sedation wears off. The throat numbness from lidocaine typically resolves within 1–2 hours. Patients must not eat or drink until the numbness is fully gone to prevent aspiration. Once cleared, outpatients can go home. Follow-up is usually 1 week later to discuss results.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Flexible bronchoscopy requires a complete safety loop: clarify the indication, review risk, prepare the patient and equipment, assess the airway, monitor oxygenation and ventilation separately, match the procedure to the clinical question, recognize complications early, protect the recovering airway, and document the objective response.

Mallampati Rule:
Classes 1 and 2 are easy. Class 3 is moderate. Class 4 is highest risk — only soft palate visible. ALWAYS prepare emergency airway equipment before sedating any Class 3 or 4 patient.
Sedation Pearl:
Monitor SpO₂, HR, RR, BP, and ETCO₂ continuously. Rising ETCO₂ + falling RR + dropping SpO₂ = hypoventilation developing. Intervene immediately. Never position patient supine.
Diagnostic Pearl:
BAL = deepest (alveolar level, scope wedged). Washing = larger airways. Brushing = lesion surface cells. Biopsy = full tissue architecture. TBNA = needle through wall for lymph nodes.
Therapeutic Pearl:
Heat (ablation) = low FiO₂ required because of airway fire risk. Cold (cryo) freezes tissue. Stents hold narrowed airways open. Balloon dilation widens stenosis. Foreign body removal and secretion clearance restore patency by removing the obstruction.
Recovery pearl:
New unilateral pain, diminished breath sounds, and rising oxygen need after biopsy suggest pneumothorax.
Documentation pearl:
“Tolerated well” is incomplete without objective pre/post findings and complications.
