Gathering Clinical Clues

A lab result, breath sound, sputum color, or x-ray finding is a clue—not a diagnosis by itself. Learn to compare each finding with what is expected, decide what it means physiologically, connect it with the rest of the patient, and recognize which abnormalities need action first.

Your interpretation routine
1 · VERIFYIs the finding reliable and current?
2 · COMPARENormal, high, low, or new change?
3 · EXPLAINWhat physiology could cause it?
4 · CORRELATEWhat other findings support or contradict it?
5 · PRIORITIZEWhat threatens airway, oxygenation, ventilation, perfusion, or neurologic status?
6 · REASSESSWhat should be checked next?
Avoid the one-clue diagnosis: Green sputum does not prove a bacterial infection. Crackles do not prove pneumonia. A high WBC does not prove infection. A normal SpO₂ does not prove normal oxygen delivery. Strong conclusions come from clusters of consistent findings.
Quick Reference — keep open while practicing
Always use the reference range printed with the patient's actual results. The values below are common adult reference ranges to help you recognize the expected magnitude and direction of an abnormality. Laboratories may use different analyzers, methods, units, populations, and cutoffs, so a value should ultimately be classified as high, low, or normal using the reference interval reported by that laboratory.

Selected Cardiac Biomarkers

TestCommon reference conceptImportant interpretation note
BNPOften <100 pg/mL is considered within a common reference rangeAge, obesity, renal function, assay, and clinical setting affect interpretation. Use the range on the report.
NT-proBNPAge-dependent and assay-dependentDo not substitute an NT-proBNP cutoff for a BNP cutoff; use the laboratory's reported reference interval and the patient's clinical context.
TroponinAssay-specific; compare with the laboratory's upper reference limit/99th percentileA value above the reference limit indicates myocardial injury, but the cause requires symptoms, ECG, serial trend, and clinical evaluation.

Reference-range rule: These are common adult examples for learning. The patient's laboratory report is the source of truth for whether a result is flagged high, low, or within range. Reference intervals can vary with laboratory method, units, age, sex, altitude, and clinical population.
