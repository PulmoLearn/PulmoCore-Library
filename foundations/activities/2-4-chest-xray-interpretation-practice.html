Chest X-Ray Interpretation Practice

A chest radiograph becomes much easier when you stop trying to “spot the diagnosis” and instead use the same viewing sequence every time. In this activity you will check image quality first, scan the image systematically, identify abnormal patterns, and then connect the radiograph with the patient.

0% complete
The repeatable routine

Do not jump straight to the lung fields.

1 · VERIFYCorrect patient, date, image, marker, and view.
2 · QUALITYRotation, inspiration, penetration/exposure, positioning.
3 · SCANAirway → bones → cardiac → diaphragm → pleura → fields → devices.
4 · CORRELATEWhat patient findings support or contradict what you see?
Why quality comes first: Rotation can distort the mediastinum and heart. Low lung volumes can make the heart and markings look falsely prominent. Poor penetration can hide a pneumothorax or simulate increased markings. If the film is technically limited, your interpretation must say so.
Station 1 · Before the image

Look at the patient first.

C2C3C5

The chest x-ray is one piece of the decision. Before you focus on the image, ask whether the patient is showing an immediate threat to airway, oxygenation, ventilation, perfusion, or neurologic status.

Increased work of breathingAccessory-muscle use, retractions, inability to speak full sentences, diaphoresis, or severe tachypnea indicate increased respiratory demand.
Respiratory fatigueA patient who was breathing rapidly but then becomes slow, shallow, confused, or somnolent may be tiring—not improving.
Oxygenation problemLow SpO₂ or PaO₂ points toward inadequate oxygenation, but always interpret the value with FiO₂ and the patient.
Ventilation problemRising PaCO₂ or ETCO₂ with drowsiness, shallow breathing, or declining respiratory effort suggests inadequate ventilation.
Refractory hypoxemiaPersistent hypoxemia despite a properly functioning high-concentration oxygen device is an escalation trigger.
Upper-airway emergencyNew inspiratory stridor and retractions after extubation suggest upper-airway narrowing and require immediate evaluation.
Patient before picture: If the patient is unstable, do not delay stabilization while completing a radiograph interpretation. Use the image to help explain the physiology and guide next steps—not to postpone care.
Station 2

Know what makes the image black, gray, and white.

C1C4
AirMost radiolucent → darkest.
Fluid / soft tissueIntermediate gray.
BoneMore radiopaque → lighter.
Metal / dense materialMost radiopaque → brightest white.
Key languageRadiolucent = darker. Radiopaque = whiter.
Clinical payoffFluid-filled alveoli become whiter; pleural air becomes darker and loses lung markings.

Station 4

Walk the film in the same order every time.

C1C4
AAirway
Follow trachea to carina and main bronchi. Midline? Narrowed? Shifted? Tube present?
BBones & soft tissue
Trace ribs, clavicles, spine, shoulders, and soft tissues. Look for fractures, deformity, subcutaneous air, and artifacts.
CCardiac silhouette & mediastinum
On a PA film, cardiothoracic ratio > 0.50 suggests enlargement. Remember: AP films magnify the heart.
DDiaphragm
Right hemidiaphragm is usually slightly higher. Check contour, gastric bubble, and lung volume.
EEffusions / pleura
Check both costophrenic angles and the periphery for pleural lines, fluid, or absent markings.
FFields
Compare side-to-side and top-to-bottom. Is the problem focal, diffuse, alveolar, interstitial, too lucent, or too opaque?
+Lines, tubes & devices
Finish by tracing every device to its tip. A device is not “present”; it is either correctly or incorrectly positioned.
Station 5 · Airway

Tracheal shift: pushed away or pulled toward?

C1C4

Think about volume and pressure.

Pushes awayLarge pleural effusion, tension pneumothorax, or a large mass can create space/pressure and push the mediastinum away.
Pulls towardAtelectasis, lobectomy/pneumonectomy, or fibrosis can reduce volume and pull structures toward the affected side.
Do not use deviation alone. Ask: Is the affected hemithorax whiter or darker? Is there volume loss or volume gain? Are peripheral lung markings present?
Station 6 · Diaphragm & lung volume

Count ribs, then look at the diaphragm.

C1C4

Normal inspiration: approximately 9–10 posterior ribs visible above the diaphragm is a useful adult target.

Reduced lung volumeFewer ribs visible, elevated diaphragms, crowding of markings. Causes include suboptimal inspiration and restrictive or volume-loss processes.
HyperinflationMore ribs visible, flattened diaphragms, increased lucency, expanded retrosternal space on lateral films. Common with COPD and sometimes acute asthma.
Call what you see reduced lung volumes unless you know the patient simply failed to inspire. Do not dismiss a low-volume film as “poor effort” without clinical context.
Station 7 · Pleura

Air and fluid behave differently.

C1C4C5
Pleural effusionFluid increases opacity and can blunt the costophrenic angle. Small effusions may be easier to see on a lateral or decubitus view.
PneumothoraxLook for a visceral pleural line with no peripheral lung markings beyond it. On supine films, a deep sulcus may be a clue.
Tension physiology is a patient emergency, not an image quiz. Severe distress, hypotension, unilateral absent breath sounds, and mediastinal shift require immediate recognition and escalation.
Station 8 · Lung fields

Describe the pattern before naming the disease.

C1C4

Use four questions:

1. Focal or diffuse?One lobe/segment or both lungs?
2. More opaque or more lucent?Is tissue becoming whiter or darker?
3. Alveolar or interstitial?Alveolar disease often has fluffy opacity and air bronchograms; interstitial disease may show lines, dots, or a reticulonodular pattern.
4. What else is present?Heart size, effusions, volume loss, hyperinflation, devices, and clinical findings help narrow the cause.
Air bronchogram: air-filled bronchi become visible because surrounding alveoli are filled with fluid, pus, blood, or other material and therefore become more opaque.
Station 9 · Lines, tubes & devices

Trace the whole device to the tip.

C1C3C5

For an endotracheal tube, find the carina first, then locate the tube tip relative to it. Patient position and neck movement can change apparent tip location.

Teaching target: the ETT tip is commonly expected several centimeters above the carina in a neutral head position. Always use local policy and the radiology report for final confirmation.
Too deep: right mainstem placement can preferentially ventilate the right lung and cause left-sided volume loss. Too high: the tube may be at risk for accidental extubation.
