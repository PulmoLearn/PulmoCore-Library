Monitor Detective: Oximetry & Capnography

Monitors give you clues—not diagnoses. In this activity, you will learn to decide what each monitor is actually telling you, whether the signal is trustworthy, what changed physiologically, and what you should do next. The biggest idea for this week: oxygenation and ventilation are different problems.

7 · RECHECKDid the patient improve?
False reassurance trap: Supplemental oxygen can keep SpO₂ looking acceptable while a patient hypoventilates, retains CO₂, becomes somnolent, and moves toward respiratory failure.
The foundation

Two monitors. Two different questions.

Pulse Oximetry → Oxygenation

SpO₂ estimates the percentage of functional hemoglobin carrying oxygen. It does not directly tell you PaO₂, PaCO₂, ventilation, oxygen content, or hemoglobin concentration.

Think: “How well is hemoglobin being oxygenated?”

Capnography → Ventilation + Perfusion Clues

ETCO₂ is the exhaled CO₂ measured at the end of expiration. The number and waveform help you assess ventilation, airway patency, respiratory pattern, and changes in pulmonary perfusion.

Think: “Is CO₂ reaching the lungs and being exhaled effectively?”
Say the category before the number: “SpO₂ is oxygenation.” “ETCO₂ is ventilation/perfusion.” This simple habit prevents a surprising number of interpretation errors.
Quick Reference — open while you practice
SpO₂Healthy adults at sea level are commonly in the mid-to-upper 90s, but context matters.
ETCO₂Usual adult reference range is approximately 35–45 mmHg.
High ETCO₂Think reduced effective ventilation, increased metabolism/CO₂ load, or rebreathing depending on waveform and context.
Low ETCO₂Think increased ventilation, decreased perfusion, increased dead space, leak/disconnection, or sampling problem.
SpO₂ reliabilityCheck patient, pleth/pulse agreement, perfusion, motion, probe placement, ambient light, coverings, and dyshemoglobinemia.
Airway placementPersistent waveform capnography is the strongest immediate bedside evidence supporting tracheal placement.
