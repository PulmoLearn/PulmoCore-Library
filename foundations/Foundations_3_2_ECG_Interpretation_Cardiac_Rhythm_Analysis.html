ECG Interpretation & Cardiac Rhythm Analysis

Respiratory therapists must recognize normal and life-threatening cardiac rhythms, calculate heart rate, and understand when to escalate care. This lesson builds systematic ECG reading skills — from paper basics through deadly rhythms and clinical decision-making.

ECG Paper, Waveforms & Intervals

An ECG records electrical activity through the heart as a series of waves on graph paper. Understanding the paper grid and what each waveform represents is the foundation of rhythm interpretation.

ECG Paper Time Values

MeasurementTime ValueVisual Cue
1 small box0.04 secondsThin grid line
5 small boxes = 1 large box0.20 secondsThick red/dark line
5 large boxes1 second5 thick lines
30 large boxes6 secondsFull strip for rate counting

The Five ECG Waveforms

P
Atrial Depolarization

Small rounded hump before QRS. Should be upright and uniform.

Q
Initial Septal Depolarization

Small downward deflection at start of QRS. May be absent normally.

R
Ventricular Depolarization

Tall upward peak. The tallest wave in the QRS complex.

S
Late Ventricular Depolarization

Downward deflection after R wave. Completes the QRS.

T
Ventricular Repolarization

Rounded hump after QRS. Should be upright and not too tall.

The Four-Step Systematic Analysis

Every ECG strip should be read using the same four steps in order. This prevents missed findings and ensures consistent, accurate interpretation every time.

Step 1 — Assess Rate
Determine atrial rate and ventricular rate. Is the rhythm fast, slow, or normal? Use one of the four rate calculation methods (covered in next section).
Step 2 — Identify Waveforms
Are P waves present? Upright and uniform? Is there one P before each QRS? Are QRS complexes present and similar in shape? Are T waves normal?
Step 3 — Measure Intervals & Segments
Measure PR interval, QRS duration, and QT interval. Check ST segment — is it at baseline, elevated, or depressed?
Step 4 — Interpret & State Clinical Significance
Name the rhythm. Include pacemaker site and mechanism. Example: "Sinus bradycardia at 38 beats/min — patient is hypotensive, intervention needed."
Regularity Check: Before naming the rhythm, check R-R intervals. If they differ by 2 small boxes or less — the rhythm is regular. If they differ by more than 2 small boxes — the rhythm is irregular. This changes which rate calculation method you use.

Which Step Comes Next?

Place the four analysis steps in the correct order.

Assess atrial and ventricular rate — fast, slow, or normal?
Measure PR interval, QRS duration, QT interval and assess ST segment.
Look for P waves, QRS complexes, and T waves — present? Normal appearance?
Name the rhythm, state pacemaker site, and describe clinical significance.

Heart Rate Calculation Methods

There are four methods for calculating heart rate from an ECG strip. The correct method depends on whether the rhythm is regular (consistent R-R intervals) or irregular (varying R-R intervals).

Six-Second Method

Best for: Irregular rhythms or any rhythm

Count QRS complexes in 6 sec × 10

Count QRS complexes in a 6-second strip (30 large boxes), then multiply by 10 to get beats per minute.

Large Box Method

Best for: Regular rhythms only

300 ÷ large boxes between R-R

Count large boxes between two consecutive R waves. Divide 300 by that number. Example: 4 large boxes = 300 ÷ 4 = 75 bpm.

Small Box Method

Best for: Regular rhythms (precise)

1500 ÷ small boxes between R-R

Count small boxes between two consecutive R waves. Divide 1500 by that number. Example: 18 small boxes = 1500 ÷ 18 = 83 bpm.

Sequence Method

Best for: Regular rhythms (fastest)

300 → 150 → 100 → 75 → 60 → 50

Place R wave on a dark line. Count the next dark lines as 300, 150, 100, 75, 60, 50. The number at the next R wave is the rate.

Sinus Rhythms

Sinus rhythms originate in the sinoatrial (SA) node — the heart's natural pacemaker. All sinus rhythms share a key feature: a P wave before every QRS complex, meaning the impulse follows the normal conduction pathway.

Normal Sinus Rhythm (NSR) 60–100 bpm
FeatureFinding
RateRegular; 60–100 bpm (both atrial and ventricular)
P wavesPresent before every QRS; uniform; upright
QRSNormal (<0.12 sec); unchanged
PR interval0.12–0.20 sec (normal)

Clinical significance: Normal. Continue monitoring. No intervention needed unless clinical signs suggest otherwise.

Sinus Bradycardia <60 bpm
FeatureFinding
RateRegular; <60 bpm
P wavesPresent before every QRS; uniform; upright
QRSNormal (<0.12 sec); unchanged
PR interval0.12–0.20 sec (normal)

Common causes: Vagal stimulation, vomiting, hypokalemia, hypothyroidism, hypoxia, beta-blockers, athletic training, right ventricular MI, sleep.

Management: Give oxygen. If vagal cause — give atropine. If unstable — transcutaneous pacing, epinephrine, or dopamine drip.

Sinus Tachycardia >100 bpm
FeatureFinding
RateRegular; >100 bpm
P wavesPresent before every QRS; may merge with preceding T wave at high rates
QRSNormal (<0.12 sec); unchanged
PR interval0.12–0.20 sec (may shorten at high rates)

Common causes: Exercise, pain, anxiety, fever, hypoxia, bleeding, hypovolemia, heart failure, PE, MI, anemia, thyrotoxicosis, shock, sympathomimetic drugs.

Management: Give oxygen. Try vagal maneuver (if stable). Treat underlying cause. If HR >150 and unstable — synchronized cardioversion.

Atrial Rhythms

Atrial rhythms originate outside the SA node but still within the atria. The key distinction: P waves are abnormal, absent, or replaced by flutter or fibrillation waves. QRS complexes are usually narrow unless there is also a bundle branch block.

Atrial Flutter Atrial 250–400 bpm / Ventricular 60–150 bpm
FeatureFinding
Atrial rateRegular; 250–400 bpm
Ventricular rateIrregular; usually 60–150 bpm (depends on AV block ratio)
P wavesAbsent — replaced by sawtooth "F" waves
QRSNormal (<0.12 sec) unless aberrant conduction
PR intervalNot measurable

Key identifier: The sawtooth (zigzag) baseline between QRS complexes — those are flutter (F) waves firing rapidly from the atria. The AV node only lets some through (often 2:1 or 4:1 block).

Management: Synchronized cardioversion if serious. Patients may complain of palpitations, difficulty breathing, fatigue, or chest discomfort.

Atrial Fibrillation (AFib) Irregularly Irregular
FeatureFinding
Atrial rateCannot be measured — chaotic fibrillation ("f") waves only
Ventricular rateIrregularly irregular — R-R intervals constantly vary
P wavesAbsent — replaced by small chaotic "f" waves creating a wavy baseline
QRSNormal (<0.12 sec) unless aberrant conduction
PR intervalNot measurable

Risk: Blood pools in the fibrillating atria → clot formation → stroke/embolism risk. Long-term management includes anticoagulation.

Causes: Cardiovascular disease, alcohol, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, obesity, COPD, sleep apnea, PE.

Management: Rate control (slow ventricular rate) OR rhythm control (restore sinus rhythm). Synchronized cardioversion if serious.

Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT) 150–250 bpm
FeatureFinding
RateRegular; 150–250 bpm
P wavesDifficult to see or hidden behind QRS at high rates
QRSNormal (<0.12 sec) — narrow QRS distinguishes SVT from ventricular tachycardia
PR intervalNot measurable

Key identifier: Very fast rate (150–250 bpm) with narrow QRS complexes. P waves hidden in or immediately after QRS. Rate is too fast to fill ventricles adequately — can cause hemodynamic compromise.

Management: Give oxygen. Try vagal maneuver. Give adenosine if stable. Synchronized cardioversion if unstable.

Ventricular Rhythms

Ventricular rhythms originate below the AV node in the ventricles. Because the impulse does not follow the normal His-Purkinje pathway, QRS complexes are wide and bizarre (≥0.12 sec). P waves are absent or hidden. These rhythms always demand immediate attention.

Monomorphic Ventricular Tachycardia (MVT) >150 bpm, Wide QRS
FeatureFinding
RateRegular; >150 bpm
P wavesObscured — buried in wide QRS
QRSWide and bizarre (≥0.12 sec); all QRS complexes the same shape (monomorphic)
PR intervalAbsent

Causes: Acid-base imbalance, acute coronary syndromes, cardiomyopathy, cocaine, digitalis toxicity, hypokalemia, hyperkalemia, shock.

Signs/symptoms: Altered mental status, chest pain, hypotension, acute heart failure, pulmonary congestion, SOB.

Management: If pulseless — CPR + defibrillation. If stable but symptomatic — O₂ + amiodarone. If unstable (HR ≥150) — O₂, IV access, sedation, synchronized cardioversion.

Polymorphic Ventricular Tachycardia (PMVT) 250–350 bpm, Variable QRS
FeatureFinding
RateIrregular/chaotic; 250–350 bpm
P wavesObscured
QRSWide, bizarre, variable — QRS complexes are NOT identical (polymorphic = many shapes)
PR intervalAbsent

Key distinction from MVT: QRS complexes change shape and amplitude — they are not all the same. Includes Torsades de Pointes (associated with prolonged QT interval).

Management: Rarely hemodynamically stable. Defibrillation if pulseless or unstable. Different underlying causes require different specific treatments.

Deadly Rhythms — Ventricular Fibrillation & Asystole

These two rhythms produce no cardiac output. The patient is unresponsive, apneic, and pulseless. Recognition must be immediate — every second without CPR worsens outcome.

Ventricular Fibrillation (VFib)

RateAbsent — no organized rhythm
P wavesAbsent
QRSAbsent — chaotic waveforms only

What you see: Completely disorganized, chaotic waveforms of constantly varying shape and amplitude. The heart muscle is quivering — not contracting.

Treatment: CPR + DEFIBRILLATION
Defibrillation delivers unsynchronized shock to reset the chaotic electrical activity.

Asystole

RateAbsent
P wavesAbsent
QRSAbsent — flatline

What you see: A flat line (isoelectric line) with no electrical activity. This is true asystole — no impulse generation anywhere in the heart.

Treatment: CPR ONLY (NOT defibrillation)
Defibrillation does NOT work on asystole. Check for lead disconnect if no pulse is found.

Reversible Causes — The 5 Hs and 5 Ts

Before or during resuscitation, consider and treat reversible causes. Memorizing these is an NBRC and ACLS requirement.

5 Hs
  • Hypovolemia
  • Hypoxia
  • Hypothermia
  • Hypo- / Hyperkalemia
  • Hydrogen ion (Acidosis)
5 Ts
  • Tamponade (cardiac)
  • Tension pneumothorax
  • Thrombosis — lungs (PE)
  • Thrombosis — coronary (MI)
  • Tablets / toxins (overdose)

Scenarios Complete — Key Takeaways

Scenario 1 lesson: Sinus bradycardia → oxygen + atropine. After treatment, mild sinus tachycardia → monitor and re-evaluate. Not every fast rhythm needs immediate intervention.
Scenario 2 lesson: Asystole → CPR only (not defibrillation). VFib during CPR → immediately defibrillate. Know your shockable vs. non-shockable rhythms cold.
Scenario 3 lesson: Stable VT with pulse → oxygen + amiodarone. VT deteriorates to VFib → defibrillate. ROSC restores NSR → continue monitoring. This is the classic VT → VFib → ROSC progression.
Core rule: Shockable rhythms = VFib and pulseless VT. Non-shockable rhythms = Asystole and PEA. Only defibrillate when you have a shockable rhythm.

FAQ

Common ECG Questions

What is the difference between defibrillation and synchronized cardioversion?

Defibrillation delivers an unsynchronized shock — it fires immediately without regard to where the heart is in its cycle. It is used for pulseless rhythms (VFib, pulseless VT) where there is no organized rhythm to synchronize with. Synchronized cardioversion times the shock to coincide with the QRS complex, avoiding the vulnerable T-wave period. It is used for organized but unstable rhythms where the patient still has a pulse (unstable SVT, unstable AFib, unstable VT with pulse).

How do I tell VT from SVT on the strip?

The key differentiator is the QRS width. SVT comes from above the ventricles — it follows normal conduction pathways, so the QRS is NARROW (<0.12 sec). VT comes from the ventricles — it bypasses the normal His-Purkinje system, so the QRS is WIDE and BIZARRE (≥0.12 sec). Both can be fast and regular, so always check the QRS width first.

Why can't you defibrillate asystole?

Defibrillation works by delivering a massive shock that simultaneously depolarizes all cardiac cells — essentially "resetting" the heart so the SA node can take over. For this to work, there must be some chaotic electrical activity happening (like VFib). Asystole means there is NO electrical activity at all — there is nothing to reset. Shocking a flat line won't create activity; it can only reset existing chaotic activity. Always check leads are connected before concluding true asystole.

What does "irregularly irregular" mean for AFib?

In most arrhythmias, even irregular ones, there is some pattern to the irregularity. AFib is different — the R-R intervals have NO pattern. They are completely random. No two consecutive R-R intervals are the same length, and there is no repeating cycle. This "irregularly irregular" pattern is the hallmark of AFib and how you distinguish it from other irregular rhythms like second-degree heart block.

Why does the sequence method use 300, 150, 100, 75, 60, 50?

These numbers come from dividing 300 by whole numbers (300÷1=300, 300÷2=150, 300÷3=100, 300÷4=75, 300÷5=60, 300÷6=50). Since one large box = 0.20 seconds and 5 large boxes = 1 second, there are 300 large boxes in a minute. So if there is 1 large box between R waves, the rate is 300 bpm; 2 large boxes = 150 bpm; and so on. It is the large box method expressed as a memorized sequence.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

ECG Paper:
1 small box = 0.04 sec. 1 large box = 0.20 sec. 5 large boxes = 1 sec. 30 large = 6 sec (rate strip).
Rate Formulas:
Small boxes: 1500 ÷ boxes. Large boxes: 300 ÷ boxes. Six-sec: QRS count × 10. Irregular → use six-second method.
Sinus Rhythms:
P before every QRS. Brady <60 → atropine. Tachy >100 → treat cause. NSR 60–100 → monitor.
Atrial Rhythms:
Flutter = sawtooth F waves. AFib = irregularly irregular, no P waves. SVT = narrow QRS, 150–250 bpm.
Ventricular Rhythms:
Wide bizarre QRS ≥0.12 sec. Mono VT (regular) → amiodarone if pulse. Poly VT (irregular) → defibrillation.
Deadly Rhythms:
VFib = chaotic, no QRS → SHOCK. Asystole = flatline, no activity → CPR ONLY. Know the 5 Hs and 5 Ts.
NBRC Exam Tip — Shockable vs. Non-Shockable: VFib and pulseless VT = Shockable (defibrillation). Asystole and PEA = Non-shockable (CPR only). This distinction is tested directly on the NBRC exam and in every ACLS scenario.
