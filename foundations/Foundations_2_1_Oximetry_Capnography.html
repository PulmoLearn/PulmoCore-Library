Monitoring Gas Exchange: Oxygenation, Ventilation, and Perfusion

Pulse oximetry and capnography answer different questions. Pulse oximetry estimates oxygen saturation. Capnography measures exhaled carbon dioxide and displays a waveform. Co-oximetry directly measures hemoglobin species such as O₂Hb and COHb.

Start With the Clinical Question

Before choosing a monitor, ask what you are trying to learn. If you need a saturation trend, use pulse oximetry. If you need to know whether abnormal hemoglobin is present, use co-oximetry. If you need real-time information about ventilation, perfusion, airway placement, or CPR quality, use capnography.

Pulse ox
Noninvasive oxygen saturation trend.
Co-oximetry
Direct blood measurement of O₂Hb, COHb, and other hemoglobin species.
Capnography
Exhaled CO₂ number and waveform pattern.

Pulse Oximetry

Pulse oximetry is a noninvasive way to continuously monitor SpO₂ and pulse. A probe sends light through pulsating capillary blood and estimates oxygen saturation based on light absorption by oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin.

What SpO₂ Can and Cannot Tell You

SpO₂ is a trend, not a full oxygenation assessment. It estimates the percentage of hemoglobin binding sites that appear saturated. It does not directly measure PaO₂, PaCO₂, ventilation, oxygen content, hemoglobin level, or whether carbon monoxide is occupying hemoglobin sites.

Fast, continuous, noninvasive.Does not measure PaO₂ or ventilation.A normal SpO₂ does not guarantee normal ventilation or normal COHb.

Troubleshooting Inaccurate SpO₂ Readings

If the monitor suddenly changes but the patient does not look different, do not assume the number is wrong or right. Work through a safe troubleshooting sequence.

Patient First, Monitor Second

An unexpected SpO₂ value should trigger assessment, not panic. First assess the patient and airway, then compare the displayed pulse with the patient’s actual pulse, check perfusion, verify the oxygen device, and inspect the probe. Treat true distress immediately while troubleshooting the monitor.

Overnight Pulse Oximetry

Overnight pulse oximetry records saturation and pulse during sleep. It can identify desaturation episodes and help determine whether a patient needs a full sleep study.

Screening, Not Diagnosis

Overnight oximetry can show whether oxygen saturation drops during sleep, but it does not identify sleep stages, airflow, respiratory effort, arousals, or the exact type of sleep-disordered breathing. Repeated or clinically significant desaturation should be connected to the patient’s symptoms and followed by appropriate sleep testing when indicated.

Description
Continuous overnight SpO₂ and pulse recording.
Indication
Screening for sleep-related breathing disorders.
Finding
Desaturation episodes or baseline drops.
Next step
Recommend polysomnography when indicated.

Co-Oximetry and Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

A co-oximeter uses a blood sample and multiple wavelengths of light to directly measure oxyhemoglobin, carboxyhemoglobin, methemoglobin, and other hemoglobin species. It is especially important when carbon monoxide poisoning is suspected.

Why Pulse Ox Can Miss CO Poisoning

Carbon monoxide binds to hemoglobin and can fool a standard pulse oximeter because the device cannot reliably separate oxyhemoglobin from carboxyhemoglobin. A patient may have a reassuring SpO₂ while true oxygen-carrying capacity is dangerously reduced.

Key safety point: COHb can make SpO₂ appear falsely normal or high. If O₂Hb is low and COHb is high, treat with 100% oxygen and escalate per facility protocol.

Capnography Basics

Capnography measures exhaled carbon dioxide and displays a waveform. ETCO₂ depends on CO₂ production, blood flow carrying CO₂ to the lungs, ventilation moving CO₂ out, and equipment that samples the exhaled gas.

ETCO₂ Is Ventilation Plus Perfusion

ETCO₂ is often taught as a ventilation number, but it also depends on pulmonary blood flow. A low ETCO₂ may mean the patient is blowing off CO₂, or it may mean CO₂ is not being delivered to the lungs because perfusion is poor. Always interpret the trend with the patient’s condition.

ETCO₂ ChangePossible MeaningExamples
Increasing ETCO₂Less CO₂ elimination or more CO₂ production.Hypoventilation, oversedation, COPD/asthma exacerbation, increased metabolism.
Decreasing ETCO₂More ventilation, less perfusion, or more dead space.Hyperventilation, hypovolemia, pulmonary embolism, falling cardiac output.
Zero ETCO₂No detected exhaled CO₂.Apnea, cardiac arrest, disconnection, esophageal intubation.

Capnography Waveform Patterns

A waveform helps identify problems that a single number may miss. Look at the baseline, upstroke, plateau, downstroke, height, and trend over time.

Read the Shape Before the Number

A normal capnogram has a rapid expiratory upstroke, a mostly flat alveolar plateau, and a quick return to baseline with inspiration. A slanted “shark-fin” shape suggests delayed exhalation from obstruction, while a baseline that does not return to zero suggests rebreathing.

Waveform ClueLikely Meaning
Shark-fin shapeAirway obstruction such as asthma, COPD, or mucus plugging.
Loss of plateau / sloping downLeak in cuff or circuit.
Notch in plateauInspiratory effort/curare cleft.
Elevated baselineRebreathing CO₂.
Sudden loss to zeroApnea, arrest, disconnection, or tube displacement.

Airway Placement and CPR Quality

A colorimetric detector changes from purple to yellow when it detects exhaled CO₂. This can help quickly support tracheal tube placement after intubation. Waveform capnography is preferred as soon as available because it shows both ETCO₂ values and waveform trends.

Why ETCO₂ Matters During CPR

During cardiac arrest, ETCO₂ reflects how much CO₂ is being delivered to the lungs by blood flow. Very low values can suggest poor perfusion from ineffective compressions, while a sudden sustained rise may be one of the earliest signs that circulation has returned.

Airway placement
CO₂ color change or waveform supports tracheal placement.
CPR quality
ETCO₂ less than about 10-15 mmHg suggests poor perfusion/compressions.
ROSC clue
Sudden ETCO₂ rise may indicate return of circulation.

FAQ

Common Monitoring Questions

Is pulse oximetry the same as oxygenation?

Pulse oximetry estimates saturation, but it does not measure PaO₂, ventilation, or nonfunctional hemoglobin. It is a trend that must be interpreted with patient assessment.

Why use co-oximetry for carbon monoxide poisoning?

Pulse oximetry cannot reliably distinguish O₂Hb from COHb. Co-oximetry directly measures hemoglobin species.

What does ETCO₂ tell us?

ETCO₂ reflects CO₂ production, pulmonary perfusion, ventilation, and equipment sampling. It is especially useful for trending ventilation and perfusion changes.

Why is waveform capnography preferred over colorimetry?

Colorimetry gives a quick CO₂ color change, but waveform capnography provides continuous numeric and waveform data.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Pulse oximetry trends oxygen saturation. Co-oximetry directly measures hemoglobin species and is essential in suspected CO poisoning. Capnography trends ventilation and perfusion, confirms airway placement, supports CPR quality monitoring, and helps identify airway or circuit problems through waveform shape.

Must know:
Always check the patient before treating the monitor.
CO pearl:
SpO₂ may look normal when COHb is high.
Capno pearl:
Shark fin = airway obstruction until proven otherwise.
CPR pearl:
Low ETCO₂ suggests poor perfusion; sudden rise suggests ROSC.
