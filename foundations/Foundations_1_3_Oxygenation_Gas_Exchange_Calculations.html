Gas Laws, Breathing Mechanics & Alveolar Stability

Oxygen calculations describe where oxygen is and how much is available, but gas laws explain why gases move and why respiratory equipment behaves as it does. Begin with the relationship that changes, identify what remains constant, and then predict the direction of the response.

PrincipleRelationshipRespiratory meaning
Boyle's lawAt constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely related.Increasing thoracic volume lowers alveolar pressure and draws gas inward. Compressing a gas raises pressure.
Charles's lawAt constant pressure, gas volume changes directly with absolute temperature.Cooling a gas reduces its volume; warming expands it. Temperature must be expressed in Kelvin for formal calculations.
Dalton's lawTotal pressure equals the sum of component partial pressures.Each gas contributes independently to the total pressure of air or a supplied gas mixture.
Henry's lawDissolved gas is proportional to its partial pressure and solubility.Increasing gas partial pressure increases the amount that dissolves in blood or liquid.

A sealed resuscitation bag or gas-filled device becomes warmer, causing the gas inside to expand when pressure is allowed to remain approximately constant.
At sea level, the total atmospheric pressure is produced by the combined partial pressures of oxygen, nitrogen, water vapor, and other gases.
Increasing the partial pressure of oxygen increases the amount of oxygen that dissolves in plasma, as occurs during hyperbaric oxygen therapy.

How Inspiration Begins

The diaphragm contracts and descends while the external intercostals assist chest expansion. Thoracic volume rises, pleural pressure becomes more negative, alveolar pressure falls slightly below atmospheric pressure, and gas flows inward down the pressure gradient. Quiet expiration is usually passive because inspiratory muscles relax and elastic recoil reduces lung volume, raising alveolar pressure above atmospheric pressure.

Do not reverse cause and effect: The diaphragm does not pull air directly into the lungs. Muscle contraction changes thoracic volume; the volume change alters pressure; the pressure gradient produces airflow.
Surfactant source
Type II alveolar cells synthesize and secrete pulmonary surfactant.
Surfactant effect
Lower surface tension improves alveolar stability and reduces the pressure needed for inflation.
Deficiency
Higher surface tension promotes instability, atelectasis, lower compliance, and greater work of breathing.

Density, Flow Pattern & Airway Resistance

Laminar flow is orderly and is influenced strongly by airway radius and gas viscosity. Turbulent flow occurs with high velocity, irregular geometry, branching, or narrowing and becomes more dependent on gas density. A lower-density gas mixture can reduce turbulence and the pressure required to move flow through a narrowed airway. It does not remove the obstruction; it may reduce the resistive load while the underlying problem is addressed.

Compliance: Volume Change per Pressure Change

Compliance = ΔV / ΔP

Compliance describes how easily the respiratory system expands. A lower value means a stiffer system: more pressure is required to produce the same volume. A higher value means the system expands more easily, although very high compliance may coexist with poor elastic recoil.

Example: If volume increases 300 mL when pressure rises 10 cm H₂O, compliance is 30 mL/cm H₂O. If the same 300 mL requires 20 cm H₂O, compliance falls to 15 mL/cm H₂O.
Clinical pattern:
Fibrosis, edema, atelectasis, and surfactant deficiency commonly reduce compliance.
Equipment connection:
Gas compression, temperature, mixture composition, pressure, and flow can change device readings or performance.

The Oxygen Journey

Before formulas make sense, students need the story. Oxygen begins as a fraction of inspired gas, becomes a pressure after humidification, enters the alveoli, diffuses into arterial blood, binds mostly to hemoglobin, gets delivered by cardiac output, and is extracted by tissues.

Fraction of inspired oxygen.
PAO₂
Estimated alveolar oxygen pressure.
CaO₂
Oxygen content in arterial blood.
DO₂
Oxygen delivered to tissues each minute.

PiO₂: Pressure of Inspired Oxygen

PiO₂ estimates the oxygen pressure available after inspired gas is humidified in the upper airway. Water vapor takes up some of the total pressure, so humidification matters.

PiO₂ = FiO₂ × (PB − PH₂O)
VariableMeaningCommon Value
FiO₂Fraction of inspired oxygen as a decimal.Room air = 0.21
PBBarometric pressure.Sea level ≈ 760 mmHg
PH₂OWater vapor pressure at body temperature.47 mmHg

PAO₂: Alveolar Oxygen Pressure

PAO₂ estimates how much oxygen pressure is present in the alveoli. It subtracts the effect of carbon dioxide because alveoli contain both oxygen entering and carbon dioxide leaving.

PAO₂ = FiO₂(PB − PH₂O) − (PaCO₂ / RQ)

P(A-a)O₂: Alveolar-to-Arterial Gradient

The A-a gradient compares estimated alveolar oxygen pressure to measured arterial oxygen pressure. If the gap is large, oxygen is available in the alveoli but is not transferring well into arterial blood.

P(A-a)O₂ = PAO₂ − PaO₂

P/F Ratio: Oxygenation Severity

The P/F ratio compares PaO₂ to FiO₂. It helps describe how well oxygen is getting into arterial blood relative to how much oxygen the patient is receiving.

P/F Ratio = PaO₂ / FiO₂
Interpretation guardrail: Use FiO₂ as a decimal. The ratio describes oxygenation efficiency but does not identify the mechanism by itself; interpret it with PEEP/support level, imaging, and clinical context.
Example: PaO₂ 80 on room air: 80 / 0.21 = 381. This is near normal. PaO₂ 80 on 60% oxygen: 80 / 0.60 = 133, which is much worse oxygenation.

CaO₂: Arterial Oxygen Content

PaO₂ tells oxygen pressure. CaO₂ estimates oxygen content in arterial blood. Most oxygen is carried by hemoglobin, while a tiny amount is dissolved in plasma.

CaO₂ = (1.34 × Hb × SaO₂) + (0.003 × PaO₂)
Pressure is not content: A patient may have an acceptable PaO₂ but low CaO₂ when hemoglobin is low. Increasing PaO₂ cannot fully replace missing hemoglobin-bound carrying capacity.

DO₂ and VO₂: Delivery and Consumption

DO₂ estimates how much oxygen is delivered to tissues each minute. VO₂ estimates how much oxygen tissues consume. Both depend on oxygen content and cardiac output.

DO₂ = CO × (CaO₂ × 10)
VO₂ = CO × (C(a-v)O₂ × 10)
Delivery depends on both content and flow: DO₂ can fall because of hypoxemia, anemia, or low cardiac output. Identify which link is failing before assuming that more FiO₂ is the complete answer.
TermMeaning
Cardiac outputHeart rate × stroke volume; the amount of blood pumped per minute.
C(a-v)O₂Difference between arterial and venous oxygen content.
DO₂Oxygen delivery to tissues.
VO₂Oxygen consumed by tissues.

O₂ER: Oxygen Extraction Ratio

Oxygen extraction ratio estimates the percentage of delivered oxygen that tissues actually use. It rises when oxygen demand increases or oxygen delivery is not enough.

O₂ER = VO₂ / DO₂ × 100

FAQ

Common Oxygenation Calculation Questions

Why are there so many oxygen numbers?

Each number describes oxygen in a different location or form: inspired gas, alveoli, arterial pressure, oxygen content, delivery, consumption, and extraction.

Why does hemoglobin matter so much?

Most oxygen in blood is bound to hemoglobin, so low hemoglobin can reduce oxygen content even when PaO₂ looks acceptable.

What is the difference between PaO₂ and SaO₂?

PaO₂ is oxygen pressure dissolved in arterial blood. SaO₂ is the percentage of hemoglobin binding sites occupied by oxygen.

Why do we multiply by 10 in DO₂ and VO₂ formulas?

CaO₂ is usually in mL/dL, while cardiac output is in L/min. Multiplying by 10 converts dL to L for the final mL/min value.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Gas behavior and respiratory physiology provide the foundation for oxygenation interpretation. Boyle, Charles, Dalton, and Henry relationships explain pressure, volume, temperature, partial pressure, and dissolution. Muscle contraction creates pressure gradients; surfactant lowers surface tension; density influences turbulent resistance; and compliance describes volume change per pressure change. PiO₂, PAO₂, A-a gradient, P/F ratio, CaO₂, DO₂, VO₂, and O₂ER then locate problems along the oxygen pathway.

Must know:
Pressure gradients move gas; most blood oxygen content is hemoglobin-bound.
Mechanics pearl:
Lower compliance means more pressure is required for the same volume.
Clinical pearl:
PaO₂ can look okay while CaO₂ is low if hemoglobin is low.
Common mistakes:
Reversing pressure-volume direction, confusing pressure with content, or forgetting ×100 for O₂ER.
