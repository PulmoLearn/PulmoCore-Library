Unit 2 Integration: Stop Reading Numbers in Isolation

Real patients do not arrive with one isolated value. The RT integrates monitor trends, lab data, ABGs, physical assessment, patient history, and response to therapy. A low SpO₂ means something different when ETCO₂ is high, BNP is high, sputum is pink and frothy, or the patient is drowsy.

Recognizing Respiratory Failure Patterns

Respiratory failure can be mostly oxygenation failure, mostly ventilatory failure, or both. Oxygenation failure means the patient cannot get enough oxygen into arterial blood. Ventilatory failure means the patient cannot remove enough CO₂. Combined failure means both are happening.

PatternCommon CluesRT Concern
Oxygenation failureLow SpO₂, low PaO₂, cyanosis, increased work of breathing.Improve oxygen delivery and treat cause.
Ventilatory failureHigh PaCO₂, rising ETCO₂, drowsiness, respiratory acidosis.Improve ventilation and consider escalation.
Combined failureLow PaO₂ plus high PaCO₂, altered mental status, fatigue.Urgent support; consider noninvasive or invasive ventilation.

Pulmonary vs Cardiac Shortness of Breath

Many patients present with dyspnea. RTs must look for patterns. Wheezing with low BNP and shark-fin capnogram points toward bronchospasm. Crackles, pink frothy sputum, high BNP, and fluid retention point toward cardiogenic pulmonary edema or CHF-related dyspnea.

Interpreting the Whole Patient

The safest RTs avoid tunnel vision. They do not treat SpO₂ alone, ABG alone, or labs alone. They ask: What does the patient look like? Is the airway patent? Is breathing effective? Is perfusion adequate? Are the numbers getting better or worse?

Prioritizing RT Interventions

RT priority begins with life threats. Airway and breathing problems come first. Then use data to refine the intervention. If the patient is unstable, do not wait for every test before acting.

Priority Check

Selecting the Most Appropriate RT Action

The best action depends on the pattern. Oxygen treats oxygenation problems. Ventilatory support treats CO₂ retention and fatigue. Bronchodilators treat bronchospasm. Airway clearance treats retained secretions. Positive pressure may support CHF-related pulmonary edema. Escalation is needed when the patient is unstable or failing therapy.

Reassessment: Did the Intervention Work?

An RT intervention is not complete until the patient is reassessed. Improvement can show up in SpO₂, ETCO₂, breath sounds, work of breathing, mental status, ABG values, or waveform shape.

Reassessment Check

FAQ

Common Clinical Integration Questions

How do I avoid getting overwhelmed by all the data?

Use a sequence: airway, breathing, circulation, monitoring, ABG/labs, intervention, reassessment. Do not try to solve every number at once.

What is the difference between oxygenation failure and ventilatory failure?

Oxygenation failure is low arterial oxygen. Ventilatory failure is poor CO2 removal, usually shown by high PaCO2, respiratory acidosis, rising ETCO2, and possible drowsiness.

What if the data conflict?

Recheck the patient and the equipment, repeat or confirm key data if needed, and escalate when the patient is unstable.

Why do we reassess after every intervention?

Because treatment can improve, fail, or worsen the patient. Reassessment tells the RT whether to continue, adjust, or escalate care.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Integrated respiratory decision-making means using the whole clinical picture. Monitors show trends, labs reveal causes, ABGs classify oxygenation and ventilation, assessment reveals severity, and reassessment confirms whether intervention helped.

Must know:
Low O₂ + high CO₂ = combined failure concern.
Priority pearl:
Airway and breathing threats come before routine testing.
Pattern pearl:
BNP high + pink frothy sputum supports cardiac fluid overload.
Reassessment pearl:
An RT action is not complete until the patient is rechecked.
