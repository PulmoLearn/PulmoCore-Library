Respiratory Care · 1.1 Competency Guide

Professional Responsibilities, Communication & Documentation

This week establishes the professional behaviors that support safe respiratory care. Students learn to apply ethical standards, protect confidentiality, communicate therapeutically, respect patient autonomy, document care accurately, and respond appropriately to safety or integrity concerns. The goal is not simply to recognize policies, but to use professional standards when making clinical decisions.

How to use this guide

Present professionalism as an observable clinical competency. A respiratory therapist demonstrates professionalism through what is assessed, communicated, documented, verified, and reported. Throughout the lesson, connect each concept to patient safety, continuity of care, legal accountability, and trust within the healthcare team.

C1: Apply professional ethical standards, accountability, patient advocacy, and appropriate escalation when confronted with unsafe or unethical respiratory care practices.
NBRC 2027 alignment: III.I.1 · III.I.1.c · II.B · III.G.1

What you need to know

  • AARC Code of Ethics – Explain that the American Association for Respiratory Care publishes the professional Code of Ethics for respiratory therapists. The code provides a framework for integrity, competence, respect, confidentiality, patient advocacy, and responsible professional conduct. Students should understand that ethical standards guide ordinary daily decisions as well as major ethical dilemmas.
  • Professional accountability – Define accountability as accepting responsibility for clinical decisions, actions, omissions, and maintenance of competence. A therapist remains responsible for confirming that care is safe and appropriate rather than assuming that an order, another clinician, or supervision removes personal responsibility. Accountability includes asking for assistance when uncertain and acknowledging errors promptly.
  • Patient advocacy – Explain that advocacy means protecting the patient’s rights, safety, and best interests. Advocacy may require clarifying an unsafe order, reporting falsified care, ensuring that a refusal is informed, or using the chain of command when concerns are not resolved. Advocacy should be respectful, factual, and focused on the patient rather than on winning a disagreement.
  • Ethical reporting and escalation – Teach that charting care that was not performed is falsification of the medical record and creates both ethical and patient-safety risks. The appropriate response is prompt reporting through the designated supervisory, compliance, or institutional process. Students should not ignore the behavior, wait for harm to occur, or confront the individual publicly in front of a patient.
  • Correct patient verification – Before treatment, use approved identifiers rather than room number alone. Verification prevents wrong-patient interventions and wrong-chart documentation.
  • Correct therapy verification – Confirm the ordered medication, dose, route, device, frequency, and intended treatment. Compare the order with the patient’s current condition and available protocol.
  • Clinical appropriateness and contraindications – Review relevant allergies, precautions, contraindications, recent response, and assessment findings before proceeding. An order does not eliminate the need for clinical judgment.
  • Equipment competence and infection prevention – Do not use unfamiliar equipment merely because it resembles another model. Seek assistance or training. Follow hand-hygiene and infection-prevention practices even when gloves are worn.

Why this matters clinically

Ethical practice protects patients from harm, preserves the accuracy of the health record, supports trust among team members, and protects the credibility of the profession. Unreported unethical conduct can lead to inappropriate future decisions because clinicians may rely on false information in the record.

Verification interrupts preventable error before it reaches the patient. Wrong-patient care, incorrect therapy, overlooked contraindications, unfamiliar equipment, and skipped infection-control steps can all cause avoidable harm.

Common traps to avoid
  • Accountability means accepting blame or punishment. Clarify that accountability means owning responsibilities and taking corrective action.
  • Following a provider order automatically protects the therapist from responsibility. Clarify that therapists must question orders that appear unsafe or inconsistent with the patient’s condition.
  • Reporting unethical behavior is disloyal to coworkers. Clarify that the primary obligation is to the patient and the integrity of the clinical record.

Apply it

A student observes a staff therapist documenting a nebulizer treatment that was never administered. The student should preserve patient confidentiality, avoid a public confrontation, and report the concern promptly through the clinical instructor or appropriate supervisory process. The issue is not merely incomplete work; the false entry could mislead future caregivers and place the patient at risk.

A therapist is asked to perform a procedure using equipment they have not been trained to operate. What professional responsibilities apply? Students should identify the need to protect the patient, acknowledge the limitation, request assistance or training, and avoid proceeding solely because the unit is busy.

C2: Use patient-centered therapeutic communication and education to support understanding, realistic expectations, informed decision-making, and cooperation with care.
NBRC 2027 alignment: III.I.1.e · III.I.2

What you need to know

  • Therapeutic communication – Define therapeutic communication as purposeful, patient-centered communication that promotes understanding, trust, participation, and safe care. It includes attentive listening, empathy, clear explanations, observation of verbal and nonverbal cues, and confirmation of understanding. It is not casual conversation centered on the therapist’s experiences or a display of technical vocabulary.
  • Clear and realistic education – Teach students to explain the purpose and expected benefit of a treatment without promising a cure or guaranteed outcome. The therapist should describe what improvement may occur, what will be reassessed, and when the patient should report worsening symptoms.
  • Actionable safety guidance – Patient education is strongest when it tells the patient what to expect and what action to take. For example, explaining that a bronchodilator may open the airways should be paired with instructions to notify staff if breathing worsens or expected relief does not occur.
  • Addressing anxiety before performance – When anxiety or misunderstanding may interfere with a procedure, address the patient’s concern before judging effort or interpreting results. Explain the procedure, demonstrate what is expected, invite questions, and check understanding. This approach can improve cooperation and data quality.
  • Teach-back and verification of understanding – After education, ask the patient to explain the purpose or instructions in their own words. Teach-back verifies how effectively the information was communicated; it is not a test of the patient.
  • Respect for autonomy – Explain that competent patients have the right to accept or refuse recommended treatment. Respecting autonomy does not mean ending the interaction immediately; the therapist should first ensure that the patient understands the treatment, expected benefits, and potential consequences of refusal.
  • Exploring the reason for refusal – A refusal may result from fear, discomfort, misunderstanding, timing, prior experiences, cultural concerns, or treatment burden. Ask open-ended questions and listen without pressure so the underlying concern can be addressed.
  • Informed decision-making – Provide accurate information, answer questions within the therapist’s scope, and confirm understanding. Do not threaten, coerce, declare the treatment mandatory, or seek authorization from a family member when the competent patient is making the decision.

Why this matters clinically

Clear communication improves adherence, reduces anxiety, supports informed choices, and helps patients recognize when they need assistance. Poor communication can lead to refusal, incorrect treatment use, unrealistic expectations, or unreliable diagnostic testing.

Respect for autonomy preserves patient rights and trust. Exploring the reason for refusal may also identify a correctable problem, allowing the patient to participate willingly rather than through coercion.

Common traps to avoid
  • Therapeutic communication means reassuring the patient that everything will be fine. Clarify that reassurance must remain honest and realistic.
  • Using more medical terminology demonstrates expertise. Clarify that patient understanding is the goal.
  • An anxious or poorly performing patient is simply uncooperative. Clarify that understanding and anxiety should be addressed first.

Apply it

A patient asks whether a breathing treatment will cure chronic lung disease. An appropriate response explains that the treatment may improve breathing symptoms, describes its purpose, and identifies what the therapist will reassess. This is accurate, patient-centered, and avoids making an unsupported promise.

A patient appears anxious and repeatedly stops early during spirometry. What should the therapist address before labeling the effort as poor? Students should explain the need to assess understanding, reduce anxiety, demonstrate the maneuver, provide clear coaching, and then reassess performance.

C3: Protect patient privacy and confidentiality by appropriately handling identifiable health information in verbal, written, and electronic communication.
NBRC 2027 alignment: III.I.1.e · III.I.2

What you need to know

  • Purpose of HIPAA privacy protections – Explain that HIPAA safeguards identifiable health information from inappropriate use or disclosure. It does not prevent necessary communication among clinicians involved in care; instead, it requires information to be shared only for legitimate purposes and through appropriate methods.
  • Identifiable information – Teach that a patient can be identified through more than a name. Room number, diagnosis, age, unusual circumstances, dates, photographs, or combinations of details may reveal identity. Removing the name does not automatically make a discussion confidential.
  • Appropriate location and audience – Patient information should be discussed only with people who have a legitimate role in care and in a location where unauthorized individuals are unlikely to overhear. Elevators, hallways, cafeterias, public transportation, and social media are inappropriate places for identifiable clinical discussions.
  • Minimum necessary communication – Explain that clinicians should communicate the information needed to perform their role without sharing unnecessary details. The minimum-necessary principle supports privacy while allowing safe coordination of care.

Why this matters clinically

Patients must be able to trust that sensitive information will be protected. Privacy violations can damage the therapeutic relationship, expose patients to embarrassment or discrimination, and create legal and institutional consequences.

Common traps to avoid
  • HIPAA prevents healthcare professionals from discussing patients with one another. Clarify that appropriate care-related communication is permitted.
  • A conversation is acceptable whenever no name is used. Clarify that other details may still identify the patient.
  • Speaking quietly makes a public conversation compliant. Clarify that the location and audience remain inappropriate.

Apply it

Two therapists discuss a patient’s rare diagnosis and room number in an elevator. Even without stating the patient’s name, the combination of details could identify the patient to a visitor or employee. The discussion should occur in a private clinical area and include only information needed for care.

A classmate begins discussing an unusual patient case in the hospital cafeteria. What details could make the patient identifiable, and how can the conversation be redirected professionally? Students should apply the concepts of identifiable information, legitimate audience, and appropriate location.

C4: Create and correct clinical documentation using objective, accurate, timely, and transparent practices that describe the intervention, patient response, education, and relevant limitations.
NBRC 2027 alignment: III.I.2 · I.A.1 · III.I.1.a

What you need to know

  • Respect for autonomy – Explain that competent patients have the right to accept or refuse recommended treatment. Respecting autonomy does not mean ending the interaction immediately; the therapist should first ensure that the patient understands the treatment, expected benefits, and potential consequences of refusal.
  • Exploring the reason for refusal – A refusal may result from fear, discomfort, misunderstanding, timing, prior experiences, cultural concerns, or treatment burden. Ask open-ended questions and listen without pressure so the underlying concern can be addressed.
  • Informed decision-making – Provide accurate information, answer questions within the therapist’s scope, and confirm understanding. Do not threaten, coerce, declare the treatment mandatory, or seek authorization from a family member when the competent patient is making the decision.
  • Follow-up and communication – After an informed refusal, respect the decision, notify the appropriate provider when required, and document the refusal, education, and notifications objectively.
  • Objective, accurate, and timely documentation – Teach students to record what they assessed, observed, performed, and communicated using factual language. Documentation should be completed promptly and should not include assumptions, labels, blame, or unsupported conclusions.
  • Documenting a refusal – A complete refusal note identifies the treatment declined, relevant education provided, the patient’s decision, and required notifications. Avoid judgmental statements such as “refused for no good reason.”
  • Intervention, response, and understanding – Treatment documentation should identify the intervention, objective patient response, and education outcome. For example, note changes in breath sounds or wheezing and whether the patient verbalized understanding of medication purpose rather than writing only “treatment worked.”
  • Documenting limitations – When patient performance limits diagnostic data, describe the observable behavior, coaching provided, number of attempts when relevant, and effect on test acceptability. Do not label the patient as uncooperative or diagnose a condition from invalid data.

Why this matters clinically

Respect for autonomy preserves patient rights and trust. Exploring the reason for refusal may also identify a correctable problem, allowing the patient to participate willingly rather than through coercion.

Accurate documentation is a patient-safety tool, a communication method, and a legal record. Vague, judgmental, incomplete, or false entries can mislead the healthcare team and interfere with safe decision-making.

Common traps to avoid
  • A prescribed treatment is mandatory. Clarify that competent patients retain the right to refuse.
  • The first step is to document the refusal and leave. Clarify that the therapist should first explore the reason and provide appropriate information.
  • A family member can override a competent adult patient’s decision. Clarify that family involvement depends on the patient’s wishes and decision-making capacity.

Apply it

During an aerosol treatment, the patient asks to stop and says they do not want it right now. The therapist pauses, asks why, explains the treatment’s purpose and possible consequences of refusal, answers questions, and respects the informed decision. The therapist then follows notification and documentation requirements.

A patient refuses a treatment because it previously caused severe shakiness. How should the therapist respond? Students should identify the need to explore the concern, provide information, assess whether the order or medication should be clarified, respect the decision, notify as appropriate, and document objectively.

C5: Perform respiratory care safely by verifying patient identity, ordered therapy, clinical appropriateness, and relevant contraindications before treatment.
NBRC 2027 alignment: II.B · III.G.1

What you need to know

  • Correct patient verification – Before treatment, use approved identifiers rather than room number alone. Verification prevents wrong-patient interventions and wrong-chart documentation.
  • Correct therapy verification – Confirm the ordered medication, dose, route, device, frequency, and intended treatment. Compare the order with the patient’s current condition and available protocol.
  • Clinical appropriateness and contraindications – Review relevant allergies, precautions, contraindications, recent response, and assessment findings before proceeding. An order does not eliminate the need for clinical judgment.
  • Equipment competence and infection prevention – Do not use unfamiliar equipment merely because it resembles another model. Seek assistance or training. Follow hand-hygiene and infection-prevention practices even when gloves are worn.

Why this matters clinically

Verification interrupts preventable error before it reaches the patient. Wrong-patient care, incorrect therapy, overlooked contraindications, unfamiliar equipment, and skipped infection-control steps can all cause avoidable harm.

Common traps to avoid
  • Room number is an acceptable patient identifier. Clarify that rooms can change and are not sufficient identifiers.
  • Gloves replace hand hygiene. Clarify that hand hygiene remains required.
  • Similar-looking devices can be operated the same way. Clarify that model-specific controls and safety features may differ.

Apply it

Before an aerosol treatment, the therapist confirms two patient identifiers, verifies the medication and order, checks relevant contraindications and allergies, performs a focused assessment, and uses equipment they are trained to operate. These steps demonstrate proactive patient safety.

A therapist recognizes the device model but has never used that specific version. The ordered therapy is due now. What should the therapist do? Students should apply accountability, equipment competence, treatment verification, and the duty to seek assistance before proceeding.
