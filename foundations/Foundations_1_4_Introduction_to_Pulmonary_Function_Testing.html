Pulmonary function testing helps respiratory therapists and providers understand airflow, lung volumes, gas exchange, and response to bronchodilators. Why Pulmonary Function Tests Are Performed

PFTs are objective breathing tests. They help classify diffuse lung disease, measure severity, monitor progression, evaluate response to bronchodilators, and sometimes determine risk before surgery or treatment. For beginners, think of PFTs as a way to answer four questions: Is air moving? How much air can the lungs hold? How fast can air leave? How well does gas cross into the blood?

Types of Pulmonary Function Tests

PFTs include standard tests that many patients can perform and specialized tests that answer more specific questions. Standard testing often begins with spirometry. Additional tests may include lung volumes and DLCO.

Test TypeExamplesWhat It Helps Answer
Standard PFTsSpirometry, lung volumes, bronchodilator response, flow-volume loopAirflow, volume, obstruction, restriction, response to therapy.
Specialized PFTsDLCO, bronchoprovocation, exercise testing, maximal pressuresDiffusion, airway hyperreactivity, exercise limitation, muscle strength.
Report componentsPredicted values, measured values, % predicted, graphs, interpretationCompares patient results to expected values based on patient characteristics.

Board Prep Frame

PFT interpretation usually begins with pattern recognition. A low FEV1/FVC ratio points toward obstruction. A low TLC confirms restriction. DLCO helps explain diffusion impairment and can narrow the differential.

Lung Volumes & Capacities

Lung volumes describe how much air is in the lungs during different parts of breathing. A capacity is a combination of volumes. These terms appear constantly in PFT reports, spirometry, ventilator management, and disease interpretation.

TermMeaningBeginner Translation
VTTidal volumeNormal breath in or out.
IRVInspiratory reserve volumeExtra air you can inhale after a normal inhale.
ERVExpiratory reserve volumeExtra air you can exhale after a normal exhale.
RVResidual volumeAir left after maximal exhalation.
ICInspiratory capacityVT + IRV.
VCVital capacityIRV + VT + ERV; the most air moved from full inhale to full exhale.
FRCFunctional residual capacityAir remaining after a normal exhalation.
TLCTotal lung capacityAll air in the lungs after maximal inhalation.

Spirometry Basics

Spirometry measures how much air a patient can exhale and how quickly they can exhale it. The patient takes a deep breath, seals around the mouthpiece, blasts the air out hard and fast, and continues exhaling until empty.

MeasurementMeaningWhy It Matters
FVCForced vital capacityTotal volume forcibly exhaled after a full inhale.
FEV1Forced expiratory volume in 1 secondHow much air leaves during the first second.
FEV1/FVCRatio of first-second exhaled volume to total forced exhaled volumePrimary screening value for obstruction.
PEFRPeak expiratory flow rateHighest flow achieved during forced exhalation.

Beginner PFT Interpretation

Do not try to interpret everything at once. Start with one question: is the FEV1/FVC ratio low? If yes, think obstruction. If FEV1/FVC is normal or high, then look at FVC and TLC to consider restriction.

Flow-Volume Loops

A flow-volume loop gives a visual picture of forced inhalation and exhalation. Beginners should first recognize the normal shape, then look for major pattern changes. Obstructive disease often creates a scooped expiratory limb. Restrictive disease often creates a smaller, narrow loop.

How Lung Volumes Are Measured

Spirometry cannot directly measure residual volume, functional residual capacity, or total lung capacity. Additional methods are needed. Common approaches include helium dilution, nitrogen washout, body plethysmography, and imaging estimates.

MethodBasic IdeaBeginner Takeaway
Helium dilutionPatient breathes a helium mixture in a closed system until equilibrium.Uses dilution to estimate FRC.
Nitrogen washoutPatient breathes 100% oxygen and nitrogen is washed out.Estimates lung gas volume from exhaled nitrogen.
Body plethysmographyPatient sits in a body box; pressure changes estimate thoracic gas volume.Often considered the most accurate for TLC, but more complex and expensive.
ImagingChest x-ray or CT measurements estimate lung volume.Effort may be easier, but estimates may be less precise.

DLCO: Diffusing Capacity

DLCO stands for diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide. It estimates how well gas crosses from alveoli into pulmonary capillary blood. Carbon monoxide is used in a tiny test dose because it crosses the alveolar-capillary membrane and binds strongly to hemoglobin.

Rate of gas diffusion increases with surface area and pressure gradient, and decreases when the membrane is thicker.
DLCO Decreases WithWhy
EmphysemaLoss of alveolar surface area.
Pulmonary fibrosisThickened membrane impairs diffusion.
Pulmonary vascular diseaseLess capillary blood available for gas transfer.
AnemiaLess hemoglobin available to bind test gas.

FAQ

Common PFT Questions

What is the easiest first step in PFT interpretation?

Start with FEV1/FVC. If it is low, think obstruction. Then use FVC, TLC, loop shape, bronchodilator response, and DLCO to refine the interpretation.

Why does TLC matter?

TLC confirms whether the lung is truly restricted. A low FVC alone may suggest restriction, but TLC is needed to confirm it.

Why does DLCO use carbon monoxide?

A very small test dose of CO is used because it crosses the alveolar-capillary membrane and binds strongly to hemoglobin, making it useful for estimating diffusion.

Do PFTs diagnose the patient by themselves?

No. PFTs must be interpreted with symptoms, history, imaging, exam findings, and clinical context.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

PFTs measure airflow, volume, gas exchange, and therapy response. Beginners should start with the FEV1/FVC ratio, confirm restriction with TLC, recognize common flow-volume loop shapes, and understand DLCO as a diffusion measurement.

Must know:
Low FEV1/FVC points toward obstruction.
Board pearl:
Low TLC confirms restriction.
Clinical pearl:
Bronchodilator response helps evaluate reversibility.
Common mistake:
Calling restriction from FVC alone without TLC confirmation.
