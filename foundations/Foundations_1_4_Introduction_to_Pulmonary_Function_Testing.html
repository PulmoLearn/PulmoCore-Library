Pulmonary Function Testing Basics

Pulmonary function testing helps respiratory therapists evaluate airflow, forced volumes, total lung volumes, gas transfer, and response to bronchodilator therapy. This lesson emphasizes the required clinical sequence: verify acceptable and repeatable data first, identify the ventilatory pattern second, and then correlate the result with symptoms, history, predicted values, and the need for additional testing.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to describe the major PFT components, determine whether maneuvers are acceptable and repeatable, interpret spirometry and flow-volume loops, confirm or exclude restriction with TLC, explain DLCO and bronchodilator response, and recommend repeat or additional testing when the available data are insufficient.

1. Identify PFT components
Connect spirometry, lung volumes, flow-volume loops, and DLCO to what each test measures.
2. Recognize airflow patterns
Use FEV₁/FVC and loop shape to identify obstruction.
3. Interpret lung volumes
Use FVC and TLC to recognize possible restriction.
4. Apply clinical reasoning
Use PFTs with symptoms, history, predicted values, test quality, and other findings.
5. Protect data quality
Repeat unacceptable or inconsistent maneuvers before interpreting disease patterns.

PFT Overview: What Are We Measuring?

PFTs are not one single number. They are a group of measurements that show how much air the lungs can move, how easily air flows, how much air remains in the lungs, and how well gas crosses from the alveoli into the blood.

Think Test Category First

Before interpreting a result, ask what category the value belongs to. Spirometry focuses on airflow and forced volumes. Lung volume testing measures how much air the lungs can hold. DLCO estimates gas transfer across the alveolar-capillary membrane.

Quality First: Is the Test Trustworthy?

Interpretation begins only after the therapist decides that the maneuver represents the patient’s best technically acceptable effort. A low value caused by early termination, leak, cough, hesitation, or inconsistent technique is not evidence of lung disease.

Acceptable start
Maximal inspiration followed by an immediate, hard, fast blast without excessive hesitation.
Acceptable maneuver
Tight seal, no obstructing cough, no leak or glottic closure, and continued exhalation to an appropriate endpoint.
Repeatable efforts
The best acceptable maneuvers should be close enough to show that the result is reproducible rather than random.

Predicted Values and Lower Limits of Normal

Measured values are interpreted against appropriate reference values that account for factors such as age, height, sex, and the laboratory’s reference equations. A fixed ratio can be useful for beginner pattern recognition, but clinical interpretation should use the reported lower limit of normal or z score when available. Do not diagnose disease from one percentage or cutoff in isolation.

Objective Documentation

Document what occurred and how it affected the data: for example, “Three attempts obtained; exhalation ended early despite repeated coaching, limiting FVC interpretation.” Avoid labels such as “poor patient” or “noncompliant.” The record should distinguish the observed limitation from a conclusion about motivation.

Lung Volumes: How Much Air Can the Lungs Hold?

Lung volumes describe different amounts of air that move in and out of the lungs or remain after exhalation. Students often mix these up, so start with the simple question each term answers.

Four Basic Volumes

VT is the normal quiet breath. IRV is extra air inhaled after a normal breath in. ERV is extra air exhaled after a normal breath out. RV is air left after maximal exhalation and cannot be measured by simple spirometry alone.

Flow-Volume Loops: Shape Matters

A flow-volume loop shows how fast air moves during forced breathing at different lung volumes. The expiratory limb is especially helpful for seeing obstructive airflow limitation.

Look at the Peak and the Curve

A normal loop rises quickly to a high peak expiratory flow and then descends smoothly. An obstructive loop often has a lower peak and a scooped-out expiratory limb because air leaves the lungs more slowly.

DLCO: Diffusing Capacity

DLCO estimates how well gas crosses the alveolar-capillary membrane into the blood. It depends on the membrane, alveolar surface area, pulmonary capillary blood volume, and hemoglobin availability.

Why Carbon Monoxide Is Used

DLCO uses a very small amount of carbon monoxide as a tracer gas because CO binds strongly to hemoglobin. The test estimates how much CO moves from the alveoli into the blood during a short breath-hold.

Bronchodilator Response: Compare Before and After

When ordered, spirometry may be repeated after a bronchodilator to determine whether airflow measurements improve. The comparison should use technically acceptable pre- and post-treatment maneuvers and the laboratory’s current response criteria.

Improvement
An increase in FEV₁, FVC, or both supports a reversible component of airflow limitation.
No significant response
Does not prove that bronchodilators are never clinically useful or exclude all variable obstruction.
Clinical limit
Bronchodilator response contributes evidence; it does not independently diagnose asthma or distinguish every obstructive disorder.
Quality rule: A post-bronchodilator value is not interpretable when the pre- and post-treatment maneuvers differ substantially in effort or acceptability.

Basic PFT Interpretation Algorithm

After confirming acceptable and repeatable data, start with the FEV₁/FVC ratio, then examine FVC, and confirm suspected restriction with TLC. Add RV, loop shape, DLCO, bronchodilator response, and clinical history only after the primary pattern is established.

Interpret in Order

A ratio below the reported lower limit of normal supports obstruction. A preserved ratio with low FVC suggests possible restriction, but low TLC is required for confirmation. A low ratio plus low TLC supports a mixed pattern. A low FVC with normal or high TLC may reflect air trapping, incomplete exhalation, or another cause rather than true restriction.

FAQ

Common PFT Questions

Why do we look at more than one PFT value?

One number rarely tells the whole story. The ratio, FVC, TLC, loop shape, DLCO, bronchodilator response, and test quality all add different pieces of information.

Can spirometry alone confirm restriction?

Spirometry can suggest restriction when FVC is low with a preserved ratio, but restriction is confirmed by low TLC.

Why does patient effort matter?

PFTs depend on coaching and technique. Poor effort, leaks, coughing, or early termination can make results look abnormal.

Why can anemia lower DLCO?

DLCO depends partly on hemoglobin binding the tracer gas. Less hemoglobin can reduce measured uptake even if the membrane itself is not the primary problem.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

PFT interpretation begins with data quality. Confirm acceptable and repeatable efforts before using FEV₁, FVC, or loop morphology. Then evaluate FEV₁/FVC for obstruction, use TLC to confirm restriction, distinguish mixed disease from air trapping, and use DLCO and bronchodilator response to add physiologic context. Report limitations objectively and recommend repeat or additional testing when the data do not support a reliable conclusion.

Must know:
Low FEV₁/FVC suggests obstruction.
