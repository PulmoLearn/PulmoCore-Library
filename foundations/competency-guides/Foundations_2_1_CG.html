Respiratory Care · 2.1 Competency Guide

Oximetry & Capnography

This week develops the student's ability to evaluate oxygenation and ventilation using noninvasive monitoring. Students learn what pulse oximetry and capnography measure, recognize normal and abnormal trends, identify common sources of error, interpret basic capnogram patterns, confirm artificial-airway placement, and recognize respiratory depression even when oxygen saturation appears acceptable. The central goal is to prevent students from treating a monitor value as a diagnosis and instead teach them to verify signal quality, assess the patient, interpret trends, and respond to the most urgent physiologic problem.

How to use this guide

Teach pulse oximetry and capnography as complementary tools that answer different clinical questions. Pulse oximetry estimates hemoglobin oxygen saturation; capnography evaluates exhaled carbon dioxide, respiratory pattern, and changes in ventilation. Require students to use the same sequence with every monitor value: assess the patient, verify the signal and equipment, interpret the number and waveform, compare with prior data, identify the likely problem, intervene when indicated, and reassess. Repeatedly emphasize that supplemental oxygen can preserve SpO2 while dangerous hypoventilation and carbon dioxide retention continue.

C1: Explain the physiologic variable estimated by pulse oximetry and distinguish SpO2 from PaO2, oxygen content, ventilation, and hemoglobin concentration.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.7.g · I.C.2 · I.D.2

What you need to know

  • Physiologic estimate - Explain that a pulse oximeter uses red and infrared light absorption in pulsatile arterial blood to estimate the percentage of functional hemoglobin that is oxygenated. The displayed value is SpO2, not PaO2, PaCO2, oxygen content, or hemoglobin concentration.
  • Expected values - A healthy adult at sea level commonly has an SpO2 in the mid-to-upper 90s, but the meaning of any value depends on altitude, diagnosis, baseline, perfusion, temperature, and the clinical situation. Avoid presenting 100% as a universal requirement.
  • Saturation versus oxygen tension - Review that the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve creates a nonlinear relationship between SpO2 and PaO2. A small fall in saturation on the steep portion of the curve may represent a clinically important fall in PaO2.
  • Saturation versus oxygen content - Emphasize that a patient with severe anemia may have a normal SpO2 and PaO2 but reduced arterial oxygen content because there is less hemoglobin available to carry oxygen.
  • Trend interpretation - A single plausible value is less informative than the direction and rate of change. Interpret SpO2 alongside respiratory rate, work of breathing, mental status, oxygen device, and response to treatment.
  • Clinical targets - Target saturation ranges may differ according to disease, order, protocol, and risk of hyperoxia or carbon dioxide retention. Students should follow the prescribed goal while continuing to assess the whole patient.

Why this matters clinically

Pulse oximetry is fast and noninvasive, but it can create false reassurance when students assume that a normal saturation means the patient is ventilating normally or carrying an adequate total amount of oxygen. Understanding the limits of the measurement prevents delayed recognition of respiratory depression, anemia, and equipment error.

Common traps to avoid
  • SpO2 is the same as PaO2. Clarify that saturation and arterial oxygen tension are related but different measurements.
  • A normal SpO2 proves that breathing is adequate. Clarify that oxygenation can remain normal while ventilation deteriorates, especially during supplemental oxygen administration.
  • Every healthy adult must have an SpO2 of 100%. Clarify that normal values vary and should be interpreted in context.

Apply it

A patient with hemoglobin 6.9 g/dL has SpO2 98% and PaO2 96 mmHg. The monitor indicates that the available hemoglobin is highly saturated, but it does not show whether enough hemoglobin is present to provide normal oxygen content. The therapist should recognize that a normal saturation does not eliminate concern about impaired oxygen delivery.

A patient has SpO2 96% on 4 L/min oxygen but is increasingly somnolent with a respiratory rate of 7/min. What additional information is needed before calling the patient stable? Students should identify ventilation, ETCO2 or blood-gas data, mental status, airway patency, medication history, and trend.

C2: Evaluate pulse-oximetry values for plausibility and identify motion, poor perfusion, dyshemoglobinemia, probe placement, ambient light, and equipment factors that can reduce accuracy.
NBRC 2027 alignment: II.A.21 · II.B.4.e

What you need to know

  • Assess the patient before the device - If the number conflicts with the patient's appearance, immediately assess airway, breathing, circulation, mental status, and work of breathing while another team member verifies the equipment when available.
  • Perfusion and motion - Poor peripheral perfusion, vasoconstriction, shock, hypothermia, and motion can weaken or distort the pulsatile signal. A displayed number is not automatically valid simply because the monitor is functioning.
  • Probe placement - Confirm correct sensor size, position, alignment, and contact. Inspect the site for pressure injury and move the sensor according to policy when prolonged monitoring is required.
  • Signal indicators - Evaluate the plethysmographic waveform, pulse signal, and agreement between the monitor pulse rate and the patient's actual pulse. A poor waveform or pulse mismatch reduces confidence in the saturation value.
  • External and patient factors - Nail coverings, ambient light, skin pigmentation, venous pulsation, dyshemoglobinemias, and some dyes may affect accuracy or interpretation. Teach students to know these limitations without assuming every unexpected reading is artifact.
  • Verification and escalation - Reposition or replace the probe, warm or select a better-perfused site, reduce motion, compare with another site or device, and obtain blood-gas or hemoximetry data when the clinical question cannot be resolved noninvasively.

Why this matters clinically

Incorrectly accepting an unreliable pulse-oximetry value can delay oxygen therapy, prompt unnecessary escalation, or hide true deterioration. Verification is a patient-safety behavior, not merely an equipment-maintenance task.

Common traps to avoid
  • If the monitor displays a number, the reading is accurate. Clarify that signal quality must be evaluated before interpretation.
  • An unexpected low value should always be dismissed as motion artifact. Clarify that the patient must be assessed first because true hypoxemia may be present.
  • Changing the probe is the only troubleshooting step. Clarify that perfusion, motion, site condition, device setup, and clinical correlation must all be considered.

Apply it

A restless patient has SpO2 values fluctuating from 78% to 96%, a weak pleth waveform, cold fingers, and a monitor pulse rate that does not match the palpated pulse. The therapist should assess the patient, improve perfusion or select another site, reduce motion when possible, replace or reposition the sensor, and determine whether confirmatory testing is needed before acting on the fluctuating numbers.

The monitor reads 82%, but the patient is awake, speaking normally, and has warm skin without distress. What should happen next? Students should not ignore the value or immediately diagnose severe hypoxemia; they should assess, verify signal quality, check the probe and pulse agreement, and repeat or confirm the measurement.

C3: Explain the components of a normal capnogram and interpret ETCO2 values, respiratory rate, waveform shape, and trends in relation to ventilation, perfusion, and airway patency.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.7.g · I.C.2 · I.D.2 · I.E.10 · I.A.8 · III.G.1

What you need to know

  • What capnography measures - Capnography continuously displays exhaled carbon dioxide over time and reports the end-tidal CO2 value at the end of expiration. Capnometry provides a number; capnography includes the waveform and trend.
  • Usual adult reference range - A typical ETCO2 is approximately 35-45 mmHg, but interpretation must include the waveform, respiratory rate, perfusion, dead space, and relationship to PaCO2.
  • Normal waveform phases - Review the inspiratory baseline, expiratory upstroke, alveolar plateau, end-tidal point, and inspiratory downstroke. Students should identify whether CO2 returns to baseline during inspiration and whether the plateau shape is changing.
  • Low ETCO2 - A low value with tachypnea may reflect hyperventilation, but low cardiac output, pulmonary embolism, increased dead space, leak, disconnection, or sampling error may also reduce ETCO2. Avoid treating one value as a specific diagnosis.
  • High ETCO2 - Elevated or rising ETCO2 with bradypnea commonly indicates hypoventilation and carbon dioxide retention. Consider sedation, opioid effect, airway obstruction, neuromuscular weakness, or reduced minute ventilation.
  • Obstructive waveform - A prolonged slanted expiratory upstroke and upward-sloping plateau create the classic shark-fin pattern associated with delayed, uneven emptying from airflow obstruction such as bronchospasm.
  • Trend over isolated number - A steadily rising ETCO2, falling respiratory rate, altered waveform, or sudden loss of exhaled CO2 may be more clinically important than whether one value lies inside the usual reference range.
  • Different clinical questions - SpO2 estimates oxygen saturation, while respiratory rate, waveform capnography, ETCO2, mental status, and blood-gas data help evaluate ventilation. A normal result in one domain does not prove normal function in the other.

Why this matters clinically

Capnography can reveal ventilatory change before oxygen saturation falls. The waveform also provides information about respiratory pattern, airway obstruction, equipment continuity, and artificial-airway position that a single oxygen-saturation number cannot provide.

Respiratory depression may become life-threatening while oxygen saturation remains deceptively normal. Integrating oxygenation, ventilation, mental status, and oxygen therapy prevents the common error of waiting for desaturation before recognizing inadequate breathing.

Common traps to avoid
  • ETCO2 always equals PaCO2. Clarify that a gradient normally exists and may widen with increased dead space or poor perfusion.
  • A low ETCO2 always means hyperventilation. Clarify that perfusion failure, pulmonary embolism, leak, and disconnection can also lower the value.
  • A high ETCO2 is an oxygenation problem. Clarify that it primarily indicates a ventilation or carbon dioxide elimination problem.

Apply it

A spontaneously breathing patient has RR 32/min and ETCO2 24 mmHg. Hyperventilation is likely, but the therapist must also evaluate blood pressure, perfusion, waveform quality, chest symptoms, and the possibility of increased dead space before deciding why the value is low.

A patient's ETCO2 falls abruptly from 38 mmHg to nearly zero. What categories of causes must be considered immediately? Students should include apnea, airway displacement, circuit or sampling disconnection, severe loss of perfusion, and equipment failure while prioritizing direct patient assessment.

C5: Use continuous waveform capnography to support confirmation and ongoing monitoring of artificial-airway placement and recognize patterns that require immediate reassessment.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.C.2 · I.D.2 · III.A.1 · III.G.1

What you need to know

  • Confirmation after intubation - Persistent waveform capnography showing exhaled CO2 provides strong immediate evidence of tracheal placement. Use it together with direct observation, chest movement, auscultation, tube depth, and subsequent imaging as indicated.
  • Continuous confirmation - Capnography helps detect displacement, disconnection, obstruction, apnea, or loss of perfusion after initial placement. Confirmation is not a one-time event.
  • Nearly flat waveform after placement - Persistent absence of exhaled CO2 immediately after intubation strongly suggests esophageal placement or a disconnected sampling system and requires immediate action. Rare low-perfusion states can reduce CO2 delivery, so the patient and system must be assessed rapidly.
  • Sudden loss after a previously normal waveform - Consider tube displacement, ventilator or circuit disconnection, apnea, severe obstruction, cardiac arrest or profound loss of perfusion, and monitor or sampling-line failure. Assess the patient first and provide manual ventilation when indicated.
  • Waveform quality - Condensation, secretions, kinking, water in the sampling line, leaks, or poor connection may distort or interrupt the waveform. Equipment troubleshooting should occur simultaneously with patient assessment.
  • Documentation and communication - Report the time and nature of the waveform change, airway assessment, equipment findings, interventions, response, and any escalation. Avoid charting only "capnography abnormal."

Why this matters clinically

An unrecognized esophageal intubation, displaced tube, or circuit disconnection can rapidly cause catastrophic hypoxemia. Waveform capnography provides continuous evidence of exhaled carbon dioxide and can alert the team before other signs become obvious.

Common traps to avoid
  • Chest rise alone confirms tracheal placement. Clarify that chest movement, condensation, and auscultation can be misleading.
  • An SpO2 of 98% confirms correct tube placement. Clarify that saturation may remain high briefly after preoxygenation even when the tube is misplaced.
  • Once placement is confirmed, capnography is no longer needed. Clarify that continuous monitoring helps detect later displacement and disconnection.

Apply it

Immediately after intubation, the capnogram remains nearly flat. The therapist should treat this as possible esophageal intubation or system disconnection, rapidly assess the airway and patient, verify the sampling setup, notify the intubator, and support ventilation rather than waiting for SpO2 to fall.

A mechanically ventilated patient suddenly loses the capnogram while SpO2 remains 99%. What should the therapist do first? Students should immediately assess the patient and airway, check ventilation and circuit continuity, and not wait for desaturation.
