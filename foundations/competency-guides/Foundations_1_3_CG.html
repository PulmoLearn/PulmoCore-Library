Follow the Oxygen: Oxygenation, Gas Exchange & Oxygen Transport

Follow oxygen from the atmosphere to the alveoli, across the alveolar-capillary interface, into arterial blood, through systemic delivery, and finally to tissue extraction. The goal is to calculate the right value, explain what it represents, and identify where the oxygen pathway is breaking down.

How to use this guide

Think of oxygenation as one connected pathway rather than a collection of unrelated formulas. For every calculation, identify where oxygen is in the pathway, what the result means physiologically, and what an abnormal result suggests.

Repeat this question: Is the limiting problem inspired oxygen, alveolar oxygen availability, transfer into blood, hemoglobin carrying capacity, blood flow/delivery, or tissue extraction?

C1: Calculate and interpret inspired and alveolar oxygen values using FiO2, barometric pressure, water-vapor pressure, PaCO2, and the alveolar gas equation.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.C.7 · I.D.7 · III.C

Inspired oxygen: calculate PiO2

  • FiO2 is a fraction or percentage, not a pressure. Room air is approximately 0.21 FiO2.
  • Inspired gas is humidified in the airways. At body temperature, water-vapor pressure (PH2O) is approximately 47 mmHg.
  • PiO2 = FiO2 × (PB − PH2O).
  • At sea level on room air: 0.21 × (760 − 47) ≈ 150 mmHg.
  • Lower barometric pressure lowers PiO2 even when FiO2 stays exactly the same. This is why altitude reduces inspired oxygen pressure.

Alveolar oxygen: calculate PAO2

  • PAO2 = FiO2(PB − PH2O) − (PaCO2/R).
  • For course calculations, use a respiratory quotient R = 0.8 unless another value is supplied.
  • At sea level on room air with PaCO2 near 40 mmHg, PAO2 is commonly near 100 mmHg.
  • Increasing FiO2 raises PAO2. Increasing PaCO2 lowers PAO2.
  • Hypoventilation can therefore cause hypoxemia by lowering alveolar oxygen availability even when FiO2 is unchanged.
Common traps to avoid
  • Do not treat 21% oxygen as a fixed oxygen pressure; barometric pressure matters.
  • Do not stop at PiO2 when the question asks about oxygen available in the alveolus; PaCO2 must be considered.

Apply it

A somnolent patient on room air has PaCO2 60 mmHg. Predict whether PAO2 will rise or fall before calculating it, then use the alveolar gas equation to explain why.

C2: Evaluate alveolar-to-arterial oxygen transfer using PaO2, the A-a oxygen gradient, and the P/F ratio, and relate abnormalities to V/Q mismatch, shunt, diffusion limitation, or reduced inspired oxygen.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.2 · I.D.5 · I.D.7 · III.C

Decide whether oxygen crossed the lung effectively

  • PaO2 is the partial pressure of oxygen dissolved in arterial plasma after oxygen has crossed into the blood.
  • A-a gradient = PAO2 − PaO2. It compares oxygen available in the alveolus with oxygen that reached arterial blood.
  • A typical young-adult room-air A-a gradient is roughly 5–15 mmHg; it rises with age and can vary with FiO2. Use the clinical and laboratory reference when available.
  • A relatively normal A-a gradient with hypoxemia points upstream toward reduced inspired oxygen or hypoventilation.
  • A widened A-a gradient suggests impaired pulmonary transfer such as V/Q mismatch, shunt, or diffusion limitation.
  • P/F ratio = PaO2 ÷ FiO2. Values above about 300 are generally reassuring for oxygenation efficiency; lower values indicate increasing impairment. Do not diagnose ARDS from the P/F ratio alone.
Mechanism clues
  • V/Q mismatch: ventilation and perfusion are not well matched; hypoxemia often improves with supplemental oxygen.
  • Shunt: blood reaches the arterial circulation after perfusing nonventilated alveoli; hypoxemia may respond poorly to oxygen alone.
  • Diffusion limitation: a stiff, thickened alveolar-capillary interface or reduced surface area can slow oxygen equilibration. The problem may become more apparent during exercise when pulmonary capillary transit time shortens.
  • Hypoventilation: PAO2 falls as PaCO2 rises; the A-a gradient can remain relatively normal when transfer itself is intact.

Apply it

If PAO2 is 180 mmHg and PaO2 is 70 mmHg, calculate the A-a gradient and decide whether the major problem is oxygen availability before the lung or transfer across the lung.

C3: Differentiate oxygen partial pressure, hemoglobin saturation, and oxygen content and explain why PaO2 or SpO2 alone cannot determine total oxygen-carrying capacity.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.2 · I.C.2 · I.D.5

Do not confuse pressure, saturation, and content

PaO2 = pressure
The pressure of oxygen dissolved in arterial plasma. It reflects arterial oxygenation, not total oxygen carried.
SaO2/SpO2 = saturation
The percentage of available hemoglobin binding sites occupied by oxygen.
CaO2 = content
The total amount of oxygen carried in each deciliter of arterial blood.
  • Most oxygen is carried bound to hemoglobin. Only a small amount is dissolved in plasma.
  • SpO2 can be 98–100% even when hemoglobin concentration is dangerously low.
  • A normal PaO2 and normal SpO2 therefore do not prove that oxygen carrying capacity or tissue oxygen delivery is adequate.

Apply it

A patient has PaO2 95 mmHg and SpO2 98%, but hemoglobin is 6 g/dL. Explain why the pulse oximeter can look reassuring while oxygen content is severely reduced.

C4: Calculate and interpret arterial oxygen content (CaO2) and predict the effects of hemoglobin concentration, saturation, and dissolved oxygen on oxygen content.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.C.7 · I.D.7 · III.C

Calculate how much oxygen arterial blood actually carries

  • CaO2 = (1.34 × Hb × SaO2) + (0.003 × PaO2).
  • Use SaO2 as a decimal in the equation: 98% = 0.98.
  • The first term represents hemoglobin-bound oxygen and contributes almost all of normal CaO2.
  • The second term represents dissolved oxygen and normally contributes only a small amount.
  • Typical arterial oxygen content is often about 16–22 mL O2/dL, depending largely on hemoglobin and saturation.
  • Severe anemia can markedly lower CaO2 even with a normal PaO2 and normal saturation.
Clinical habit: When oxygenation looks normal but the patient still appears poorly perfused or symptomatic, check whether the problem is carrying capacity rather than oxygen pressure.

Apply it

Compare a patient with Hb 15 g/dL and SaO2 92% with a patient who has Hb 7 g/dL and SaO2 100%. Calculate CaO2 before deciding who carries more oxygen.

C5: Calculate and interpret systemic oxygen delivery (DO2) and explain how cardiac output and arterial oxygen content work together to determine oxygen available to tissues.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.C.7 · I.D.7 · III.C · III.E.2

Add blood flow to oxygen content

  • CaO2 tells you what each deciliter of arterial blood carries. DO2 asks how much oxygen is delivered to tissues each minute.
  • DO2 = cardiac output × CaO2 × 10.
  • When cardiac output is in L/min and CaO2 is in mL O2/dL, the result is mL O2/min.
  • Resting adult DO2 is commonly around 900–1100 mL O2/min, with variation based on body size, hemoglobin, saturation, and cardiac output.
  • Low cardiac output can cause inadequate oxygen delivery even when PaO2, SpO2, and CaO2 appear acceptable.
  • Raising FiO2 may improve arterial oxygenation without correcting severe anemia or profound low-flow shock.

Apply it

A patient has CaO2 18 mL O2/dL but cardiac output is only 2.5 L/min. Calculate DO2, then explain why normal oxygen content cannot compensate for inadequate blood flow.

C6: Evaluate venous oxygen content, arteriovenous oxygen difference, and oxygen extraction in context, then identify the most likely location of an oxygen-transport problem and the most appropriate next assessment or response.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.8 · III.C · III.E.2

What came back from the tissues?

  • After tissues remove oxygen, venous blood returns with less oxygen than arterial blood.
  • Mixed venous saturation (SvO2) is commonly about 60–80% in a stable adult, depending on delivery and metabolic demand.
  • Venous oxygen content can be estimated with the same content equation using Hb, SvO2, and PvO2: CvO2 = (1.34 × Hb × SvO2) + (0.003 × PvO2).
  • C(a-v)O2 = CaO2 − CvO2. A larger difference means more oxygen was extracted from each deciliter of blood.
  • VO2 = CO × C(a-v)O2 × 10 estimates oxygen consumption.
  • O2 extraction ratio = VO2 ÷ DO2 × 100. Resting extraction is commonly about 20–30%.
  • Low SvO2 can reflect reduced delivery, increased tissue demand, or both. High SvO2 is not automatically reassuring; it can occur when tissues cannot adequately extract or use oxygen.
  • Interpret venous oxygen values with perfusion, lactate, hemodynamics, temperature, activity, mental status, urine output, and overall clinical condition.
Do not stop when SpO2 improves.

If arterial oxygenation improves but hypotension, cool skin, oliguria, low SvO2, worsening mental status, or rising lactate persist, the remaining limiting step may be systemic blood flow and tissue delivery rather than the lungs.

Apply it

A shocked patient improves from SpO2 86% to 96% after oxygen, but cardiac output remains very low, SvO2 is 48%, urine output falls, and lactate rises. Identify which step improved and which step still requires intervention and reassessment.

Supporting Unit 1 foundations that still matter

The PulmoLearn lesson intentionally includes broader respiratory-physiology foundations. They support the oxygen pathway even though they are not separate Week 1.3 competencies.

  • Quiet inspiration: diaphragm contracts and descends → thoracic volume increases → alveolar pressure falls below atmospheric pressure → air flows inward.
  • Effective ventilation: total minute ventilation can look acceptable while alveolar ventilation is inadequate. Rapid, shallow breathing sends a larger fraction of each breath to dead space and can allow PaCO2 to rise.
  • Diffusion reserve: a stiff, thickened alveolar-capillary interface can impair oxygen diffusion; exercise may reveal the problem because capillary transit time shortens.
