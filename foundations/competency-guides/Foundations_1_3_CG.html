Follow the Oxygen: Oxygenation, Gas Exchange & Oxygen Transport

This week follows oxygen from the atmosphere to the alveoli, across the alveolar-capillary membrane, into arterial blood, through systemic delivery, and finally to tissue extraction. Students learn what can be measured or calculated at each step, what a normal or abnormal result suggests, and why a reassuring value at one step does not guarantee adequate oxygen delivery at the next.

How to use this guide

Teach oxygenation as a pathway rather than a collection of unrelated formulas. For every calculation, require students to identify where oxygen is in the pathway, what the result represents physiologically, the expected range or direction, and what abnormal values suggest. Students should repeatedly ask: Is the problem inspired oxygen, alveolar oxygen availability, transfer into blood, hemoglobin carrying capacity, perfusion/delivery, or tissue extraction?

C1: Calculate and interpret inspired and alveolar oxygen values using FiO₂, barometric pressure, water-vapor pressure, PaCO₂, and the alveolar gas equation.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.C.7 · I.D.7 · III.C

What you need to know

  • FiO₂ is a fraction, not a pressure. Room air is approximately 0.21 FiO₂, but oxygen partial pressure also depends on barometric pressure.
  • Inspired gas is humidified in the conducting airways. At body temperature, water-vapor pressure is approximately 47 mmHg and must be subtracted before calculating inspired oxygen pressure.
  • Teaching formula: PiO₂ = FiO₂ × (PB − PH₂O). At sea level on room air: 0.21 × (760 − 47) ≈ 150 mmHg.
  • A lower barometric pressure lowers inspired oxygen partial pressure even when FiO₂ is unchanged. This is why altitude can reduce oxygen availability.
  • A normal FiO₂ does not guarantee a normal alveolar or arterial oxygen level; it describes only the fraction of oxygen being delivered.

Why this matters clinically

Students need to understand that oxygen concentration and oxygen pressure are related but not interchangeable. This distinction supports later interpretation of altitude, oxygen devices, PAO₂, and the A-a gradient.

Common traps to avoid
  • “21% oxygen always means the same oxygen pressure.” Correct this by changing barometric pressure while keeping FiO₂ constant.
  • “FiO₂ is measured in mmHg.” Reinforce that FiO₂ is a fraction or percentage; partial pressure is measured in mmHg.

Apply it

A patient travels from sea level to high altitude while breathing the same 21% oxygen. What changes first: FiO₂ or inspired oxygen partial pressure? Students should explain why barometric pressure changes the pressure available to drive oxygen into the alveoli.

C2: Evaluate alveolar-to-arterial oxygen transfer using PaO₂, the A-a oxygen gradient, and the P/F ratio, and relate abnormalities to V/Q mismatch, shunt, diffusion limitation, or reduced inspired oxygen.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.2 · I.D.5 · I.D.7 · III.C

What you need to know

  • Use the alveolar gas equation to estimate alveolar oxygen pressure: PAO₂ = FiO₂(PB − PH₂O) − (PaCO₂/R).
  • For introductory calculations, use a respiratory quotient (R) of 0.8 unless otherwise stated.
  • At sea level on room air with PaCO₂ near 40 mmHg, PAO₂ is commonly near 100 mmHg.
  • Increasing FiO₂ raises PAO₂. Increasing PaCO₂ lowers PAO₂ because retained CO₂ occupies more of the available alveolar gas pressure.
  • A low PAO₂ may reflect reduced inspired oxygen pressure, low FiO₂, or hypoventilation before a membrane-transfer problem is considered.

Why this matters clinically

PAO₂ estimates the oxygen pressure available in the alveolus before oxygen crosses into arterial blood. It creates the reference point needed to decide whether low PaO₂ is explained by low alveolar oxygen or impaired transfer.

Apply it

A hypoventilating patient has PaCO₂ 70 mmHg on room air. The alveolar gas equation predicts a lower PAO₂ even if the alveolar-capillary membrane is normal. Students should recognize hypoventilation as a cause of hypoxemia that can occur without a widened A-a gradient.

C3: Differentiate oxygen partial pressure, hemoglobin saturation, and oxygen content and explain why PaO₂ or SpO₂ alone cannot determine total oxygen-carrying capacity.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.2 · I.C.2 · I.D.5

What you need to know

  • PaO₂ measures the partial pressure of oxygen dissolved in arterial plasma. It tells us how successfully oxygen reached arterial blood, but not how much total oxygen is being carried.
  • A-a gradient = PAO₂ − PaO₂. It asks how much oxygen was lost between the alveolus and arterial blood.
  • A small/expected A-a gradient with hypoxemia points toward reduced inspired oxygen or hypoventilation. A widened gradient suggests impaired pulmonary transfer such as V/Q mismatch, shunt, or diffusion limitation.
  • A typical young adult room-air A-a gradient is roughly 5–15 mmHg and rises with age and FiO₂. Always interpret against the clinical context and laboratory/teaching reference.
  • P/F ratio = PaO₂ ÷ FiO₂. Values above about 300 are generally reassuring for oxygenation efficiency; lower values indicate increasing impairment. Do not diagnose ARDS from the P/F ratio alone.
  • A normal PaO₂ does not prove normal tissue oxygen delivery if hemoglobin or cardiac output is low.

Apply it

Two patients have the same PaO₂ of 55 mmHg. One has a normal A-a gradient and PaCO₂ of 70; the other has a markedly widened A-a gradient with diffuse pneumonia. Why are their mechanisms different even though the PaO₂ is identical?

C4: Calculate and interpret arterial oxygen content (CaO₂) and predict the effects of hemoglobin concentration, saturation, and dissolved oxygen on oxygen content.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.C.7 · I.D.7 · III.C

What you need to know

  • SaO₂ is arterial hemoglobin oxygen saturation measured from an arterial sample; SpO₂ is the pulse-oximeter estimate. Neither measurement tells you the hemoglobin concentration.
  • Most oxygen is carried bound to hemoglobin. Only a small amount is dissolved in plasma.
  • Teaching formula: CaO₂ = (1.34 × Hb × SaO₂) + (0.003 × PaO₂). Use saturation as a decimal.
  • Typical CaO₂ is often approximately 16–22 mL O₂/dL, depending heavily on hemoglobin concentration and saturation.
  • Severe anemia can produce a normal PaO₂ and normal SpO₂ but a markedly reduced CaO₂.
  • Increasing PaO₂ above already adequate saturation adds very little oxygen content because the dissolved component is small.

Why this matters clinically

This is the point in the pathway where students see why pulse oximetry alone can be falsely reassuring. A patient can have a saturation of 98% and still carry too little oxygen if hemoglobin is profoundly low.

Apply it

Patient A: Hb 15 g/dL, SaO₂ 92%. Patient B: Hb 7 g/dL, SaO₂ 100%. Students should calculate CaO₂ and determine which patient actually carries more arterial oxygen.

C5: Calculate and interpret systemic oxygen delivery (DO₂) and explain how cardiac output and arterial oxygen content work together to determine oxygen available to tissues.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.C.7 · I.D.7 · III.C · III.E.2

What you need to know

  • Oxygen content describes what each deciliter of arterial blood carries. Oxygen delivery adds blood flow.
  • Teaching formula: DO₂ = CO × CaO₂ × 10, producing mL O₂/min when CO is in L/min and CaO₂ is in mL O₂/dL.
  • Resting adult DO₂ is commonly around 900–1100 mL O₂/min, but expected values vary with body size, hemoglobin, saturation, and cardiac output.
  • Low cardiac output can cause inadequate oxygen delivery even when PaO₂, SpO₂, and CaO₂ appear acceptable.
  • An intervention can improve one part of the pathway without fixing the limiting step. Raising FiO₂ cannot correct severe anemia or profound low-flow shock by itself.

Apply it

A patient has SpO₂ 99%, Hb 14 g/dL, but cardiac output is 2 L/min with cool extremities and oliguria. Why can tissue oxygen delivery still be inadequate?

C6: Evaluate venous oxygen content, arteriovenous oxygen difference, and oxygen extraction in context, then identify the most likely location of an oxygen-transport problem and the most appropriate next assessment or response.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.8 · III.C · III.E.2

What you need to know

  • After tissues remove oxygen, venous blood returns with a lower oxygen content. Mixed venous saturation (SvO₂) is commonly about 60–80% in a stable adult, depending on metabolic demand and delivery.
  • Venous oxygen content can be estimated using the oxygen-content equation with venous saturation and PvO₂.
  • Arteriovenous oxygen difference: C(a-v)O₂ = CaO₂ − CvO₂. A larger difference indicates greater extraction from each unit of blood.
  • Oxygen extraction ratio can be conceptualized as the fraction of delivered oxygen removed by tissues; resting extraction is commonly about 20–30%.
  • A low venous saturation may reflect reduced delivery, increased metabolic demand, or both. A high venous saturation can occur when delivery is excessive relative to demand or when tissues cannot adequately extract/use oxygen.
  • Trend venous oxygen values with perfusion, lactate, hemodynamics, temperature, activity, and overall clinical condition.

Why this matters clinically

The oxygen pathway does not end at the arterial sample. Students should be able to explain why a patient with apparently adequate arterial oxygenation may still show evidence of inadequate tissue delivery or unusually high extraction.
