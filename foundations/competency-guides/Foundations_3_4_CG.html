Respiratory Care · 3.4 Competency Guide

Nutrition, Ethics & Electronic Medical Records

This week integrates three areas that frequently intersect in respiratory care: nutrition, ethical decision making, and responsible use of electronic health information. Students learn to connect cardiopulmonary disease with practical dietary recommendations, recognize how dyspnea and increased work of breathing interfere with nutritional intake, apply professional ethical principles to patient-care conflicts, distinguish electronic medical records from broader electronic health records, protect confidential information, and verify the correct patient before care. The central goal is to select actions that are clinically appropriate, ethically defensible, accurately documented, and safe.

How to use this guide

Present nutrition, ethics, and electronic documentation as parts of one clinical decision-making process rather than as unrelated topics. The respiratory therapist assesses the patient, identifies barriers and risks, communicates recommendations, verifies identity and orders, protects confidential information, documents objectively, and escalates concerns when necessary. Emphasize practical reasoning: what problem is present, what recommendation fits the diagnosis and functional limitations, whose preferences must be respected, what information may be shared, and what documentation supports continuity and accountability.

C1: Differentiate major dietary priorities for patients with congestive heart failure, coronary artery disease, and COPD based on the underlying cardiopulmonary problem.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.1 · I.B.1.d · III.I.2.c

What you need to know

  • Congestive heart failure - Emphasize sodium restriction because sodium promotes water retention, increasing intravascular volume, edema, pulmonary congestion, and cardiac workload. Fluid restriction may also be ordered when retention or hyponatremia is significant. Recommendations must match the prescribed plan rather than a universal fluid limit.
  • Coronary artery disease - Emphasize a heart-healthy pattern that limits saturated and trans fats, supports appropriate calorie intake, and favors vegetables, fruits, whole grains, lean proteins, and unsaturated fats. The goal is long-term cardiovascular risk reduction rather than immediate relief of dyspnea.
  • COPD - Emphasize adequate calories and protein, smaller meals, reduced meal-related fatigue, and foods that are easy to chew and prepare. Some patients require calorie-dense choices because the work of breathing raises energy expenditure. (C1-C3)
  • Diagnosis changes the rationale - A low-sodium plan addresses fluid retention, a heart-healthy pattern addresses atherosclerotic risk, and COPD strategies address increased work of breathing and poor meal tolerance. Avoid giving every cardiopulmonary patient the same diet advice.
  • Individualize recommendations - Consider diabetes, renal disease, swallowing status, dentition, food access, culture, medication effects, and the patient’s goals. Respiratory therapists reinforce the care plan and refer complex nutrition decisions to the dietitian.

Why this matters clinically

Dietary recommendations can either relieve or worsen cardiopulmonary symptoms. Sodium excess may aggravate pulmonary congestion, while inadequate calories and protein may weaken respiratory muscles and impair recovery. Correctly matching the recommendation to the disease mechanism is a clinical reasoning skill.

Common traps to avoid
  • All patients with heart or lung disease should restrict fluids. Clarify that fluid restriction is diagnosis- and order-specific.
  • COPD patients should simply eat less because large meals cause dyspnea. Clarify that they often need the same or greater daily energy intake distributed across smaller meals.
  • A “cardiac diet” and a “COPD diet” are interchangeable. Clarify that the primary goals differ even when healthy foods overlap.

Apply it

A patient admitted with acute decompensated heart failure has bilateral crackles, dependent edema, and rapid weight gain. A family member brings high-sodium soup. The therapist should connect sodium intake with fluid retention, reinforce the prescribed plan, and notify the appropriate team member if education or diet modification is needed rather than making an independent medical nutrition prescription.

A patient with COPD and low body weight is also prescribed a low-sodium diet for heart failure. How can the team provide calorie-dense meals without undermining the sodium restriction?

C2: Explain how dyspnea, fatigue, early satiety, and increased work of breathing can reduce intake while increasing energy demand in chronic lung disease.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.B.1.d · I.A.8 · III.C.1-2 · III.I.2.e

What you need to know

  • Increased energy expenditure - Chronic respiratory muscle activity can substantially increase calorie requirements. A patient may lose weight even when intake appears reasonable because the metabolic cost of breathing is high.
  • Dyspnea competes with eating - Chewing and swallowing briefly alter breathing. Patients who are tachypneic may stop eating early, avoid textured foods, or become anxious because they feel unable to coordinate breathing with meals.
  • Early satiety - Hyperinflation, abdominal pressure, constipation, and large fluid volumes with meals may create fullness before adequate calories are consumed. Smaller, more frequent meals can reduce this burden. (C2-C3)
  • Rest before meals - Schedule meals after the patient has recovered from activity or treatment. Airway clearance and bronchodilator therapy may improve tolerance when timed appropriately, but exhausting procedures immediately before food can have the opposite effect.
  • Positioning and oxygen - Upright positioning, prescribed oxygen during meals, pacing, and easy-to-reach food reduce respiratory demand. Oxygen should be used as ordered and tubing should be managed safely.
  • Energy-conserving food choices - Recommend foods that are nutrient-dense, easy to chew, and simple to prepare. Limit foods that cause troublesome bloating for that individual rather than applying an inflexible list.
  • Referral triggers - Recurrent coughing during meals, wet voice, choking, prolonged meal time, unexplained weight loss, or inability to maintain intake requires referral for nutritional and/or swallowing evaluation.

Why this matters clinically

Malnutrition reduces muscle mass, including respiratory muscle reserve, and can impair immunity, wound healing, and rehabilitation. Practical meal strategies may determine whether a patient can meet nutritional needs without worsening distress.

Common traps to avoid
  • Oxygen should be removed during meals to make eating easier. Clarify that prescribed oxygen commonly supports meal tolerance and should not be stopped without a clinical reason.
  • Weight loss is expected and harmless in severe COPD. Clarify that unintentional loss is a risk marker and may worsen outcomes.
  • Any cough while eating proves aspiration. Clarify that repeated or clinically significant signs require assessment rather than an automatic diagnosis.

Apply it

A patient with severe COPD eats less than one-quarter of each meal, becomes tachypneic while chewing, and has lost 4 kg in one month. Appropriate actions include upright positioning, rest and treatment before meals, prescribed oxygen, smaller calorie-dense meals, and referral to the dietitian. Coughing or wet voice would also support a swallowing assessment.

A patient’s SpO2 is acceptable while resting but falls and dyspnea increases during meals. What should be assessed before simply increasing calories?

C5: Apply the AARC Code of Ethics and the principles of autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice, confidentiality, and professional accountability to respiratory care decisions.
NBRC 2027 alignment: III.I.1.e · III.I.2 · III.E.2.i

What you need to know

  • AARC Code of Ethics - Explain that the professional code guides integrity, competence, confidentiality, patient advocacy, nondiscrimination, evidence-based practice, and responsible relationships with patients, colleagues, employers, and the public.
  • Autonomy - Respect the informed choices of a patient who has decision-making capacity, including the right to refuse treatment. Respect does not mean withholding accurate education or failing to assess capacity. (C5-C6)
  • Beneficence - Act to promote the patient’s welfare. The beneficial option should be supported by evidence, clinical data, and the patient’s goals.
  • Nonmaleficence - Avoid preventable harm. This includes verifying the patient, questioning unsafe care, maintaining competence, protecting privacy, and stopping when an intervention creates danger.
  • Justice - Provide fair, respectful care and allocate attention based on clinical need rather than bias, social status, disability, or personal approval.
  • Confidentiality - Protect information while sharing what is necessary for treatment and safety. Confidentiality is both an ethical obligation and a legal requirement.
  • Accountability - Own clinical decisions, limitations, documentation, and escalation. A therapist is not relieved of responsibility merely because an order exists. (C5-C6)

Why this matters clinically

Ethical principles help therapists explain why an action is appropriate when policies, preferences, and clinical risks conflict. They also provide a consistent framework for patient advocacy and professional conduct.

Common traps to avoid
  • Autonomy means the patient can demand any treatment. Clarify that patients may accept or refuse offered care but cannot require unsafe or nonindicated treatment.
  • Beneficence allows clinicians to override any refusal. Clarify that capacity and informed choice remain central except in defined emergencies or legal circumstances.
  • Ethics only applies to end-of-life care. Clarify that routine privacy, communication, verification, and documentation decisions are ethical actions.

Apply it

A competent patient refuses noninvasive ventilation after receiving clear information about the risks and alternatives. The therapist should assess understanding, answer questions, respect the informed refusal, document objectively, notify the provider, and continue appropriate supportive care rather than coercing the patient.

A patient with capacity chooses a treatment plan that the team believes is less beneficial. What must occur before the choice can be considered informed refusal?

C7: Differentiate an electronic medical record from an electronic health record and explain how accurate documentation supports safety and continuity across settings.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.1 · III.H.8 · III.H.10 · III.I.1.b · III.I.1.a

What you need to know

  • Elements of informed decision making - The patient receives understandable information about the intervention, expected benefits, material risks, alternatives, and the option to decline. The decision must be voluntary and made by a person with capacity or an authorized surrogate.
  • Capacity is decision-specific - A diagnosis, age, communication disability, or unusual choice does not automatically remove capacity. Assess whether the patient can understand, appreciate consequences, reason about options, and communicate a choice.
  • Informed refusal - Use the same care as informed consent. Clarify the treatment, risks of declining, alternatives, and opportunities for questions; then document the refusal and notifications without judgmental language.
  • Emergency exceptions - Immediate care may proceed according to law and institutional policy when delay threatens life and no authorized decision maker is available. Students should not generalize emergency exceptions to routine care.
  • Escalate unresolved conflict - Use the chain of command, ethics consultation, risk management, or specialist consultation when patient wishes, surrogate requests, clinical recommendations, or team actions remain in conflict.
  • Document the reasoning process - Record relevant assessment findings, education, patient or surrogate statements, capacity concerns, consultations, notifications, and the agreed plan. (C6-C7)
  • Electronic medical record (EMR) - Describe the digital record used within a particular organization or practice to document diagnoses, assessments, orders, treatments, and responses.
  • Electronic health record (EHR) - Describe the broader longitudinal record designed to support information sharing across organizations and settings. In everyday use the terms may overlap, but the conceptual distinction is scope and interoperability.

Why this matters clinically

Poorly managed consent and refusal can produce coercion, unwanted treatment, delayed care, or legal and ethical harm. Clear communication and documentation protect the patient’s rights while supporting team accountability.

Clinicians make decisions from the record. Inaccurate, incomplete, delayed, or copied documentation can lead to duplicate treatment, missed deterioration, wrong-patient care, and unreliable handoffs.

Common traps to avoid
  • A signature alone proves informed consent. Clarify that understanding and voluntariness matter more than the form itself.
  • Any confused patient lacks all decision-making authority. Clarify that delirium, sedation, language barriers, and capacity require careful assessment.
  • The therapist should personally resolve every ethical conflict. Clarify the importance of consultation and chain of command.

Apply it

A patient scheduled for bronchoscopy states, “I signed the form, but nobody told me I might receive sedation.” The therapist should pause preparation, notify the procedural team, and ensure questions are answered before proceeding. The signature does not replace informed discussion.

What should the therapist do when a surrogate demands continued treatment that conflicts with a valid advance directive?

C10: Integrate nutrition, ethical reasoning, patient verification, communication, documentation, and reassessment in complex respiratory care scenarios.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.8 · III.E.2.i · III.I.1-2

What you need to know

  • Begin with the patient, not the category - Assess respiratory status, symptoms, intake, weight trend, functional limitations, orders, preferences, and immediate safety concerns before choosing an action.
  • Identify the dominant problem - Decide whether the priority is respiratory distress, fluid retention, inadequate intake, aspiration risk, an ethical conflict, a privacy risk, or a verification discrepancy.
  • Match the intervention to scope - Provide respiratory care and approved education, but obtain dietary, swallowing, ethics, social-work, or provider consultation when the problem exceeds respiratory therapy scope.
  • Communicate objectively - State the patient data, concern, recommendation, and urgency. Avoid assumptions about motivation or compliance.
  • Document and reassess - Record the intervention, patient decision, education, notification, and response. Reassess whether symptoms, intake, oxygenation, and safety improved.
  • Escalate unresolved risk - Use the chain of command when wrong-patient risk, unsafe care, privacy breach, impaired capacity, or clinical deterioration remains unresolved.

Why this matters clinically

Real respiratory care rarely presents one issue at a time. A patient may have COPD, poor intake, an oxygen requirement, a treatment refusal, and a family demanding information. Structured integration keeps the therapist patient-centered and safe.

Common traps to avoid
  • The first identified issue is always the highest priority. Clarify that airway, breathing, circulation, severe deterioration, and immediate safety risks can supersede other concerns.
  • Referral means transferring responsibility. Clarify that the therapist remains responsible for respiratory assessment and follow-through.
  • Good documentation compensates for failing to act. Clarify that charting supports care but never replaces intervention or escalation.

Apply it

A patient with COPD is tachypneic during meals, has lost weight, refuses a treatment, and asks that family not receive updates. The therapist should first assess immediate respiratory stability, provide clear education about the treatment, respect an informed refusal if capacity is intact, protect confidentiality, document objectively, and request nutrition evaluation for persistent intake and weight concerns.
