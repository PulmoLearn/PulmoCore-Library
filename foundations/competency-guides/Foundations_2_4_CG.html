Respiratory Care · 2.4 Competency Guide

Integrated Respiratory Decision Making

This week brings the Unit 2 assessment skills together into a repeatable clinical decision-making process. Students integrate patient history, respiratory assessment, pulse oximetry, capnography, laboratory findings, imaging clues, and arterial blood gases to determine the dominant respiratory problem, identify immediate threats, select an appropriate first action, and evaluate the response. The goal is not simply to name a diagnosis. Students must explain which objective findings support the conclusion, what must be addressed first, and what new information would change the plan.

How to use this guide

Teach clinical reasoning as a loop rather than a one-time answer: assess and verify the data, classify the problem, prioritize the threatened function, intervene within the respiratory therapist’s role, reassess, and escalate or modify the plan when goals are not met. Require students to separate oxygenation from ventilation, interpret trends rather than isolated values, and recognize that a quieter or slower-breathing patient may be deteriorating from fatigue. In urgent scenarios, assessment and stabilization occur simultaneously; students should never delay support while waiting for every test result.

C1: Integrate history, monitoring trends, physical assessment, laboratory data, imaging findings, and ABGs into a coherent respiratory assessment.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.1-8 · I.B.1-4 · I.D.1-25 · III.E.2 · I.A.1 · I.D.18 · I.E.2-3

What you need to know

  • Begin with the patient - Before interpreting a monitor, laboratory result, or ABG, assess appearance, mental status, airway patency, breathing pattern, work of breathing, speech, and perfusion. Data are clinically meaningful only when they are consistent with the patient or have been verified.
  • Use a repeatable loop - Teach assess and verify → identify the dominant problem → prioritize → intervene → reassess → communicate or escalate. Each new response becomes the next set of data.
  • Interpret clusters, not isolated values - Tachypnea, tachycardia, hypoxemia, accessory-muscle use, limited speech, and altered mental status together establish greater severity than any finding alone.
  • Ask what changed - Onset, trend, procedure timing, medication exposure, position, and response to therapy often identify the mechanism. Sudden deterioration after central-line placement should be interpreted differently from gradual dyspnea with fever and sputum.
  • State the decision explicitly - Students should finish every case with: the primary problem, the evidence, the first action, the reassessment target, and the escalation threshold. (C5-C7)
  • Pulmonary pattern - Wheezing, cough, purulent sputum, fever, focal crackles, and focal infiltrate support obstructive or infectious lung disease when they occur together.
  • Cardiogenic pulmonary edema - Acute dyspnea, orthopnea, hypertension, bilateral crackles, diffuse opacities, and pink frothy sputum form a high-risk cardiac congestion pattern.
  • Pneumothorax after a procedure - Sudden dyspnea and unilateral absent breath sounds after central-line insertion strongly suggest ipsilateral pneumothorax and require immediate evaluation.

Why this matters clinically

Clinical care fails when information remains fragmented. A normal saturation can conceal hypoventilation, an improved respiratory rate can represent fatigue, and an abnormal ABG may not be the most immediate threat. The structured loop helps students convert data into safe, defensible actions.

Pattern recognition narrows the differential and guides the first safe action, but clusters of findings must remain connected to severity and reassessment. Similar symptoms can arise from very different mechanisms and require different therapies.

Common traps to avoid
  • More data always produce a better decision. Clarify that immediate airway, breathing, or circulation threats require action while focused assessment continues.
  • The most abnormal number is automatically the highest priority. Clarify that priority depends on the threatened function and the patient’s trajectory.
  • Once the diagnosis is identified, the reasoning is complete. Clarify that the therapist must still select an action and reassess the response.

Apply it

A patient has RR 30/min, HR 118/min, SpO₂ 88%, accessory-muscle use, and can speak only a few words at a time. The combined pattern indicates substantial respiratory distress. The therapist should support oxygenation, perform a focused assessment, identify the likely cause, and prepare to escalate rather than attributing the findings to anxiety.

A patient’s SpO₂ rises after oxygen therapy, but ETCO₂ increases and mental status declines. Which part of the problem improved, which worsened, and what should happen next?

C2: Differentiate oxygenation failure, ventilatory failure, and combined respiratory failure using objective patient data and clinical presentation.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.C.2-5 · I.D.2 · I.A.8

What you need to know

  • Oxygenation failure - Low PaO₂ and/or SpO₂ with a normal or low PaCO₂ indicates that oxygen transfer is the dominant problem. Causes include V/Q mismatch, shunt, diffusion impairment, atelectasis, edema, and pneumonia. Oxygen support is often needed while the cause is evaluated.
  • Ventilatory failure - Elevated PaCO₂ with acidemia, bradypnea, fatigue, or altered mental status indicates inadequate effective alveolar ventilation. Supplemental oxygen may preserve SpO₂ while CO₂ retention worsens.
  • Combined failure - Low PaO₂ together with elevated PaCO₂ and acidemia reflects both impaired oxygenation and inadequate ventilation. These patients may require simultaneous oxygen support, airway management, ventilatory assistance, and rapid escalation.
  • Separate the questions - Ask first, “Is oxygen getting into arterial blood?” and then, “Is CO₂ being removed?” Do not use SpO₂ to answer a ventilation question or PaCO₂ alone to answer an oxygenation question.
  • Use chronicity carefully - A patient with chronic hypercapnia may have an elevated baseline PaCO₂, but new acidemia, worsening drowsiness, and increased work of breathing indicate acute decompensation rather than stable chronic disease.

Why this matters clinically

Oxygenation and ventilation often require different interventions. Treating only the saturation can delay recognition of opioid depression, fatigue, or acute-on-chronic hypercapnic failure, while treating only CO₂ can leave severe hypoxemia uncorrected.

Common traps to avoid
  • Normal SpO₂ rules out respiratory failure. Clarify that oxygen can mask hypoventilation.
  • Any high PaCO₂ in COPD is chronic and acceptable. Clarify that pH, mental status, work of breathing, and trend determine stability.
  • Low PaO₂ and high PaCO₂ represent one problem with one treatment. Clarify that both components must be assessed and supported.

Apply it

A postoperative patient on 2 L/min oxygen has SpO₂ 99%, RR 8/min, ETCO₂ 60 mmHg, and increasing somnolence. Oxygenation appears supported, but ventilation is failing. The priority is immediate assessment and support of ventilation, not reassurance from the saturation.

An ABG shows PaO₂ 48, PaCO₂ 68, and pH 7.20. What two physiologic failures are present, and why is oxygen alone insufficient?

C6: Reassess after every intervention, interpret response trends, document the outcome, and determine whether to continue, modify, or escalate care.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.E.1-13 · III.E.1-3 · III.G.1 · III.I.1.a

What you need to know

  • Choose the action that addresses the immediate mechanism - Hypoxemia requires oxygen support; bronchospasm may require bronchodilator therapy; hypoventilation requires ventilatory assessment and support; airway obstruction requires airway-focused intervention. Avoid treatments that address a different problem.
  • Reassess specific targets - After intervention, reassess SpO₂/PaO₂, respiratory rate and depth, work of breathing, breath sounds, ETCO₂/PaCO₂, mental status, hemodynamics, and symptoms. The targets should match the original problem.
  • Improvement is not completion - A rise in SpO₂ and decrease in respiratory rate are favorable only when mental status, work of breathing, and ventilation are also stable. Continued abnormalities require ongoing treatment and reassessment.
  • Escalate when goals are not met - Worsening distress, refractory hypoxemia, rising PaCO₂ with acidemia, altered mental status, shock, airway compromise, or failure to improve after appropriate therapy requires prompt communication and additional support.
  • Document intervention and response - Record the therapy delivered, relevant settings, objective pre/post findings, patient tolerance, communication, and next plan. Reassessment without documentation does not support continuity of care. (C6-C7, Q12, E17-E19)

Why this matters clinically

Respiratory therapy is an iterative process. A treatment recommendation is incomplete unless the therapist defines how effectiveness and safety will be evaluated and what findings will trigger a change in the plan.

Common traps to avoid
  • One improved number proves the treatment worked. Clarify that the whole patient and the original goals must be reassessed.
  • Escalation means the therapist failed. Clarify that recognizing limitations and obtaining additional support is a core safety behavior.
  • Documenting “patient improved” is sufficient. Clarify that objective pre/post findings and the next plan are required.

Apply it

After oxygen therapy, SpO₂ improves from 86% to 93%, RR decreases from 30 to 22/min, and dyspnea improves. The response is favorable, but the therapist should document the intervention and objective response, continue reassessment, and determine whether the patient remains within the target range. (C6-C7, Q12, E19)

A patient receives bronchodilator therapy and oxygen. Wheezing improves and SpO₂ rises, but PaCO₂ remains elevated and the patient becomes drowsier. What should be continued, what must be reassessed, and why is escalation needed?
