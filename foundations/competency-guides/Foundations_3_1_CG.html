Respiratory Care · 3.1 Competency Guide

Flexible Bronchoscopy

This week develops the respiratory therapist’s role before, during, and after flexible bronchoscopy. Students learn to distinguish diagnostic from therapeutic procedures, assess airway and procedural risk, prepare the patient and equipment, support moderate sedation, monitor oxygenation and ventilation, assist with specimen collection and airway interventions, recognize complications, and complete post-procedure reassessment and documentation.

How to use this guide

Present bronchoscopy as a coordinated respiratory procedure rather than a physician-only task. The respiratory therapist must anticipate how the bronchoscope, sedation, topical anesthesia, secretions, bleeding, bronchospasm, and patient positioning can affect airway patency, oxygenation, and ventilation. Require students to connect every procedure choice with preparation, monitoring, complication recognition, immediate response, and reassessment.

C1: Differentiate diagnostic and therapeutic flexible bronchoscopy and match common procedures to the clinical indication.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.E.4 · III.H.2 · III.H.3 · I.A.1-2 · I.A.7-8 · I.B.1.i · III.A.2 · III.I.1.e

What you need to know

  • Diagnostic versus therapeutic purpose - Diagnostic bronchoscopy visualizes the airway and obtains information or specimens. Therapeutic bronchoscopy removes or reduces an obstruction, restores patency, controls selected bleeding, or treats an endobronchial lesion. The same procedure may include both purposes.
  • Team roles - The physician advances and operates the bronchoscope, while the respiratory therapist commonly prepares oxygen and airway equipment, assists with ventilation and topical anesthesia, monitors respiratory status, manages specimens, and responds to complications. Roles vary by policy and credentialing. (C1-C2)
  • The bronchoscope changes respiratory mechanics - The scope occupies airway cross-sectional area, increases resistance, and may impair ventilation, especially through an endotracheal tube or in patients with severe obstruction.
  • Procedure selection follows the clinical problem - Suspected malignancy, infection, hemoptysis, retained secretions, foreign body, stenosis, and endobronchial obstruction require different diagnostic or therapeutic approaches.
  • Confirm the indication and procedure plan - Review the reason for bronchoscopy, anticipated route, planned sampling or therapy, sedation plan, and whether the patient has an artificial airway. (C1-C2)
  • Verify preparation - Confirm identity, consent according to policy, NPO status, allergies, medication history, anticoagulants, relevant imaging, and baseline respiratory status.
  • Review high-risk data - Evaluate platelet count, coagulation studies, hemoglobin, oxygen requirement, ABGs or ETCO₂ when indicated, and recent cardiopulmonary trends. Biopsy or active bleeding risk requires special attention.
  • Assess the airway - Mallampati Class 4, limited mouth opening, reduced neck mobility, facial or airway abnormality, obesity, obstructive sleep apnea, prior difficult intubation, or stridor increases risk. Mallampati is one component, not a complete airway assessment.

Why this matters clinically

Bronchoscopy can rapidly change airway resistance, ventilation, oxygenation, and hemodynamics. Understanding the purpose and the therapist’s role allows preparation to be matched to the actual risk rather than treating every bronchoscopy as routine.

Complications are easier to prevent or treat when risk is identified before sedation and scope insertion. A structured pre-procedure check protects the patient from aspiration, bleeding, medication, oxygenation, and airway emergencies.

Common traps to avoid
  • Diagnostic bronchoscopy only looks at the airway. Clarify that washing, brushing, biopsy, and BAL are diagnostic procedures performed through the working channel.
  • Therapeutic bronchoscopy is unrelated to respiratory therapy. Clarify that oxygen delivery, ventilation, suction, airway support, and complication management are central RT responsibilities.
  • One bronchoscopy cannot be both diagnostic and therapeutic. Clarify that visualization, sampling, secretion removal, and treatment may occur in one session.

Apply it

A patient with lobar atelectasis from a mucus plug undergoes bronchoscopy. The airway is inspected to identify the obstruction, secretions are suctioned, and a specimen is obtained for culture. The procedure therefore has both diagnostic and therapeutic components.

A patient has hemoptysis and a suspected endobronchial lesion. Which parts of the procedure are diagnostic, which could become therapeutic, and what respiratory risks should be anticipated?

C6: Distinguish diagnostic sampling methods, including bronchial washing, brushing, biopsy, and bronchoalveolar lavage, and protect specimen quality through correct handling.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.E.4 · III.H.2 · III.H.3 · I.E.5 · II.B.1-3

What you need to know

  • Diagnostic versus therapeutic purpose - Diagnostic bronchoscopy visualizes the airway and obtains information or specimens. Therapeutic bronchoscopy removes or reduces an obstruction, restores patency, controls selected bleeding, or treats an endobronchial lesion. The same procedure may include both purposes.
  • Team roles - The physician advances and operates the bronchoscope, while the respiratory therapist commonly prepares oxygen and airway equipment, assists with ventilation and topical anesthesia, monitors respiratory status, manages specimens, and responds to complications. Roles vary by policy and credentialing. (C1-C2)
  • The bronchoscope changes respiratory mechanics - The scope occupies airway cross-sectional area, increases resistance, and may impair ventilation, especially through an endotracheal tube or in patients with severe obstruction.
  • Procedure selection follows the clinical problem - Suspected malignancy, infection, hemoptysis, retained secretions, foreign body, stenosis, and endobronchial obstruction require different diagnostic or therapeutic approaches.
  • Bronchial washing - Instilling and suctioning a smaller volume of saline samples secretions from larger airways. It is useful for microbiology or cytology but is less localized than BAL.
  • Bronchial brushing - A brush collects cells from a visible or localized airway lesion and is commonly used for cytology.
  • Endobronchial biopsy - Forceps obtain tissue from a visible lesion. Bleeding risk and specimen integrity require preparation and close observation.
  • Bronchoalveolar lavage - The bronchoscope is wedged in a segment, aliquots of sterile saline are instilled, and fluid is recovered to sample the distal airways and alveoli. BAL is useful for selected infections, diffuse lung disease, hemorrhage, and inflammatory processes.

Each sampling method answers a different clinical question. Correct selection and specimen handling determine whether the procedure yields clinically useful information.
