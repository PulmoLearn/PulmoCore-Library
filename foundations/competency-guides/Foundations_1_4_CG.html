Respiratory Care · 1.4 Competency Guide

Introduction to Pulmonary Function Testing

This week introduces pulmonary function testing as a structured method for measuring ventilatory function and evaluating the quality of a patient’s respiratory maneuvers. Students learn what spirometry measures, distinguish FEV1 from FVC, use the FEV1/FVC ratio to recognize obstructive and possible restrictive patterns, and determine when results are acceptable and repeatable enough to interpret. The emphasis is on connecting valid technique, objective data, and appropriate clinical follow-up rather than assigning a diagnosis from a single number.

How to use this guide

Teach spirometry as both a patient-performance test and a clinical decision-making process. The numbers are meaningful only when the therapist has prepared and coached the patient, obtained technically acceptable and repeatable maneuvers, and interpreted the pattern in context. Require students to follow a consistent sequence: verify test quality, examine the FEV1/FVC ratio, assess FVC and FEV1, identify the most likely ventilatory pattern, and state what additional testing or reassessment is needed before drawing a final conclusion.

C1: Explain the purpose and basic limitations of spirometry and distinguish spirometry from tests of oxygenation, gas exchange, and complete lung volumes.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.3 · I.C.20 · I.D.21

What you need to know

  • Spirometry as a measurement tool – Explain that spirometry measures the volume of gas a patient can move and the speed of forced expiration. It does not directly measure arterial blood gases, oxygen saturation, cardiac output, residual volume, or total lung capacity.
  • Forced Vital Capacity (FVC) – Define FVC as the total volume exhaled forcefully after a maximal inspiration. A reduced FVC may reflect restriction, air trapping, submaximal inspiration, early termination, or poor effort; it should not be interpreted in isolation.
  • Forced Expiratory Volume in One Second (FEV1) – Define FEV1 as the volume exhaled during the first second of the forced maneuver. FEV1 is strongly influenced by airway caliber and expiratory flow limitation.
  • FEV1/FVC ratio – Explain that the ratio describes how much of the FVC is exhaled during the first second. A reduced ratio supports airflow obstruction. A preserved or increased ratio with reduced FVC suggests possible restriction but requires confirmation with lung volumes.
  • Predicted values and clinical context – Introduce that measured values are compared with appropriate reference values and lower limits of normal. Age, sex, height, population reference equations, symptoms, history, and test quality all affect interpretation.
  • Spirometry versus complete pulmonary function testing – Clarify that spirometry cannot directly measure residual volume or total lung capacity. Restriction is confirmed by reduced total lung capacity, not by low FVC alone.

Why this matters clinically

Spirometry is frequently the first objective test used to evaluate airflow limitation and ventilatory patterns. Correctly understanding what the test can and cannot measure prevents overdiagnosis and guides the therapist toward the appropriate next procedure.

Common traps to avoid
  • Spirometry measures every lung volume. Clarify that residual volume, functional residual capacity, and total lung capacity require additional techniques.
  • A low FVC automatically proves restriction. Clarify that low FVC may result from poor effort, early termination, or air trapping and must be confirmed with lung volumes.
  • FEV1 alone determines whether obstruction is present. Clarify that the FEV1/FVC ratio is the primary indicator of airflow obstruction.

Apply it

A patient has FEV1 58% predicted, FVC 92% predicted, and FEV1/FVC 0.58. The reduced ratio with relatively preserved FVC supports an obstructive ventilatory pattern. FEV1 helps describe the degree of airflow limitation, while the ratio identifies the pattern.

A report shows a normal FEV1/FVC ratio but reduced FVC. What explanations should be considered before calling the pattern restrictive? Students should include true restriction, poor inspiration, early termination, air trapping, and inadequate effort, then identify lung-volume testing as the confirmatory step.

C3: Prepare, instruct, and coach a patient through an acceptable forced vital capacity maneuver using clear, patient-centered communication.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.C.20 · I.E.6 · III.I.1.e · I.A.3 · III.I.1.a

What you need to know

  • Patient-centered preparation – Explain the purpose of the test, what the patient will feel, and that the maneuver should not be painful. Address anxiety before labeling a patient as uncooperative. Confirm that the patient understands the sequence and can follow instructions.
  • Position and equipment preparation – Follow laboratory policy for seated or standing position, use an appropriate mouthpiece and nose clip when indicated, and confirm that dentures or other factors do not interfere with the seal. Infection-prevention and equipment-quality procedures remain essential.
  • Maximal inspiration – The patient must inhale completely before beginning the forced exhalation. A submaximal inspiration lowers the measured FVC and may distort the pattern.
  • Rapid, forceful start – Coach the patient to blast the air out immediately and hard after maximal inspiration. Hesitation or a slow start reduces early expiratory flow and can lower FEV1.
  • Continued exhalation – Encourage the patient to keep exhaling until no more air can be expelled or the maneuver meets the applicable end-of-test criteria. Premature termination can falsely lower FVC.
  • Consistent coaching – Demonstrate the maneuver, use the same clear commands for each attempt, observe the patient closely, and provide specific corrective feedback between attempts. Coaching is part of test accuracy, not an optional courtesy.
  • Integrate symptoms and history – Interpret spirometry alongside dyspnea, cough, smoking or exposure history, diagnosis, medications, and prior results. A pattern supports clinical reasoning but does not replace the patient assessment.
  • Repeat before escalating – When technique is inadequate, the first intervention is often education, demonstration, and repeat testing rather than diagnostic labeling.

Why this matters clinically

Spirometry is effort-dependent. The therapist’s explanation, demonstration, observation, and coaching directly affect the validity of the data and therefore the safety of decisions based on the test.

Pulmonary function testing influences diagnosis, treatment, surgical evaluation, disability decisions, and longitudinal monitoring. Clear communication and objective documentation make the results useful to the entire care team and protect the patient from decisions based on misunderstood limitations.

Common traps to avoid
  • An anxious patient is being intentionally difficult. Clarify that anxiety, misunderstanding, pain, fatigue, language, cognition, and technique can all affect performance.
  • Telling the patient to “blow” is enough. Clarify that the patient needs a maximal inspiration, explosive start, sustained effort, and encouragement to continue.
  • The therapist should stop coaching once the maneuver begins. Clarify that active, standardized encouragement helps the patient sustain maximal effort.

Apply it

A patient repeatedly stops exhaling after two seconds. The therapist should not accept the best-looking value or diagnose restriction. The appropriate response is to re-explain and demonstrate the maneuver, identify the specific technique problem, coach the patient through a sustained effort, and repeat the test.

A patient’s efforts vary widely even after instructions. What should the therapist assess before concluding that the patient is not trying? Students should consider comprehension, demonstration quality, anxiety, fatigue, pain, mouth seal, maximal inspiration, start of test, and continued exhalation.

C4: Evaluate spirometry maneuvers for adequate start, continued exhalation, consistency, acceptability, and repeatability before interpreting results.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.C.20 · I.E.6 · III.I.1.e · I.A.3 · I.D.21 · II.B.4.c · I.D.23 · III.I.1.a

What you need to know

  • Patient-centered preparation – Explain the purpose of the test, what the patient will feel, and that the maneuver should not be painful. Address anxiety before labeling a patient as uncooperative. Confirm that the patient understands the sequence and can follow instructions.
  • Position and equipment preparation – Follow laboratory policy for seated or standing position, use an appropriate mouthpiece and nose clip when indicated, and confirm that dentures or other factors do not interfere with the seal. C6: Recommend appropriate next steps, including repeat testing, bronchodilator assessment, or lung-volume testing, when spirometry findings or test quality require clarification.
