Arterial Blood Gas Interpretation

This week develops a disciplined approach to arterial blood gas interpretation. Students learn to evaluate acid-base balance, ventilation, and oxygenation as related but distinct components of respiratory status. They begin with normal values and a repeatable pH-first sequence, then identify primary respiratory and metabolic disorders, recognize compensation, distinguish acute from chronic ventilatory disturbances, and detect mixed disorders. The central goal is not memorizing labels; it is explaining what the ABG reveals about the patient’s physiology and determining when the pattern indicates urgent respiratory compromise.

How to use this guide

Teach one consistent sequence every time: verify the sample and clinical context, classify the pH, identify the component that explains the pH direction, evaluate compensation, assess oxygenation separately, and then correlate the ABG with the patient. Require students to say what is abnormal, what physiologic process caused it, whether compensation is expected, and what additional assessment or intervention is needed. Repeatedly reinforce that a normal pH does not guarantee a normal ABG and that compensation never eliminates the underlying disorder.

C1: Recall normal adult ABG reference ranges and distinguish acidemia, alkalemia, normocapnia, hypercapnia, hypocapnia, and hypoxemia.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.2 · I.D.5

What you need to know

  • Normal reference ranges - Use approximate adult arterial ranges of pH 7.35-7.45, PaCO₂ 35-45 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 22-26 mEq/L, and PaO₂ interpreted in relation to age, inspired oxygen, and clinical condition. Students should know these ranges before attempting pattern recognition.
  • Begin with pH - Classify the pH as acidemic, alkalemic, or within the reference range. The pH establishes the direction of the disturbance and prevents students from labeling an elevated or decreased component without determining its effect.
  • Identify the primary component - PaCO₂ behaves as an acid: an increase lowers pH and a decrease raises pH. HCO₃⁻ behaves as a base: a decrease lowers pH and an increase raises pH. Match the component moving in the same direction as the pH disturbance.
  • Continue beyond the label - After naming the primary disorder, evaluate compensation, oxygenation, chronicity, and clinical context. An interpretation is incomplete if it stops at “respiratory acidosis.” (C2-C7)

Why this matters clinically

ABGs become safer and easier to interpret when students use the same sequence every time. A structured process reduces errors caused by jumping directly to PaCO₂, overlooking oxygenation, or assuming a normal pH means normal physiology.

Common traps to avoid
  • A normal pH means the ABG is normal. Clarify that opposing abnormalities can return pH toward normal while significant disease remains.
  • PaCO₂ is interpreted as a base because carbon dioxide is exhaled. Clarify that PaCO₂ functions as the respiratory acid component.
  • Oxygenation should be interpreted before acid-base status. Clarify that all components matter, but using a consistent pH-first acid-base sequence prevents classification errors.

Apply it

A patient has pH 7.29, PaCO₂ 52 mmHg, and HCO₃⁻ 24 mEq/L. The pH is acidemic, the elevated PaCO₂ explains the acidemia, and bicarbonate is still normal. The primary disorder is acute respiratory acidosis.

A student sees pH 7.40, PaCO₂ 40, HCO₃⁻ 24, and PaO₂ 55. What is normal, what is abnormal, and why must oxygenation be addressed even though the acid-base values are normal?

C2: Apply a pH-first interpretation sequence to identify primary respiratory and metabolic acid-base disorders.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.2 · I.D.5 · I.C.3 · I.D.4-5 · I.A.8

What you need to know

  • Normal reference ranges - Use approximate adult arterial ranges of pH 7.35-7.45, PaCO₂ 35-45 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 22-26 mEq/L, and PaO₂ interpreted in relation to age, inspired oxygen, and clinical condition. Students should know these ranges before attempting pattern recognition.

  • Respiratory alkalosis - A primary fall in PaCO₂ raises pH and reflects alveolar hyperventilation. Causes include hypoxemia, pain, anxiety, fever, sepsis, pregnancy, and excessive mechanical ventilation.

C3: Differentiate acute, chronic, uncompensated, partially compensated, and fully compensated respiratory disturbances.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.D.5 · I.A.7 · I.A.8 · III.E.2 · III.G.1

What you need to know

  • Compensation limits pH change - The nonprimary system changes in a direction that opposes the pH disturbance. Respiratory compensation occurs quickly; renal compensation requires more time. Compensation does not reverse the primary disorder.
  • Acute respiratory disorders - In acute respiratory acidosis or alkalosis, bicarbonate has changed little because the kidneys have not had time to respond substantially.
  • Chronic respiratory disorders - Persistent PaCO₂ abnormalities lead to renal bicarbonate retention or excretion. A near-normal pH with abnormal PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ may represent a fully compensated chronic respiratory disorder.
  • Acute-on-chronic failure - A chronically elevated bicarbonate with a newly acidemic pH and markedly increased PaCO₂ suggests acute worsening superimposed on chronic CO₂ retention.
  • Correlate the ABG with the patient - Interpretation must include respiratory rate and depth, work of breathing, mental status, SpO₂, ETCO₂, oxygen device, diagnosis, medications, and recent interventions.
  • Identify immediate threats - Severe hypoxemia, worsening acidemia, rising PaCO₂ with altered mental status, respiratory fatigue, or failure to improve despite oxygen requires prompt escalation. (C5-C7)
  • Use trends - Compare with prior ABGs and monitor data. A change from compensated chronic hypercapnia to acidemia may be more important than the absolute PaCO₂ alone.
  • Reassess after intervention - After oxygen therapy, airway management, ventilator adjustment, bronchodilator therapy, reversal of sedation, or other treatment, reassess symptoms, vital signs, SpO₂, ETCO₂, and ABG data as appropriate.

Why this matters clinically

Chronicity changes the meaning of the ABG. Recognizing renal compensation helps distinguish a stable chronic condition from a new life-threatening deterioration in ventilation.

An ABG is a decision-support tool, not an isolated laboratory exercise. Clinical integration determines whether the result reflects a stable chronic pattern, an evolving emergency, or a response to treatment.

Common traps to avoid
  • Compensated means corrected. Clarify that the primary disorder remains present.
  • A patient with COPD and elevated PaCO₂ is always at baseline. Clarify that pH, bicarbonate, symptoms, and prior values determine whether acute worsening is present.
  • Bicarbonate changes immediately in respiratory disorders. Clarify the different time courses of chemical buffering and renal compensation.

Apply it

A patient with long-standing COPD has pH 7.36, PaCO₂ 60, and HCO₃⁻ 34. The near-normal pH with elevated PaCO₂ and elevated bicarbonate indicates compensated chronic respiratory acidosis. If the pH fell to 7.31 while PaCO₂ rose to 68, acute-on-chronic ventilatory failure should be considered.

A patient with chronic hypercapnia becomes confused and the pH falls despite an elevated bicarbonate. Which finding indicates chronic compensation, and which finding indicates acute deterioration?

C4: Evaluate expected compensatory responses and recognize when PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ changes suggest a mixed disorder.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.2 · I.D.5 · I.A.8 · I.A.7

What you need to know

  • Metabolic acidosis - A primary fall in HCO₃⁻ lowers pH. Causes include lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis, renal failure, bicarbonate loss, and selected toxic ingestions. Respiratory compensation occurs through hyperventilation and a fall in PaCO₂.
  • Kussmaul respirations - Deep, rapid breathing in severe metabolic acidosis is a compensatory effort to lower PaCO₂. Suppressing this response without providing adequate ventilation can worsen acidemia.
  • Metabolic alkalosis - A primary rise in HCO₃⁻ raises pH. Causes include gastric acid loss, diuretic use, volume contraction, and excess alkali. Compensation is limited hypoventilation, but severe hypoxemia should not be accepted as “appropriate compensation.”
  • Correlate with chemistry - Electrolytes, glucose, lactate, renal function, chloride, ketones, and the anion gap help explain metabolic disturbances and reveal mixed disorders.
  • Compensation limits pH change - The nonprimary system changes in a direction that opposes the pH disturbance. Respiratory compensation occurs quickly; renal compensation requires more time. Compensation does not reverse the primary disorder.
  • Acute respiratory disorders - In acute respiratory acidosis or alkalosis, bicarbonate has changed little because the kidneys have not had time to respond substantially.
  • Chronic respiratory disorders - Persistent PaCO₂ abnormalities lead to renal bicarbonate retention or excretion. A near-normal pH with abnormal PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ may represent a fully compensated chronic respiratory disorder.
  • Acute-on-chronic failure - A chronically elevated bicarbonate with a newly acidemic pH and markedly increased PaCO₂ suggests acute worsening superimposed on chronic CO₂ retention.

Why this matters clinically

Metabolic disorders often present first as respiratory changes because the lungs compensate rapidly. Respiratory therapists must recognize when abnormal breathing is a compensatory response rather than the primary problem.

Chronicity changes the meaning of the ABG. Recognizing renal compensation helps distinguish a stable chronic condition from a new life-threatening deterioration in ventilation.

Common traps to avoid
  • Low PaCO₂ always means primary respiratory alkalosis. Clarify that it may be appropriate compensation for metabolic acidosis.
  • Kussmaul breathing should be slowed because the respiratory rate is high. Clarify that the patient is attempting to maintain pH by removing CO₂.
  • Respiratory compensation can fully normalize a severe metabolic disorder. Clarify that compensation limits pH change but does not correct the cause.

Apply it

A patient with diabetic ketoacidosis has pH 7.20, PaCO₂ 22, and HCO₃⁻ 9. The low bicarbonate explains the acidemia; the low PaCO₂ reflects compensatory hyperventilation. The primary disorder is metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation.

A patient with metabolic acidosis becomes less tachypneic and more somnolent. Why might the slowing respiratory rate indicate deterioration rather than improvement?

C5: Assess oxygenation independently from acid-base balance and ventilation using PaO₂, SpO₂, oxygen delivery, and the clinical setting.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.2 · I.D.2 · I.E.8 · I.A.7 · I.A.8 · III.E.2 · III.G.1

What you need to know

  • Evaluate PaO₂ separately - A patient can have normal pH and PaCO₂ while experiencing severe hypoxemia. Always interpret PaO₂ in relation to FiO₂, oxygen device, age, altitude, and clinical status.
  • PaO₂ versus SpO₂ - PaO₂ reflects dissolved oxygen tension, while SpO₂ estimates hemoglobin saturation. Neither alone defines ventilation, oxygen content, or tissue oxygen delivery.
  • Oxygen therapy changes interpretation - A PaO₂ that appears acceptable on room air may be inadequate on high inspired oxygen. Document the oxygen device and concentration at the time of sampling.
  • Do not let acid-base normality delay action - Normal acid-base values do not reduce the urgency of a PaO₂ of 55 mmHg in a symptomatic patient. Treat significant hypoxemia while continuing assessment.
  • Correlate the ABG with the patient - Interpretation must include respiratory rate and depth, work of breathing, mental status, SpO₂, ETCO₂, oxygen device, diagnosis, medications, and recent interventions.
  • Identify immediate threats - Severe hypoxemia, worsening acidemia, rising PaCO₂ with altered mental status, respiratory fatigue, or failure to improve despite oxygen requires prompt escalation. (C5-C7)
  • Use trends - Compare with prior ABGs and monitor data. A change from compensated chronic hypercapnia to acidemia may be more important than the absolute PaCO₂ alone.
  • Reassess after intervention - After oxygen therapy, airway management, ventilator adjustment, bronchodilator therapy, reversal of sedation, or other treatment, reassess symptoms, vital signs, SpO₂, ETCO₂, and ABG data as appropriate.

Why this matters clinically

Acid-base balance, ventilation, and oxygenation require different assessments and may require different interventions. Separating them prevents the common error of declaring the ABG normal because pH and PaCO₂ are normal.

An ABG is a decision-support tool, not an isolated laboratory exercise. Clinical integration determines whether the result reflects a stable chronic pattern, an evolving emergency, or a response to treatment.

Common traps to avoid
  • A normal PaCO₂ means gas exchange is normal. Clarify that oxygenation can be impaired independently.
  • Normal SpO₂ rules out ventilatory failure. Clarify that supplemental oxygen may maintain saturation while CO₂ rises.
  • PaO₂ should be interpreted without knowing the oxygen device. Clarify that inspired oxygen is essential context.

Apply it

A patient has pH 7.40, PaCO₂ 40, HCO₃⁻ 24, and PaO₂ 55 on room air. Acid-base status and ventilation are normal, but oxygenation is impaired and requires prompt evaluation and treatment.

A patient’s SpO₂ is 97% on 4 L/min nasal cannula, but the patient is somnolent with RR 8 and elevated ETCO₂. Which problem can still be present despite the reassuring saturation?

Apply it

A mechanically ventilated patient has pH 7.22, PaCO₂ 65, and HCO₃⁻ 26. The pattern indicates acute respiratory acidosis from inadequate alveolar ventilation. The therapist should assess the patient, airway, circuit, delivered tidal volume, minute ventilation, and ventilator synchrony before changing settings.

A postoperative patient is difficult to arouse with RR 8/min, ETCO₂ 58, and SpO₂ 97% on oxygen. Which data show a ventilation problem, and why is the saturation potentially misleading?
