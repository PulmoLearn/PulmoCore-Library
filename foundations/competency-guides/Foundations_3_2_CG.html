Respiratory Care · 3.2 Competency Guide

ECG Interpretation & Cardiac Rhythm Analysis

This week develops a systematic approach to basic ECG interpretation for respiratory care practice. Students learn ECG paper timing, waveform and interval terminology, heart-rate calculation, rhythm regularity, sinus and atrial rhythm recognition, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, asystole, pulseless electrical activity, and the relationship between the monitor rhythm and the patient’s pulse, perfusion, mental status, oxygenation, and ventilation.

How to use this guide

Present the ECG as one source of patient data rather than a diagnosis by itself. Require students to use the same sequence every time: verify the patient and signal, determine rate, assess regularity, identify P waves, evaluate PR and QRS duration, name the rhythm, then correlate the tracing with pulse and perfusion. Rhythm recognition should always lead to a clinically appropriate response, including respiratory support, escalation, CPR, synchronized cardioversion, or defibrillation when indicated.

C1: Explain ECG paper timing and identify the P wave, PR interval, QRS complex, ST segment, T wave, and QT interval.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.A.4 · I.C.1 · I.D.1 · III.H.9 · I.A.7 · I.A.8

What you need to know

  • ECG paper timing - At the standard speed of 25 mm/sec, one small box equals 0.04 second and one large box equals 0.20 second. Five large boxes equal 1 second, and 30 large boxes equal 6 seconds.
  • P wave - Represents atrial depolarization. Evaluate whether P waves are present, upright and uniform, and whether one P wave precedes each QRS. (C1-C2)
  • PR interval - Measured from the beginning of the P wave to the beginning of the QRS. A normal adult PR interval is approximately 0.12-0.20 second. (C1-C2)
  • QRS complex - Represents ventricular depolarization. A narrow QRS is generally less than 0.12 second; a wide complex suggests delayed intraventricular conduction or ventricular origin. (C1-C2, C5-C6)
  • ST segment, T wave, and QT interval - The ST segment and T wave reflect ventricular repolarization. The QT interval includes ventricular depolarization and repolarization and changes with heart rate. This lesson emphasizes recognition and escalation rather than advanced ischemia diagnosis.
  • Calibration and artifact - Before interpreting a rhythm, verify paper speed, gain when available, lead attachment, patient movement, electrical interference, and whether the tracing matches the patient.
  • Step 1: Determine rate - Select a rate method based on rhythm regularity and available strip length. (C2-C3)
  • Step 2: Determine regularity - Compare R-R intervals and, when relevant, P-P intervals. Describe the rhythm as regular, regularly irregular, or irregularly irregular.

Why this matters clinically

Accurate rhythm interpretation depends on time measurement. Misreading one small box as 0.20 second creates fivefold errors in rate and interval calculations. Signal verification also prevents artifact or disconnected leads from being mistaken for a lethal dysrhythmia.

A repeatable sequence improves accuracy under stress. The same strip may represent a stable rhythm, an unstable rhythm, or pulseless electrical activity depending on the patient’s pulse and perfusion.

Common traps to avoid
  • Every box has the same time value regardless of paper speed. Clarify that the standard values assume 25 mm/sec.
  • The QRS represents the mechanical pulse. Clarify that electrical depolarization may occur without effective mechanical contraction.
  • A flat line always means true asystole. Clarify that lead disconnection, low gain, or artifact must be checked while CPR begins when the patient is pulseless.

Apply it

A rhythm strip contains 18 small boxes between two consecutive R waves. Using 1500 ÷ 18 gives approximately 83 beats/min. The student should then continue the analysis rather than naming a rhythm from rate alone. (C1-C3, Q1)

C4: Recognize normal sinus rhythm, sinus bradycardia, sinus tachycardia, and sinus arrhythmia and correlate the rhythm with patient status.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.C.1 · I.D.1 · I.A.7 · I.A.8 · I.B.1-2 · III.E.2

What you need to know

  • Step 1: Determine rate - Select a rate method based on rhythm regularity and available strip length. (C2-C3)
  • Step 2: Determine regularity - Compare R-R intervals and, when relevant, P-P intervals. Describe the rhythm as regular, regularly irregular, or irregularly irregular.
  • Step 3: Evaluate atrial activity and conduction - Identify P waves, determine whether each P is followed by a QRS, and measure PR consistency. (C1-C2)
  • Step 4: Evaluate ventricular conduction - Measure QRS width and morphology. Narrow complexes usually originate above the ventricles; wide bizarre complexes raise concern for ventricular origin or aberrant conduction.
  • Name the rhythm only after completing the sequence - Pattern recognition is useful, but a consistent method reduces premature closure.
  • Correlate with the patient - Check pulse, blood pressure, skin signs, mental status, chest discomfort, dyspnea, oxygenation, ventilation, and hemodynamic stability.
  • 300 method - For regular rhythms, divide 300 by the number of large boxes between consecutive R waves. This is fast but less precise when the R wave does not fall on a bold line.
  • 1500 method - For regular rhythms, divide 1500 by the number of small boxes between consecutive R waves. This method is more precise.

Why this matters clinically

A repeatable sequence improves accuracy under stress. The same strip may represent a stable rhythm, an unstable rhythm, or pulseless electrical activity depending on the patient’s pulse and perfusion.

Correct rate calculation helps distinguish bradycardia, normal rate, tachycardia, atrial flutter conduction patterns, SVT, and ventricular tachycardia. The wrong method can produce a misleading estimate in irregular rhythms.

Common traps to avoid
  • Rate alone identifies the rhythm. Clarify that multiple rhythms can share the same rate.
  • A monitor rhythm automatically confirms a pulse. Clarify that the pulse must be assessed clinically.
  • Once the rhythm is named, the assessment is finished. Clarify that patient correlation determines urgency and treatment.

Apply it

A monitor shows an organized rhythm at 70 beats/min, but the patient is unresponsive and pulseless. The electrical rhythm does not equal effective circulation; this is pulseless electrical activity and requires CPR and treatment of reversible causes.

Two patients have the same wide-complex tachycardia. One is alert with a blood pressure; the other is pulseless. How does the response differ?

C7: Recognize ventricular fibrillation, asystole, and pulseless electrical activity and distinguish shockable from non-shockable cardiac-arrest rhythms.
NBRC 2027 alignment: I.C.1 · I.D.1 · I.A.7 · I.A.8 · III.G.1 · III.H.9

What you need to know

  • Step 1: Determine rate - Select a rate method based on rhythm regularity and available strip length. (C2-C3)
  • Step 2: Determine regularity - Compare R-R intervals and, when relevant, P-P intervals. Describe the rhythm as regular, regularly irregular, or irregularly irregular.
  • Step 3: Evaluate atrial activity and conduction - Identify P waves, determine whether each P is followed by a QRS, and measure PR consistency. (C1-C2)
  • Step 4: Evaluate ventricular conduction - Measure QRS width and morphology. Narrow complexes usually originate above the ventricles; wide bizarre complexes raise concern for ventricular origin or aberrant conduction.
  • Name the rhythm only after completing the sequence - Pattern recognition is useful, but a consistent method reduces premature closure.
  • Correlate with the patient - Check pulse, blood pressure, skin signs, mental status, chest discomfort, dyspnea, oxygenation, ventilation, and hemodynamic stability.
  • Ventricular tachycardia - Usually a regular wide-complex rhythm, often above 150 beats/min, with bizarre QRS complexes and no consistent relationship to P waves. Treat wide-complex tachycardia as VT until proven otherwise in an emergency context.
  • VT with a pulse - Determine stability. Hypotension, altered mental status, shock, ischemic chest discomfort, or acute heart failure indicates instability and need for synchronized cardioversion according to protocol. Stable monomorphic VT may receive antiarrhythmic therapy.

Why this matters clinically

A repeatable sequence improves accuracy under stress. The same strip may represent a stable rhythm, an unstable rhythm, or pulseless electrical activity depending on the patient’s pulse and perfusion.

Life-threatening rhythm recognition must be immediate and tied to pulse assessment. The distinction between shockable and non-shockable rhythms determines whether defibrillation is appropriate, but high-quality CPR and treatment of reversible causes remain central.

  • A monitor rhythm automatically confirms a pulse. Clarify that the pulse must be assessed clinically.
  • Once the rhythm is named, the assessment is finished. Clarify that patient correlation determines urgency and treatment.
