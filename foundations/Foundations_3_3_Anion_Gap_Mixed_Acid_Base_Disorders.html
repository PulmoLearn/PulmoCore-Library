Anion Gap, Bicarbonate Gap & Mixed Acid-Base Disorders

When metabolic acidosis is present, the anion gap identifies whether unmeasured acids have accumulated or bicarbonate has been replaced by chloride. The course bicarbonate gap helps identify an additional metabolic process. This lesson connects the arithmetic to laboratory quality, expected compensation, clinical mechanism, severity, intervention, and serial reassessment.

Validate the Laboratory Story Before Calculating

Use sodium, chloride, and chemistry-panel total CO₂ collected at the same time. Confirm units, recent fluids or treatment, and whether the ABG bicarbonate and chemistry values are reasonably consistent. A mathematically correct calculation from mismatched values can produce a false mixed disorder.

Same time point
Do not combine pre-treatment electrolytes with a post-treatment ABG.
Local reference range
The normal gap varies by analyzer and whether potassium is included.
Albumin effect
Low albumin lowers the expected gap and can conceal unmeasured acid accumulation.
Plausibility
Recheck arithmetic, transcription, specimen quality, and internal consistency.
Clinical mechanism
A high gap narrows the mechanism; it does not name the exact disease.
Trend
A falling pH with rising lactate or gap may indicate worsening despite one improved value.
Calculation sequence: Confirm metabolic acidosis → verify laboratory values → calculate anion gap → identify likely mechanism → evaluate expected compensation → use the course bicarbonate gap only after AGMA is established.

Additional ABG Tools: Base Excess & Lactate

Before calculating anion gaps, two supporting ABG values help confirm metabolic acid-base status: base excess and lactate. Understanding what they reflect — and their limitations — prepares you to interpret complex clinical situations.

Base Excess (BE)

A calculated value derived from pH and PaCO₂ — it reflects the metabolic component of acid-base status after accounting for the respiratory contribution.

Positive Metabolic alkalosis present
-2 to +2 Normal range
Negative Metabolic acidosis present
Limitation: BE does not tell you whether the metabolic change is primary or a compensation for a respiratory disorder. Interpret with the full clinical picture.

Lactate

Lactate rises when production exceeds clearance. Tissue hypoperfusion is important, but elevated lactate may also reflect adrenergic stimulation, medications, seizures, severe work of breathing, impaired hepatic clearance, or other disease.

<2 mmol/L Normal lactate
>2 mmol/L Elevated — interpret mechanism and trend

Normal pathway: Pyruvate → Acetyl-CoA → Krebs cycle (aerobic)

Clinical interpretation: Compare lactate with perfusion, oxygen delivery, medications, work of breathing, hepatic clearance, and serial trend.

Two Types of Metabolic Acidosis

When the ABG shows metabolic acidosis (low pH, low HCO₃⁻), your next question is: why is the bicarbonate low? There are two fundamentally different answers, and the anion gap tells you which one is occurring.

AGMA — Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis

Cause: Excess acid is added to the blood.

The acid displaces bicarbonate — but the acid's anion (unmeasured negative ion) stays in the blood, raising the anion gap above normal.

AG > 12 = AGMA
Common causes: Lactic acidosis, DKA, AKA, uremia, salicylate, methanol

NAGMA — Non-Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis

Cause: Bicarbonate is lost from the body.

When bicarbonate leaves (through GI tract or kidneys), chloride rises to maintain electrical balance — so the anion gap stays normal.

AG ≤ 12 = NAGMA
Common causes: Diarrhea, renal tubular acidosis, urinary bicarbonate loss, excess chloride-rich saline

Calculating the Anion Gap

The anion gap is calculated from three electrolytes that are routinely measured in a basic metabolic panel. It reflects the presence of unmeasured anions (acids) in the blood.

AG = Na⁺ − ( Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻ )
Normal AG = 12 ± 4  |  Normal range: 8–16  |  High AG > 12
AG ResultClassificationInterpretation
≤ 12Normal AGNAGMA — bicarbonate has been lost, not excess acid
> 12High AGAGMA — unmeasured acid is present in the blood
> 16Markedly high AGStrongly suggests lactic acidosis, DKA, or uremia

Worked Examples

Example A — Normal AG
Na⁺ = 140  |  Cl⁻ = 104  |  HCO₃⁻ = 24
AG = 140 − (104 + 24) = 12 Result: Normal AG → NAGMA
Example B — High AG
Na⁺ = 140  |  Cl⁻ = 104  |  HCO₃⁻ = 16
AG = 140 − (104 + 16) = 20 Result: High AG → AGMA

Types of Ketoacidosis & AGMA Causes

When the anion gap is elevated (AGMA), the next step is identifying the cause. The most common causes of high anion gap metabolic acidosis have specific clinical clues that help confirm them.

AGMA CauseAGKey Confirming Clues
Lactic Acidosis>16Signs of hypoxia or hypoperfusion; Lactate >2 mmol/L
DKA (Diabetic Ketoacidosis)>16Glucose >300 mg%; elevated ketones; type 1 diabetes history; fruity breath
AKA (Alcohol Ketoacidosis)>16Glucose <300 mg%; elevated ketones; ETOH history; starvation component
SKA (Starvation Ketoacidosis)ElevatedNo food intake for 3–14 days; glucose <300 mg%; elevated ketones
NAGMA8–16Diarrhea, renal tubular acidosis, urinary bicarbonate loss, or chloride-rich saline exposure
DKA in Depth — Diabetic Ketoacidosis

In DKA, the body cannot use insulin (type 1) or lacks sufficient insulin (type 2) to convert glucose to energy. The liver converts fat to ketones as an alternative energy source. Ketones are acidic — when they accumulate, they drop blood pH, causing metabolic acidosis with a high anion gap.

Signs and symptoms: Glucose >300 mg%, extreme thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, confusion, stomach pain, nausea, fruity-smelling breath (key clinical clue — acetone), blurry vision, fainting.

Why fruity breath? Acetone (a ketone) is volatile and exhaled through the lungs. This is a bedside clue for the RT during assessment.

AKA vs. DKA — Key Distinguishing Feature

Both DKA and AKA show elevated ketones and high anion gap metabolic acidosis. The differentiator is glucose level:

  • DKA: Glucose >300 mg% — hyperglycemia is the trigger
  • AKA: Glucose <300 mg% — alcohol + starvation is the trigger, not high glucose

AKA typically occurs after a period of heavy alcohol use followed by starvation (not eating). The patient's history of alcohol use (ETOH) combined with normal or low glucose and elevated ketones is the diagnostic pattern.

Expected Respiratory Compensation in Metabolic Acidosis

Before labeling a mixed respiratory disorder, compare the measured PaCO₂ with the expected compensatory range.

Expected PaCO₂ = (1.5 × HCO₃⁻) + 8 ± 2
Measured PaCO₂InterpretationClinical meaning
Within expected rangeAppropriate respiratory compensationThe lungs are responding as expected.
Above expected rangeConcurrent respiratory acidosisVentilation is inadequate; fatigue, CNS depression, obstruction, or respiratory failure may be present.
Below expected rangeConcurrent respiratory alkalosisAn independent hyperventilation stimulus may be present, such as sepsis, pain, liver disease, or salicylate effect.
Near-normal pH warning: Markedly abnormal PaCO₂ and bicarbonate with a pH near 7.40 may reflect compensation or two opposing primary disorders. A normal-looking pH does not end the analysis.

The Bicarbonate Gap — Detecting Hidden Mixed Disorders

Once you have confirmed AGMA, the course bicarbonate gap answers a second question: Is there an additional hidden metabolic disorder on top of the AGMA?

The key insight is this: in a pure AGMA, every mole of acid added should consume exactly one mole of bicarbonate. If the bicarbonate is lower or higher than expected, a second process must be occurring.

Step 1:   AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻)
Step 2:   BG = Patient HCO₃⁻ + Measured AG
Course interpretation: below ~22 suggests AGMA + NAGMA; 22–26 supports predominantly pure AGMA; above ~26–28 suggests AGMA + metabolic alkalosis

Bicarbonate Gap Interpretation

< 20
AGMA + NAGMA — Hidden bicarbonate loss. A second process is consuming HCO₃⁻ beyond what the AGMA alone would explain.
22 – 26
Pure AGMA only — The bicarbonate deficit matches what the anion gap rise would predict. No hidden second disorder.
> 28
AGMA + Metabolic Alkalosis — The bicarbonate is higher than expected, meaning metabolic alkalosis (HCO₃⁻ excess) is also present alongside the AGMA.

Mixed Respiratory & Metabolic Disorders

Mixed disorders occur when two separate acid-base disturbances are present simultaneously — one respiratory and one metabolic, or two metabolic processes. These are identified when the compensation is more or less than expected, or when clinical context reveals two competing pathologies.

Resp. Acidosis + Metabolic Acidosis

Example: Acute pulmonary edema after MI

Poor cardiac output→ Tissue hypoxia→ Lactic acidosis
Pulmonary edema→ Poor alveolar ventilation→ CO₂ retention
Both disorders drive pH DOWN. Severe acidemia possible.

Resp. Alkalosis + Metabolic Alkalosis

Example: Hepatic cirrhosis + diuretics

Hepatopulmonary syndrome→ Dyspnea→ Resp. alkalosis
Diuretics→ Renin-angiotensin activation→ HCO₃⁻ retention
Both disorders drive pH UP. Severe alkalemia possible.

Resp. Acidosis + Metabolic Alkalosis

Example: COPD patient given diuretics for CHF

Chronic COPD→ CO₂ retention→ Resp. acidosis
Diuretics→ H⁺ + Cl⁻ loss→ HCO₃⁻ rise
Disorders oppose each other — pH may be near normal despite two active processes.

Resp. Alkalosis + Metabolic Acidosis

Example: Chronic renal failure + anxiety

Chronic renal failure→ Uremia→ Metabolic acidosis
Anxiety / pain→ Hyperventilation→ CO₂ blown off
Disorders oppose each other — pH depends on which process dominates.

Metabolic Acidosis + Metabolic Alkalosis

Example: Chronic renal failure + vomiting

Chronic renal failure→ Cannot excrete acids→ Metabolic acidosis
Vomiting→ H⁺ and Cl⁻ lost→ Metabolic alkalosis
Two metabolic processes in opposite directions — bicarbonate gap helps unmask the NAGMA component.

Connecting the Calculations — Step by Step

Work through this patient case performing each calculation and clinical interpretation in sequence.

Stage 1 — Classify the ABG

ABG results: pH 7.10, PaCO₂ 14 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ = 4 mEq/L

The pH is 7.10 (acidic). PaCO₂ is 14 (very low — blowing off CO₂). HCO₃⁻ is 4 (very low).

What is the primary acid-base disorder?

Stage 2 — Calculate the Anion Gap

Electrolytes: Na⁺ = 128, Cl⁻ = 90, HCO₃⁻ = 4

Using AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻), the anion gap is 128 − (90 + 4) = 128 − 94 = 34.

How do you classify an anion gap of 34?

Stage 3 — Calculate the Bicarbonate Gap

AG = 34  |  Normal AG = 12  |  Patient HCO₃⁻ = 4

Step 2: BG = HCO₃⁻ + measured AG = 4 + 34 = 38

A course bicarbonate gap of 38 falls in which range?

Stage 4 — Same Patient, Different Chloride

Now repeat the bicarbonate gap calculation but with Cl⁻ = 100 instead of 90. Everything else stays the same (Na⁺ = 128, HCO₃⁻ = 4).

AG = 128 − (100 + 4) = 24.   BG = HCO₃⁻ + measured AG = 4 + 24 = 28.

With a course bicarbonate gap of 28, what does this mean?

Case Summary — Key Calculation Insights

Problem A (Cl⁻ = 90): AG = 34, BG = 38. → AGMA with a higher-than-expected course bicarbonate gap; consider concurrent metabolic alkalosis and verify context. The bicarbonate deficit is fully explained by the high anion gap.
Problem B (Cl⁻ = 100): AG = 24, BG = 28. → Borderline-high course bicarbonate gap; interpret with vomiting, diuresis, chloride status, and treatment timing. The higher chloride (lower bicarbonate) reveals a hidden second process also consuming HCO₃⁻.
Key insight: The course bicarbonate gap compares the measured bicarbonate with the size of the anion gap. Use it after confirming AGMA, then verify the result against baseline bicarbonate, albumin, renal status, respiratory disease, treatment timing, and the patient’s mechanism.

FAQ

Common Questions — Anion & Bicarbonate Gap

Why does the bicarbonate fall when acid is added to the blood?

When an acid (H⁺) is added, it immediately reacts with bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) as a buffer: H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ → CO₂ + H₂O. The bicarbonate is consumed in neutralizing the acid. This is why metabolic acidosis — regardless of whether it is AGMA or NAGMA — always shows a low bicarbonate on the ABG.

What makes AGMA different from NAGMA if bicarbonate falls in both?

In AGMA, an acid enters the blood with its own anion (a negatively charged particle). When the acid reacts with bicarbonate, the acid's anion remains unmeasured in the blood — this is what raises the anion gap. In NAGMA, bicarbonate simply leaves the body (through stool or urine). No new acid anion enters, so the anion gap stays normal. Chloride rises to fill the charge balance instead.

Why is the normal anion gap set at 12?

Even in a healthy person, there are unmeasured anions in the blood — albumin, phosphate, sulfate, and organic acids. These naturally create a small gap between the measured cation (Na⁺) and the measured anions (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻). The normal value of 12 (range 8–16) reflects these always-present unmeasured anions. A gap above 16 means abnormal unmeasured anions (like lactate or ketones) have been added.

When would I actually calculate the bicarbonate gap in clinical practice?

Only after you have already confirmed AGMA. If the anion gap is normal (NAGMA), there is no reason to calculate the bicarbonate gap. But once you find a high anion gap, the bicarbonate gap answers: "Is there ALSO a hidden second metabolic disorder?" This matters clinically because a patient with DKA who is also having severe diarrhea needs treatment for both, not just DKA. The bicarbonate gap surfaces that hidden NAGMA component.

How do I tell DKA from AKA if both have elevated ketones?

The glucose level is the key distinguishing feature. DKA: glucose >300 mg% (hyperglycemia is the trigger — insulin deficiency prevents glucose from entering cells). AKA: glucose <300 mg% (starvation + alcohol means the patient has not been eating — glucose may even be low). Both will have elevated ketones and high anion gap metabolic acidosis. Add the patient history — ETOH use and starvation = AKA; diabetes + missed insulin = DKA.
