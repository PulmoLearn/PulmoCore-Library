Interpreting Clinical & Laboratory Data

Respiratory therapists use lab data to identify anemia, infection, acid-base clues, fluid status, cardiac causes of dyspnea, sputum patterns, bleeding risk, tuberculosis testing, allergy testing, and respiratory microorganisms. This lesson teaches beginner pattern recognition while emphasizing that labs support clinical assessment rather than replace it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to identify common respiratory-relevant labs, recognize basic abnormal patterns, connect sputum findings and microbiology categories to likely causes, and recommend appropriate next diagnostic steps.

1. Interpret CBC clues

2. Connect electrolytes

3. Use sputum and tests

Clinical Lab Data: What Is the RT Looking For?

Lab results are not just numbers. They help explain why a patient is short of breath, hypoxemic, weak, febrile, bleeding, infected, fluid overloaded, or not responding as expected. The RT role is to connect lab clues with assessment findings, monitors, imaging, and treatment priorities.

Complete Blood Count: Oxygen Carrying Capacity and Infection Clues

A CBC helps identify whether the patient has enough red blood cells and hemoglobin to carry oxygen, and whether the white blood cell pattern suggests infection, inflammation, allergy/asthma, or other immune response.

LabBeginner InterpretationRT Connection
Low RBC/Hb/HctAnemia pattern.Less oxygen carrying capacity can worsen dyspnea/fatigue.
High RBC/Hb/HctPolycythemia pattern.Can occur with chronic hypoxemia such as COPD.
High WBCLeukocytosis.Often suggests bacterial infection/inflammation.
Low WBCLeukopenia.May suggest viral infection or immune suppression.
Eosinophils highAllergy/asthma pattern.Can support asthma/allergic trigger suspicion.

Electrolytes and Acid-Base Clues

Electrolytes affect muscle function, cardiac rhythm, fluid status, acid-base balance, and respiratory function. Potassium affects muscle and ECG patterns. Sodium reflects fluid balance. Chloride and bicarbonate are connected to acid-base interpretation.

Renal Function and Fluid Status

The kidneys influence fluid balance, acid-base status, electrolyte levels, and urine output. Creatinine and BUN help identify kidney dysfunction or altered perfusion. Low urine output is important because fluid can back up and worsen shortness of breath.

Creatinine
High values suggest kidney impairment.
BUN
Can increase with renal issues, dehydration, CHF, or MI.
Urine output
Normal adult minimum is often taught around 40 mL/hr.

Sputum Analysis

Sputum is evaluated for amount, consistency, color, and lab testing. Color can suggest a possible cause, but it is not definitive. Gram stain, culture, sensitivity, and acid-fast stain help confirm organisms and guide therapy.

Sputum FindingPossible Association
Pink and frothyPulmonary edema.
White/gray and mucoidChronic bronchitis.
Yellow and tenaciousGram positive bacterial pattern / WBC-rich sputum.
Green and tenaciousStagnant sputum / gram negative bacterial pattern.
Brown/darkOld blood / anaerobic lung infection.
Red-streakedHemoptysis, TB, tumor, or bleeding source.

Coagulation Studies

Coagulation studies evaluate clotting and bleeding risk. They are especially important when a patient is on anticoagulant therapy or needs an invasive procedure such as bronchoscopy biopsy or arterial puncture.

Platelets
Needed for clot formation.
PT
Often monitored with warfarin therapy.
APTT
Often monitored with heparin therapy.
RT safety
Hold pressure longer after arterial puncture when bleeding risk is high.

Cardiac Labs: BNP and Troponin

Cardiac labs help distinguish cardiac and pulmonary causes of shortness of breath. BNP rises with heart failure. Troponin rises with myocardial injury. RTs use these clues to recommend appropriate respiratory and cardiopulmonary support.

LabBeginner InterpretationPossible RT Recommendation
BNP below 100 pg/mLHeart failure less likely.Consider pulmonary causes such as bronchospasm when assessment fits.
BNP elevatedCHF/worsening heart failure more likely.Consider diuretics, positive pressure support, and provider notification per protocol.
Troponin elevatedMyocardial injury concern.Oxygen if indicated, aspirin/morphine/nitro per protocols, urgent provider escalation.

Skin Testing and Special Tests

Skin and special tests provide targeted diagnostic information. Mantoux testing screens for TB exposure and must be read 48-72 hours after placement. Allergy testing helps identify asthma triggers. Sweat chloride testing helps diagnose cystic fibrosis.

Mantoux
Read at 48-72 hours. Positive result prompts chest x-ray and sputum testing.
Allergy testing
Scratch/prick or intradermal methods; monitor for reactions.
Sweat chloride
High chloride supports cystic fibrosis diagnosis.

FAQ

Common Lab Interpretation Questions

Can one lab diagnose the whole patient?

No. Labs are clues that must be interpreted with symptoms, history, physical assessment, imaging, and trends.

What is the difference between Gram stain, culture, and sensitivity?

Gram stain is rapid and classifies bacteria broadly. Culture identifies the organism. Sensitivity identifies which antibiotics are likely to work.

Why does anemia matter to an RT?

Anemia lowers oxygen carrying capacity, so a patient may feel short of breath even when oxygen pressure or SpO2 looks acceptable.

Why do cardiac labs matter in respiratory care?

CHF and myocardial injury can cause or worsen shortness of breath. BNP and troponin help distinguish cardiac from pulmonary causes.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Clinical labs help RTs identify oxygen-carrying problems, infection patterns, electrolyte and acid-base clues, renal or fluid concerns, sputum causes, bleeding risk, cardiac contributions, TB exposure, allergy triggers, cystic fibrosis testing, and microbiology categories.

Must know:
Low Hb can worsen dyspnea by reducing oxygen carrying capacity.
Infection pearl:
WBC with neutrophils and bands supports bacterial pattern.
Sputum pearl:
Color suggests possibilities but testing confirms direction.
Safety pearl:
Coagulation studies matter before invasive procedures and ABGs.
