ABG Interpretation

Arterial blood gases help RTs evaluate acid-base balance, ventilation, oxygenation, compensation, and clinical severity. This lesson teaches a structured ABG pathway with respiratory/metabolic classification, compensation, acute versus chronic respiratory analysis, oxygenation severity, and hypoventilation correction.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to identify acidemia or alkalemia, determine whether the primary disorder is respiratory or metabolic, evaluate compensation, classify acute versus chronic respiratory patterns, classify oxygenation severity, and recognize possible mixed disorders.

The ABG Interpretation Framework

ABG interpretation becomes easier when you use the same order every time. First identify the pH. Then determine whether PaCO₂ or HCO₃⁻ explains the pH. Then evaluate compensation. Finally, interpret oxygenation and decide what the patient needs.

Respiratory or Metabolic Primary Cause

The ROME method helps: Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal. In respiratory disorders, pH and PaCO₂ move in opposite directions. In metabolic disorders, pH and HCO₃⁻ move in the same direction.

pH 7.34, PaCO₂ 60, HCO₃⁻ 32, PaO₂ 58

Primary Cause Check

For this ABG, pH is low and PaCO₂ is high. Is the primary cause respiratory or metabolic?

Technical Classification: Uncompensated, Partially Compensated, or Fully Compensated

The technical classification describes whether the body has corrected the pH. If pH is abnormal and the opposite system is normal, the disorder is uncompensated. If pH is abnormal and the opposite system is abnormal, it is partially compensated. If pH is normal but PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ are abnormal, it is fully compensated.

Is Compensation Appropriate?

Compensation should move in a predictable direction. In respiratory acidosis, PaCO₂ is high and the kidneys retain HCO₃⁻ to buffer acidemia. If the HCO₃⁻ change does not match the expected acute or chronic compensation, consider a mixed disorder.

Compensation Check

For pH 7.34, PaCO₂ 60, HCO₃⁻ 32, is compensation present?

Functional Classification: Acute or Chronic Respiratory Acidosis

For respiratory disorders, acute and chronic compensation differ. Acute compensation has smaller HCO₃⁻ changes. Chronic compensation has larger kidney-driven HCO₃⁻ changes. Compare the expected change with the actual ABG.

Example: PaCO₂ is 60, which is 20 above 40. That is two 10-mmHg changes. Chronic respiratory acidosis predicts HCO₃⁻ increase of 2 × 4 = 8, so expected HCO₃⁻ is about 24 + 8 = 32.

Oxygenation Classification

After acid-base classification, evaluate PaO₂. PaO₂ tells whether oxygen pressure in arterial blood is normal or low. Many beginner assignments classify hypoxemia as mild, moderate, or severe.

Hypoventilation Correction Method

If PaCO₂ is elevated, some hypoxemia may be due to hypoventilation. A quick beginner estimate corrects PaO₂ by adding 1.25 mmHg for each 1 mmHg PaCO₂ above 40. If corrected PaO₂ remains below 90, the patient likely also needs more oxygen, not just more ventilation.

Correct the PaO₂

For PaCO₂ 60 and PaO₂ 58, calculate corrected PaO₂. Round to the nearest whole number.

Hint: (60 − 40) × 1.25 = 25. Then 58 + 25 = corrected PaO₂.

Mixed Disorders: When the Numbers Do Not Match

A mixed disorder may be present when compensation is not appropriate, the pH is more abnormal than expected, both PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ move in acid-producing or alkali-producing directions, or the clinical situation does not fit a single disorder.

FAQ

Common ABG Questions

Why do students get confused with ABGs?

Most students try to interpret every number at once. Use the same order every time: pH, cause, compensation, oxygenation, clinical action.

What does PaCO₂ tell me?

PaCO₂ reflects ventilation. High PaCO₂ means hypoventilation or CO₂ retention. Low PaCO₂ means hyperventilation or CO₂ blowing off.

How do I know if compensation is full?

If the pH is back in the normal range while PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ are both abnormal, the disorder is fully compensated.

Why correct PaO₂ for hypoventilation?

If PaCO₂ is high, the low PaO₂ may partly be due to hypoventilation. Correcting PaO₂ estimates whether oxygenation would normalize if PaCO₂ were normal.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

ABG interpretation is a sequence. Check pH, identify respiratory or metabolic cause, evaluate compensation, decide acute versus chronic when respiratory, classify oxygenation, correct PaO₂ for hypoventilation when appropriate, and watch for mixed disorders.

Must know:
ROME = Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal.
Ventilation pearl:
PaCO₂ is the ventilation value.
Compensation pearl:
pH determines uncompensated, partial, or full compensation.
Oxygenation pearl:
PaO₂ 58 = moderate hypoxemia.
