Competency Priorities

What You Must Be Able to Apply

Account for age-specific anatomy
Predict why infants obstruct, desaturate, fatigue, and develop respiratory failure faster than adults.
Assess in the right order
Begin with appearance, work of breathing, and circulation before adding detailed measurements.
Interpret trends
Use SpO₂, ETCO₂, blood gases, vital capacity, peak flow, and imaging together.
Recognize decompensation
Identify altered mental status, fatigue, poor air movement, bradycardia, and rising PaCO₂ as late warning signs.
Why Children Deteriorate Faster

Pediatric Anatomy Creates Less Margin for Error

FeatureClinical consequence
Smaller airway radiusSmall amounts of edema, mucus, or foreign material greatly increase resistance.
Larger tongue and relatively small mandibleUpper-airway obstruction occurs more easily, especially with decreased tone.
More compliant chest wallRetractions are prominent and the chest wall may collapse inward during increased effort.
Fewer collateral ventilation pathways in young childrenAtelectasis develops more easily when airways obstruct.
Higher oxygen consumptionDesaturation occurs rapidly during apnea or ineffective ventilation.
Less respiratory reserveFatigue may progress quickly from tachypnea to hypoventilation and bradycardia.

Match Anatomy to Clinical Risk

Upper-airway obstruction worsens when tone decreases

First Look

Appearance, Work of Breathing, and Circulation Reveal Severity Quickly

Appearance
Tone, interactiveness, consolability, gaze, speech or cry, and response to caregivers.
Work of breathing
Position, retractions, nasal flaring, grunting, head bobbing, stridor, wheeze, and air movement.
Circulation to skin
Color, pallor, mottling, cyanosis, capillary refill, temperature, and pulse quality.

Then obtain age-appropriate vital signs, SpO₂, blood pressure, temperature, focused history, lung sounds, and neurologic status. A child who becomes quiet after prolonged distress may be improving—or may be tiring.

Organize the Pediatric Assessment

Compensated Versus Decompensated

Respiratory Distress Can Progress to Respiratory Failure

StageTypical findings
Early distressTachypnea, retractions, nasal flaring, agitation, tachycardia, and preserved mental status.
Compensated failureIncreasing effort, persistent hypoxemia, reduced speech or feeding, anxiety, and rising fatigue.
Decompensated failureDecreasing respiratory effort, poor air movement, altered mental status, cyanosis, bradycardia, hypotension, or apnea.
High-risk pattern: A child with less wheezing but worsening air movement, fatigue, and rising PaCO₂ is not improving.

Continuous Trends

Each Monitor Answers a Different Question

MonitorWhat it helps assessKey limitation
Pulse oximetryArterial oxyhemoglobin saturation trend.Does not measure ventilation and may lag during oxygen therapy.

Ventilation and Metabolism

Blood Gases Clarify the Type and Severity of Failure

PatternInterpretation
Low PaO₂ with normal or low PaCO₂Hypoxemic failure with preserved or increased ventilation.
High PaCO₂ with acidemiaHypercapnic ventilatory failure.
Low HCO₃⁻ with low pHMetabolic acidosis, often from poor perfusion, sepsis, or renal/metabolic disease.
Rising lactateMay indicate inadequate oxygen delivery or impaired metabolism.

Interpret the sample type, oxygen device, support settings, temperature, and clinical trend. Venous gases may help evaluate pH and CO₂ trend, but do not substitute for arterial oxygenation assessment.

Diagnostic Imaging and Procedures

Use Imaging to Answer a Specific Clinical Question

Chest radiograph
Evaluates lung volume, focal or diffuse opacity, air leak, effusion, foreign body clues, and device position.
Lung ultrasound
Useful for pleural fluid, pneumothorax, consolidation, diaphragmatic motion, and bedside reassessment.
CT or MRI
Provides detailed anatomy when the benefit outweighs radiation, transport, sedation, or time concerns.
Bronchoscopy
Directly evaluates airway anatomy, obstruction, bleeding, secretions, malacia, and foreign body.

External images are not required for this foundational lesson. Disease-specific radiographs and bronchoscopy findings will be embedded where image recognition is essential.

Key Takeaways

Children have less reserve
Small airways, compliant chest walls, and high oxygen consumption speed deterioration.
Assessment begins before equipment
Appearance, breathing, and circulation reveal severity quickly.
SpO₂ does not equal ventilation
Use ETCO₂, blood gases, and clinical effort to detect hypercapnia and fatigue.
Diagnostics must answer a question
Choose blood gases, PFTs, imaging, or bronchoscopy based on the suspected mechanism.
