Information Gathering

Maternal History Predicts the Infant’s First Problems

Before delivery, the respiratory therapist should review gestational age, estimated fetal weight, prenatal care, maternal disorders, medications, infection status, membrane rupture, amniotic fluid, fetal monitoring, and anticipated mode of delivery.

History elementWhy it matters to the neonatal team
Gestational agePredicts airway size, surfactant maturity, temperature risk, respiratory drive, and likely support needs.
Membrane ruptureProlonged rupture increases infection risk; early prolonged rupture may contribute to oligohydramnios and pulmonary hypoplasia.
Amniotic fluidOligohydramnios suggests impaired fluid production or leakage; polyhydramnios may accompany impaired fetal swallowing or maternal diabetes.
Maternal medicationsOpioids, anesthetics, magnesium, and other agents may alter neonatal respiratory effort, tone, or blood pressure.
Fetal presentation and delivery planBreech, shoulder dystocia, emergency cesarean delivery, or assisted delivery may increase resuscitation risk.
NPS connection: The updated outline specifically includes maternal history, amniotic fluid index, maternal medication, biophysical profile, APGAR score, and fetal lung maturity indices.
Risk Recognition

Different Maternal Problems Create Different Neonatal Risks

Maternal or pregnancy conditionImportant fetal/neonatal concerns
DiabetesMacrosomia, birth trauma, congenital anomalies, hypoglycemia, delayed surfactant maturation, and respiratory distress.
Hypertension or preeclampsiaPlacental insufficiency, fetal growth restriction, abruption, prematurity, and fetal compromise.
Placental abruptionAcute fetal hypoxia, blood loss, shock, and emergency delivery.
Placenta previaMaternal hemorrhage and potential preterm or cesarean delivery.
Infection or chorioamnionitisPrematurity, neonatal sepsis, pneumonia, temperature instability, and respiratory failure.
Substance exposure or sedating medicationRespiratory depression, altered tone, withdrawal, growth restriction, or congenital effects depending on exposure.
Post-term pregnancyPlacental insufficiency, meconium passage, macrosomia, and aspiration risk.

Fetal Wellbeing

Antenatal Testing Looks for Placental Failure and Fetal Stress

Nonstress test
Evaluates fetal heart-rate accelerations associated with movement.
Biophysical profile
Combines fetal movement, tone, breathing movement, amniotic fluid, and heart-rate testing.
Amniotic fluid index
Estimates amniotic fluid volume and helps identify oligohydramnios or polyhydramnios.

Fetal heart-rate decelerations provide clues to the mechanism of compromise:

PatternTypical relationshipClinical meaning
Early decelerationMirrors the contractionUsually caused by fetal head compression.
Variable decelerationAbrupt and variable timingSuggests umbilical cord compression.
Late decelerationBegins after contraction starts and recovers after it endsSuggests uteroplacental insufficiency and fetal hypoxemia.

Team Preparation

High-Risk Delivery Preparation Happens Before the Infant Arrives

Preparation should be matched to the predicted risk. At least one qualified person must be assigned to the newborn and able to begin ventilation. Higher-risk births require additional personnel skilled in advanced airway management, chest compressions, vascular access, medications, and stabilization.

Environment
Warm room, preheated radiant warmer, warm linens, hat, thermal wrap or mattress when appropriate.
Ventilation
Flow-inflating or self-inflating device, T-piece when used, masks, blender, oxygen source, suction, and pulse oximetry.
Airway
Appropriate laryngoscope blades, ETT sizes, securement, CO₂ detector, and supraglottic airway when appropriate.
Escalation
Medication access, chest-compression space, transport incubator, and communication with NICU or referral center.

Rapid Newborn Description

APGAR Describes Adaptation; It Does Not Replace Resuscitation Decisions

APGAR is assigned at 1 and 5 minutes and may be repeated when low. Resuscitation should begin based on breathing and heart rate without waiting for the score.

First Minutes and Hours

Respiratory Distress Is a Pattern, Not a Single Sign

The newborn assessment includes temperature, color, level of consciousness, tone, posture, respiratory rate and pattern, chest movement, breath sounds, heart rate, perfusion, oxygen saturation, and evidence of congenital abnormalities.

Work of breathing
Grunting, nasal flaring, retractions, head bobbing, seesaw movement, or tachypnea.
Gas exchange
Central cyanosis, low saturation, apnea, altered mental status, or worsening blood gas values.
Systemic adaptation
Temperature instability, poor perfusion, weak feeding, hypotonia, or abnormal glucose may accompany serious illness.
Thermoregulation matters: Cold stress increases metabolic demand and oxygen consumption and can worsen hypoglycemia, acidosis, and respiratory distress.

Key Takeaways

History predicts risk
Maternal disease, medications, infection, membranes, fluid, and gestation guide preparation.
Fetal patterns have mechanisms
Late decelerations indicate placental insufficiency; variable decelerations suggest cord compression.
Prepare before birth
The team, warmer, ventilation, airway, monitoring, and transport plan should match the anticipated risk.
Assess the whole newborn
APGAR is descriptive; respiratory effort, heart rate, color, temperature, tone, and perfusion guide care.
