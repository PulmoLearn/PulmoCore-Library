C1: Perform a focused neonatal physical and cardiopulmonary assessment.

Begin with the baby, not the monitor

A neonatal assessment begins with observation. Before deciding whether a monitor value is good or bad, look at the infant. A newborn can tell you a great deal through posture, tone, color, breathing pattern, chest movement, and response to handling. These findings create the clinical picture that gives meaning to the numbers on the bedside monitor.

A focused assessment does not mean looking at only the lungs. Respiratory problems can affect circulation, neurologic status, temperature, and metabolism. In the same way, problems outside the lungs—such as poor perfusion or abnormal glucose—can make breathing and oxygenation worse. The respiratory therapist therefore gathers information from several systems and asks whether the findings fit together.

AppearanceGestational age, activity, posture, tone, consolability, and response to handling.
BreathingRate, rhythm, apnea, chest movement, retractions, grunting, flaring, breath sounds, and synchrony.
Gas exchangeCentral color, SpO₂ trend, oxygen requirement, blood gas values, and preductal-postductal relationship.
CirculationHeart rate, pulses, capillary refill, blood pressure, skin temperature, urine output, lactate, and NIRS trends when used.

Work of breathing is a pattern

Retractions, nasal flaring, grunting, and abnormal chest movement are clues that the newborn is spending extra energy to breathe. One sign by itself may be mild or temporary. Several findings occurring together, especially when oxygen need is rising or chest movement is becoming less effective, are more concerning.

Do not forget the equipment

A newborn may appear to deteriorate because the patient is changing, because the support device is not functioning correctly, or because the monitor is giving poor information. Check airway position, interface fit, circuit patency, humidification, alarms, sensor placement, and waveform quality as part of the bedside assessment.

Clinical reasoning: If a monitor suddenly changes but the infant looks unchanged, first ask whether the signal is trustworthy. If the infant looks worse too, treat the change as possible true deterioration while you verify the cause.

C2: Evaluate neonatal oxygenation and ventilation using pulse oximetry, blood gases, end-tidal carbon dioxide, and transcutaneous monitoring.

Oxygenation and ventilation are related—but they are not the same thing

Oxygenation describes how well oxygen moves into the blood and is carried to the body. Ventilation describes how effectively carbon dioxide is removed through breathing. A newborn can have an oxygenation problem, a ventilation problem, or both. That is why no single monitor answers every respiratory question.

The safest habit is to ask, “What does this device actually measure?” Pulse oximetry estimates hemoglobin saturation. ETCO₂ estimates exhaled carbon dioxide at the end of exhalation. Transcutaneous monitors estimate oxygen or carbon dioxide through the skin. Blood gases directly provide acid-base and gas-exchange information, but the usefulness of the value depends on the sample source.

ToolBest question it helps answerImportant limitation
Pulse oximetryWhat is the estimated arterial oxyhemoglobin saturation trend?It does not directly measure PaO₂, PaCO₂, ventilation, oxygen content, or tissue perfusion.
ETCO₂Is exhaled CO₂ present, and how is the ventilation/perfusion trend changing?Leaks, small tidal volumes, dead space, low pulmonary blood flow, and severe lung disease may widen the PaCO₂–ETCO₂ difference.
Transcutaneous CO₂/O₂How is oxygen or carbon dioxide trending continuously over time?Accuracy depends on calibration, site, perfusion, temperature, edema, and skin condition.
Blood gasWhat is the current acid-base, ventilation, and—when arterial—oxygenation status?Interpretation depends on sample source and conditions at the time of collection.

Preductal and postductal saturation

Preductal SpO₂ is measured on the right hand or wrist. Postductal SpO₂ is measured on a foot. Comparing the two can help reveal a difference in oxygenation across the ductal circulation. A meaningful difference may suggest ductal shunting, but the numbers still have to be interpreted with the infant’s overall clinical condition.

Always judge signal quality

Motion, poor perfusion, edema, ambient light, poor sensor contact, venous pulsation, and dyshemoglobins can mislead pulse oximetry. A number without a reliable signal should not be treated as if it were unquestionably true.

Beginner checkpoint: SpO₂ is mainly an oxygenation monitor. ETCO₂ and transcutaneous CO₂ help follow ventilation. Blood gases provide a more direct snapshot, but you must know where the sample came from.

C3: Select and troubleshoot transcutaneous and end-tidal carbon dioxide monitoring systems.

Troubleshooting starts with one question: does the infant match the monitor?

A monitoring system is useful only when its signal is believable. If a number changes suddenly, first assess the patient. Then inspect the signal or waveform, check the connection or sensor, consider the known limitations of the device, and confirm the finding with another source when needed. This approach prevents unnecessary treatment based on artifact.

ETCO₂: follow the breath-by-breath signal

ETCO₂ depends on exhaled gas reaching the sensor or sampling line. In a very small infant, a leak around the airway, tiny tidal volumes, added dead space, low pulmonary blood flow, or severe lung disease can make the exhaled value look much lower than the arterial PaCO₂. That means the waveform and the patient’s condition matter as much as the number.

  1. Assess the infant: Is the baby ventilating? Is the airway patent?
  2. Inspect the waveform: Is there a recognizable exhaled CO₂ pattern?
  3. Check the system: Look for disconnection, obstruction, or a large leak.
  4. Consider physiology: Small tidal volume, dead space, low pulmonary blood flow, and severe lung disease can alter the reading.
  5. Confirm when needed: Correlate a questionable trend with examination and blood-gas data.

Transcutaneous monitoring: the skin site matters

Transcutaneous monitoring estimates gas values through the skin and is especially useful for trends. The sensor must be calibrated and must have proper contact with an appropriate site. Perfusion, skin temperature, edema, skin thickness, and the condition of the site can all affect the value.

  1. Confirm calibration and sensor condition.
  2. Verify proper contact and site placement.
  3. Consider perfusion, temperature, edema, skin thickness, and site condition.
  4. Reposition or change the heated-sensor site according to unit practice.
  5. Correlate an unexplained value with a blood gas before making a major treatment change.
Safety point: A monitor and a patient that disagree require investigation. Do not automatically treat the number before checking whether the signal, sensor, connection, or physiology can explain the mismatch.

C4: Obtain or assist with blood gas sampling and interpret the resulting acid-base and gas-exchange information.

Choose the sample that answers the clinical question

A blood gas is not simply “a blood gas.” The source matters because arterial, capillary, venous, and cord samples represent different parts of the circulation. Before collecting a specimen, decide what you need to know. If accurate arterial oxygenation is the question, an arterial sample is the best direct choice. If the goal is a less invasive trend in pH and carbon dioxide in a stable infant, a capillary sample may be useful.

SampleWhat it is useful forWhat not to assume
ArterialDirect assessment of pH, PaCO₂, PaO₂, and arterial oxygenation.The value still must be linked to current FiO₂ and respiratory support.
CapillaryUseful for pH and PCO₂ trends in many stable infants.Capillary PO₂ is not a reliable substitute for arterial oxygenation.
VenousSystemic acid-base and metabolic assessment.Venous PO₂ does not represent arterial oxygenation; venous PCO₂ is usually higher than arterial.
Umbilical arterial cordFetal condition and placental gas exchange near delivery.It is not the same as a later arterial sample from the newborn.
Umbilical arterial catheterRepeated arterial sampling and continuous blood-pressure monitoring in selected neonates.Line position and sample validity still require attention.

Protect the validity of the sample

A technically measured result can still be misleading if the wrong sample source was assumed, if the respiratory-support conditions were not documented, or if there was a collection problem. Verify the infant, sample type, and site or line. Document oxygen concentration and respiratory support. Use the unit-approved collection and handling process. If the result does not fit the baby, reassess both the patient and the sample.

Interpret in the same order every time

1. pH
Acidemia or alkalemia?
2. PCO₂
Respiratory contribution?
3. HCO₃⁻ / base deficit
Metabolic contribution?
4. Oxygenation
Is oxygenation adequate?
5. Trend
What changed recently?
Worked example from the lesson: pH 7.24, PaCO₂ 62 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 26 mEq/L, and PaO₂ 58 mmHg on FiO₂ 0.35 represents acute respiratory acidosis with hypoxemia. The low pH shows acidemia, the elevated PaCO₂ identifies the respiratory cause, the near-normal bicarbonate supports an acute pattern, and the PaO₂ shows impaired oxygenation.

C5: Interpret neonatal chest imaging, including correct airway, gastric tube, and vascular catheter position.

Read the image in a repeatable order

A chest radiograph contains a large amount of information, so beginners often focus on the first abnormality they notice. A safer approach is systematic. First ask whether the image itself is good enough to interpret. Check patient identity, projection, rotation, exposure, inspiration, and body position. Only then move through the anatomy and the devices.

After quality, inspect the airway and mediastinum, lungs and pleural spaces, diaphragm, abdomen, bones and soft tissues, and every line or tube. A newborn can deteriorate because of disease, but also because an endotracheal tube moved, an air leak developed, or a vascular catheter is malpositioned.

Recognize the major lung patterns taught in this lesson

PatternWhat the image looks likeWhat to notice first
RDSLow-volume, diffuse granular lungs with air bronchograms.The overall lung volume and diffuse nature of the pattern.
Meconium aspirationHyperinflation with coarse, patchy opacities.The uneven, patchy pattern together with increased lung volume.
Tension pneumothoraxUnilateral hyperlucency, absent markings, and mediastinal shift.One side becomes unusually dark with loss of normal lung markings.

Every image is also a line-and-tube check

DeviceExpected positionWhy position matters
Endotracheal tubeMid-trachea with the head neutral, safely above the carina.A tube that is too deep may ventilate one lung more than the other; a tube that is too high may be unstable.
OG/NG tubeTip and side holes below the diaphragm within the stomach.The tube must actually reach the stomach for its intended use.
Umbilical arterial catheterAccepted high descending thoracic aortic position or accepted low abdominal position according to policy.Position is part of safe arterial monitoring and sampling.
Umbilical venous catheterNear the IVC–right atrial junction and outside the cardiac silhouette.A catheter extending into the heart is not the desired position.

Connect imaging back to the bedside

Imaging should confirm or challenge what you found at the bedside. If a ventilated infant suddenly has asymmetric breath sounds, falling SpO₂, lower ETCO₂, and lower exhaled volume, think about airway displacement, obstruction, or an air leak before simply increasing ventilator support. The chest image is one part of that investigation.

Systematic image sequence: Quality → airway/mediastinum → lungs/pleura → diaphragm/abdomen → bones/soft tissues → every line and tube.
