C1: Select initial pediatric ventilator settings according to patient size, physiology, and disease process.

There is no single “pediatric ventilator setup”

Initial ventilator settings should be chosen from the child’s size, lung mechanics, disease pattern, blood gas, respiratory drive, and hemodynamic condition. The ventilator is a tool for solving a physiologic problem. A useful first question is: Is the dominant problem oxygenation, ventilation, airway protection, excessive work of breathing, or a combination?

After the child is connected, reassess immediately. The initial setup is only a starting strategy; the delivered tidal volume, pressures, gas exchange, chest movement, waveforms, and perfusion determine whether the plan is actually working.

Match the ventilator strategy to the mechanics

Disease patternVentilator reasoningMajor risk to watch
Low-compliance diffuse diseaseUse lung-protective tidal volume, adequate PEEP, pressure limitation, and assess recruitmentExcessive stretch, pressure, overdistention, or inadequate recruitment
Severe obstructionUse lower rate, shorter inspiratory time, and enough expiratory time for lung emptyingBreath stacking, gas trapping, and auto-PEEP
Neuromuscular weaknessSupport ventilation and cough while avoiding unnecessary oxygen and excessive sedationWeak drive, secretion retention, and prolonged dependence
Hemodynamic instabilityUse positive pressure cautiously and reassess perfusion with each major changeReduced venous return and worsening circulation

Why more breaths can worsen severe obstruction

In obstructive disease, exhalation is slow. If the next breath begins before the previous breath has finished leaving the lung, gas remains trapped. Repeated incomplete exhalation raises end-expiratory lung volume and can create intrinsic PEEP, also called auto-PEEP.

A lower rate and shorter inspiratory time can create more expiratory time. This is why “more breaths” is not always the solution to rising CO₂ in a child with severe obstruction. Controlled hypercapnia may sometimes be accepted when it helps avoid excessive ventilator injury and the clinical context permits it.

Positive pressure also affects circulation

Increasing intrathoracic pressure can reduce venous return to the heart. A ventilator change that improves oxygenation can therefore still be harmful if blood pressure and perfusion worsen. Pair meaningful pressure changes with reassessment of heart rate, blood pressure, perfusion, and the child’s overall response.

Clinical reasoning: Ventilator settings should match the disease. A low-compliance lung may need recruitment; an obstructed lung may need time to empty. The same adjustment can help one child and harm another.

C2: Select and differentiate invasive ventilation modes.

A mode name matters less than knowing what is controlled and what can change

Mechanical ventilation modes differ in which variables the ventilator controls and which variables are allowed to change with patient mechanics or effort. Understanding this relationship makes it easier to predict what happens when compliance, resistance, leak, or respiratory drive changes.

ModeWhat is mainly set?What changes with patient mechanics/effort?
Pressure controlInspiratory pressure and inspiratory timeDelivered tidal volume varies with compliance, resistance, effort, and leak
Volume controlTidal volume and flow patternAirway pressure changes as mechanics change
PRVCA tidal-volume target with pressure limitsPressure adjusts breath to breath toward the volume target
SIMV + pressure supportMandatory breaths plus support for spontaneous breathsSpontaneous contribution depends on drive and selected support
Pressure supportLevel of inspiratory assistanceRate, inspiratory time, and tidal volume depend largely on patient effort and cycling

Pressure control: fixed pressure, variable volume

If compliance worsens while inspiratory pressure remains unchanged, the same pressure may deliver less tidal volume. If compliance improves, the same pressure may deliver more tidal volume. This is why exhaled VT must be monitored continuously rather than assumed from the set pressure.

Volume control: fixed volume target, variable pressure

When tidal volume is set, the pressure required to deliver that volume changes as compliance and resistance change. Rising airway pressure may therefore be the first clue that mechanics have worsened or airway resistance has increased.

PRVC responds to the child’s changing mechanics

Pressure-regulated volume control automatically adjusts pressure breath to breath toward a target tidal volume within the programmed limits. It still requires careful monitoring of the pressure being used and the volume actually delivered.

Clinical reasoning: Ask “What is controlled, and what is allowed to vary?” before deciding how a mode will respond to worsening compliance or resistance.

C3: Evaluate oxygenation, ventilation, pressures, volumes, pulmonary mechanics, waveforms, and patient-ventilator interaction.

Separate oxygenation from ventilation before changing a setting

Ventilator management becomes much easier when oxygenation and ventilation are treated as related but different goals. Oxygenation is influenced strongly by inspired oxygen, lung recruitment, PEEP, and mean airway pressure. Carbon-dioxide clearance depends strongly on effective minute ventilation, which is influenced by rate and delivered tidal volume.

OxygenationThink FiO₂, PEEP, recruitment, mean airway pressure, lung volume, and perfusion.
VentilationThink effective rate, delivered tidal volume, minute ventilation, dead space, airway resistance, and patient effort.

Peak pressure and plateau pressure answer different questions

Peak airway pressure reflects the total pressure needed during active gas flow. It is affected by both airway resistance and respiratory-system compliance. Plateau pressure is measured during a no-flow period and more closely approximates alveolar pressure.

Driving Pressure = Plateau Pressure − PEEP

Driving pressure helps frame the pressure used to move tidal volume above the baseline PEEP. In the lesson, plateau and driving pressure are interpreted together with delivered tidal volume and chest-wall mechanics rather than treated as isolated numbers.

Ventilator data must be interpreted with the child

SpO₂, ETCO₂ or blood gas, chest movement, work of breathing, perfusion, exhaled VT, pressures, and trends should tell a coherent story. If the numbers and the child do not match, investigate before making a major change.

Clinical reasoning: If the child is fighting the ventilator, do not assume the solution is more sedation. First ask whether the ventilator is actually responding to the child’s effort correctly.

C4: Apply lung-protective ventilation and adjunctive strategies for pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome.

PARDS is severe acute lung injury with impaired oxygenation

Pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome develops acutely after a clinical insult and includes new imaging abnormalities, pulmonary edema not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload, and impaired oxygenation. The lungs are often poorly compliant and vulnerable to additional injury from excessive tidal volume and pressure.

Lung protection is a balance

Using too little support can leave lung units collapsed and worsen oxygenation. Using too much volume or pressure can overdistend already vulnerable lung regions. The goal is adequate recruitment and gas exchange with the least harmful pressure and volume burden.

StrategyReasoning
Smaller tidal volumes with poor complianceReduces excessive alveolar stretch
Pressure limitationLimits excessive lung stress while maintaining needed support
PEEP optimizationBalances recruitment against overdistention and hemodynamic compromise
Permissive hypercapnia when appropriateMay reduce pressure/volume injury when pH and contraindications are considered
Avoid unnecessary positive fluid balance after stabilizationLimits avoidable pulmonary fluid burden while preserving organ perfusion

PEEP can help and harm

Adequate PEEP can recruit unstable lung units and improve oxygenation. Excessive PEEP can overdistend lung and reduce venous return. This is why the response must include both oxygenation and hemodynamic assessment.

Adjuncts are rescue strategies, not automatic next steps

Prone positioningMay be considered when severe disease remains refractory.
Brief neuromuscular blockadeMay be considered for severe dyssynchrony when clinically appropriate.
High-frequency ventilationMay be used as an advanced strategy for refractory gas-exchange failure.
ECMOA rescue option for potentially reversible refractory cardiorespiratory failure.
Clinical reasoning: PARDS management is not about normalizing every number. It is about maintaining acceptable gas exchange while limiting the amount of additional injury caused by the ventilator.

C5: Optimize patient-ventilator interaction and troubleshoot deterioration.

When a ventilated child deteriorates, do not start by turning up the ventilator

Sudden deterioration can result from the patient, the airway, the circuit, the ventilator, or the disease itself. Increasing pressure before identifying the cause can worsen an obstruction, air leak, or overdistention problem.

Patient → Airway → Circuit → Ventilator → Disease

Rising peak pressure plus falling tidal volume is a clue, not a diagnosis

This pattern should trigger assessment for airway obstruction or displacement, increased circuit resistance, bronchospasm, worsening compliance, or air leak. The correct intervention depends on the cause.

Repositioning can move the tube or reveal an acute lung problem

If a child suddenly desaturates after being turned and breath sounds become unilateral, immediately assess the airway and patient for ETT migration, mainstem position, or pneumothorax. Do not wait to “see if the ventilator catches up.”

Dyssynchrony should be solved at the cause when possible

Persistent distress can come from inadequate flow, poor timing, inappropriate trigger sensitivity, intrinsic PEEP, obstruction, pain, agitation, or worsening disease. Sedation may sometimes be needed, but it should not substitute for correcting a mechanical or timing problem that the ventilator is creating.

Reassessment closes the loop

After every significant change, reassess oxygenation, ventilation, pressures and volumes, waveform interaction, work of breathing, and perfusion. The ventilator change is not complete until the response is known.

Clinical reasoning: More inspiratory pressure is not a universal rescue. If the airway is obstructed, displaced, or the child has a pneumothorax, more pressure may make the situation worse.

C6: Assess readiness for liberation while reducing ventilator-associated harm.

Liberation begins when the disease begins to improve

Waiting until the child is already on very low settings to begin thinking about extubation can prolong exposure to invasive ventilation. As the underlying disease improves, the team should begin reducing unnecessary support and assessing whether the child can sustain breathing with less assistance.

Extubation readiness is a bundle

Disease improvementThe condition that caused respiratory failure is improving.
Stable gas exchangeOxygenation and ventilation remain acceptable on reduced support.
Spontaneous driveThe child reliably initiates breathing and sedation is manageable.
Strength / coughRespiratory-muscle performance and cough are adequate for the next level of support.
Secretions / airwaySecretions are manageable and airway patency/edema risk are acceptable.
Postextubation planOxygen, HFNC, CPAP, or bilevel support and re-escalation criteria are defined in advance.

Low ventilator settings do not equal readiness

A child may have modest settings and stable blood gases yet still be a poor extubation candidate because of heavy sedation, weak cough, copious secretions, airway edema risk, poor muscle performance, or a planned procedure requiring continued airway control.

Liberation reduces ventilator-associated harm

Avoidable harm can come from excessive tidal volume and pressure, repeated derecruitment, unnecessary sedation, fluid burden, and prolonged invasive ventilation. The safest strategy is not merely to avoid injury while ventilated, but also to remove unnecessary support when the child is ready.

Clinical reasoning: Extubation should be planned, not improvised. The child’s drive, gas exchange, cough, secretions, airway, sedation, and postextubation support all matter.
