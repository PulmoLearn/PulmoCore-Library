C1: Establish and maintain a patent pediatric airway.

Airway management begins before an airway device is inserted

A patent airway means air can move from the environment to the lungs without significant blockage. In children, obstruction can develop from anatomy, decreased muscle tone, secretions, blood, vomitus, swelling, foreign material, or poor positioning. The first task is therefore to determine whether air is moving effectively.

Assess chest movement, breath sounds, stridor, snoring, gurgling, secretions, voice or cry, work of breathing, and the visible movement of air. Airway noises are clues: snoring can suggest upper-airway soft-tissue obstruction, gurgling can suggest fluid or secretions, and stridor signals upper-airway narrowing but does not identify the cause by itself.

Positioning must match pediatric anatomy

Infants have a relatively large occiput. When an infant lies flat, the head may be pushed forward and the neck may flex, narrowing the airway. A small shoulder roll may improve alignment. The goal is a neutral, open airway—not extreme extension.

Suction only when there is something to remove

Suction is useful when secretions, blood, vomitus, or mucus are obstructing airflow. It should not be performed reflexively when the airway is already clear. Use an age-appropriate device and the least traumatic effective technique, then reassess air movement and oxygenation.

AdjunctBest conceptual use

Bag-mask ventilation must actually inflate the lungs

When one-person bag-mask ventilation is ineffective, two-person BVM can improve both airway position and mask seal. The goal is visible chest rise using only enough pressure and volume to produce effective ventilation. Poor chest movement should trigger reassessment of position, seal, obstruction, and adjunct need before pressure is simply increased.

Clinical reasoning: If BVM is not moving the chest, think “position, seal, obstruction, adjunct, two-person technique” before thinking “more pressure.”

C2: Select intubation equipment, assist with intubation, and verify placement.

Successful intubation begins with preparation

A safe pediatric intubation plan includes more than the tube itself. The team should prepare the primary ETT plus backup sizes, verify cuff integrity when applicable, have suction ready, prepare the laryngoscope and stylet, and ensure that BVM, carbon-dioxide detection, a rescue supraglottic airway, and tube securement are immediately available.

Preoxygenation, team roles, an attempt limit, a backup operator, and a failed-airway pathway should be considered before the first attempt. These steps help reduce repeated traumatic attempts and prevent loss of oxygenation while the team improvises.

ETT size estimates are starting points

Pediatric tube-size formulas are estimates. Prepare a smaller and larger option because actual fit must be assessed clinically. The therapist should consider ventilation, resistance, leak or cuff pressure when applicable, and how the tube performs once in place rather than assuming a calculated size is automatically correct.

Verification answers two questions: Is the tube in the trachea, and is it at the right depth?

Sustained exhaled carbon dioxide, when perfusion is present, strongly supports tracheal placement. But exhaled CO₂ does not prove that the tube is positioned at the correct depth. A tube can be inside the trachea and still be advanced too far into a main bronchus.

Immediate confirmationOngoing / depth confirmation
Sustained exhaled CO₂ when perfusion is presentExternal depth mark
Visible chest riseSecurement
Bilateral breath soundsVentilator pressure/volume behavior
Improving oxygenation and clinical responseImaging and repeat exam after movement

Asymmetry after intubation is a warning sign

If exhaled CO₂ is present but breath sounds are much louder on one side, reassess tube depth and possible mainstem position. Oxygenation may deteriorate and airway pressures may rise even though the tube remains within the tracheobronchial tree.

Clinical reasoning: “CO₂ present” means the tube is likely in the trachea when circulation is present. It does not mean the depth is automatically correct.

C3: Manage established artificial airways.

An artificial airway requires continuous management

An ETT or tracheostomy does not remain safe simply because it was positioned correctly earlier. Artificial airways can become displaced, obstructed, kinked, poorly humidified, or disconnected. Secretions and skin injury can also develop over time.

ETT management priorities

  • Maintain patency and adequate humidification.
  • Assess secretion burden and suction need.
  • Verify securement and document depth.
  • Provide skin and oral care.
  • Monitor cuff pressure when applicable.

Tracheostomy management adds stoma and emergency planning

  • Assess tube patency and securement.
  • Inspect the stoma and surrounding skin.
  • Maintain humidification and secretion clearance.
  • Know the child-specific emergency airway plan and required backup equipment.

Movement can change airway position

Reassess artificial airways after transport, repositioning, procedures, or unexplained clinical changes. Loss of a depth mark, sudden desaturation, asymmetric breath sounds, absent CO₂, low exhaled volume, or inability to ventilate can indicate displacement, obstruction, mainstem position, or device/circuit failure.

C4: Respond to acute obstruction, difficult/congenital airway, and unplanned displacement.

Stridor is a sign—not the diagnosis

Upper-airway narrowing can produce stridor, but the cause determines the safest response. Croup, epiglottitis or deep neck infection, foreign-body obstruction, anaphylaxis, and postextubation edema are not managed as one identical condition.

PatternKey cluesSafety priority
CroupBarking cough, hoarseness, inspiratory stridorKeep calm, support oxygenation, provide prescribed therapy, monitor fatigue
Epiglottitis / deep neck infectionDrooling, tripod posture, muffled voice, toxic appearanceAvoid agitation/repeated throat exam; prepare controlled expert airway
Foreign bodySudden choking, abrupt cough/cyanosis, focal or asymmetric breath soundsAge-appropriate choking response for complete obstruction; persistent obstruction may require bronchoscopy
AnaphylaxisRapid edema, wheeze, rash/urticaria, hypotension or GI symptomsTreat as emergency while supporting airway and oxygenation

Known difficult anatomy changes the plan before the crisis

A known difficult or congenital airway should trigger early expert involvement, review of prior airway history, and preparation of backup oxygenation and advanced-airway options. Repeated traumatic attempts can worsen swelling, bleeding, and loss of airway control.

The priority is preserving oxygenation while moving deliberately through the failed-airway plan, not proving that a particular intubation technique can eventually succeed.

Unplanned displacement is an airway emergency

When an ETT or tracheostomy is suspected to have moved, assess the child first, call for help, and restore oxygenation and ventilation by the safest available route. Then evaluate tube patency and position. Waiting for imaging before supporting the child can be dangerous when gas exchange is already failing.

Clinical reasoning: The correct response to stridor depends on the cause. The correct response to sudden artificial-airway displacement is immediate support plus airway reassessment—not waiting for a perfect diagnosis.

Be ready to distinguish croup, epiglottitis/deep neck infection, foreign body, and anaphylaxis and to explain how difficult-airway history or device displacement changes the response plan.
C5: Select and evaluate noninvasive support and recognize failure.

Choose NIV based on what the child needs help doing

Noninvasive support can improve lung volume, reduce work of breathing, and assist ventilation without an artificial airway. But CPAP, bilevel support, and HFNC do not provide the same kind of assistance.

SupportPrimary roleImportant limitation
CPAPContinuous distending pressure for recruitment/FRC or upper-airway patencyDoes not add the same inspiratory assistance as bilevel support
BilevelAdds inspiratory assistance for hypercapnia, weakness, severe obstruction, or mixed failureStill requires tolerance and enough spontaneous respiratory function to succeed
HFNCHeated humidified flow, dead-space washout, reduced work of breathingNot reliable inspiratory pressure support during progressive ventilatory failure

Evaluate more than the settings

NIV assessment includes interface fit and leak, synchrony, humidification, skin pressure, gastric distention, patient tolerance, oxygen saturation, work of breathing, mental status, CO₂ or blood gases, and perfusion. A technically functioning machine does not equal a successful therapy if the child is clinically worsening.

NIV failure is a trend

  • Rising PaCO₂
  • Worsening acidemia
  • Falling air movement or tidal volume
  • Fatigue or apnea
  • Persistent hypoxemia
  • Somnolence or deteriorating mental status
  • Shock or worsening perfusion
  • Aspiration risk or inability to tolerate the interface

Why “just turn up the NIV” can be dangerous

If the child is becoming somnolent, acidemic, hypercapnic, or apneic, increasing NIV indefinitely may delay needed airway protection and invasive ventilation. Escalation should occur while the team still has time to prepare rather than after the child arrests.

Clinical reasoning: CPAP supports lung volume. Bilevel adds inspiratory assistance. HFNC can reduce work and wash out dead space. None of these should be continued simply because they are “noninvasive” when the child’s physiology shows failure.

Be ready to choose CPAP, bilevel, or HFNC by physiologic need and to identify the clinical trend that means NIV is failing.
C6: Apply resuscitation principles when airway or ventilatory failure becomes an emergency.

Escalate before the child reaches arrest

A pediatric airway emergency is not defined only by cardiopulmonary arrest. Emergency help should be activated when the child can no longer maintain oxygenation, ventilation, airway protection, or perfusion. Early escalation creates time for coordinated ventilation and airway management before physiology collapses further.

Return to the fundamentals

Open and clear airway → provide effective BVM → verify visible chest rise → correct ineffective ventilation → advance airway if needed → reassess oxygenation, heart rate, perfusion, and chest movement

If BVM is not effective, reassess airway position, mask seal, obstruction, need for an adjunct, and whether a different ventilation device or two-person technique is needed. Do not allow ineffective bagging to continue without correction.

When should the airway be advanced?

A supraglottic or endotracheal airway may be needed when basic ventilation is inadequate, mask ventilation cannot be maintained, airway protection is required, or prolonged ventilatory support is expected. The exact device depends on the clinical situation and failed-airway plan.

If arrest develops, ventilation remains part of resuscitation

If cardiopulmonary arrest develops, transition immediately to the age-appropriate pediatric resuscitation sequence while maintaining effective ventilation and clear team communication. Airway management and circulation support must progress together.

Difficult airway and NIV failure are reasons to mobilize the team early

Waiting for arrest before asking for help is a major safety error. A known difficult airway, worsening hypercapnic failure, repeated apnea, or loss of airway protection should trigger preparation for escalation while the child can still be supported.

Clinical reasoning: The safest airway emergency is the one the team recognizes before arrest. Early activation, effective BVM, backup airway planning, and reassessment create time to rescue the child deliberately.
