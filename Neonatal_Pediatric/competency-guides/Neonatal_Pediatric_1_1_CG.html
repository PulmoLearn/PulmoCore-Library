C1: Relate gestational age, fetal lung development, surfactant production, and fetal lung maturity to neonatal respiratory risk.

The fetal lung develops in stages

The lungs are not built all at once. They develop in a predictable sequence from early airway formation to a structure that can support gas exchange. Gestational age matters because it gives the respiratory therapist a clue about what the lungs are likely able to do at birth. A baby born very early may have enough lung structure for limited gas exchange, but the lungs may still be small, fragile, and short of effective surfactant.

The important skill is not simply memorizing dates. You should connect each stage with the new capability being developed. As the fetus moves from early airway formation toward thinner gas-exchange surfaces and more mature alveoli, the chance of successful breathing after birth improves.

EmbryonalDay 26–52Lung bud, major airways, early pulmonary veins, and diaphragm develop.
PseudoglandularDay 52–16 weeksConducting airways branch; cilia, glands, and cartilage appear.
Canalicular17–26 weeksCapillary growth accelerates; acini and type I/type II pneumocytes develop.
Saccular26–36 weeksTerminal saccules enlarge; the diffusion barrier thins and gas-exchange area increases.
Alveolar / postnatalAbout 36 weeks onwardAlveoli continue to mature and multiply rapidly after birth.

Why the canalicular phase matters

During the canalicular phase, the lung begins to develop a closer relationship between air spaces and pulmonary capillaries. Type I and type II pneumocytes also appear. This is why limited gas exchange can become possible at very early gestational ages. However, “possible” does not mean “mature.” The gas-exchange surface, pulmonary structure, and surfactant system are still developing.

Clinical connection

A newborn at about 24 weeks is in the canalicular period. The best way to think about that infant is: capillary growth and gas-exchange structures are developing, but they remain very immature. This helps explain why survival may be possible while severe respiratory support needs remain likely.

Type I and type II pneumocytes have different jobs

Two cell types are especially important in this lesson. Type I pneumocytes form a very thin surface across much of the alveolus. Their thin structure keeps the distance for oxygen and carbon dioxide movement as short as possible. Type II pneumocytes have a different role. They produce, store, secrete, and recycle pulmonary surfactant. They can also multiply after alveolar injury.

CellMain roleWhy it matters
Type I pneumocyteCreates a thin gas-diffusion surface.A thinner barrier supports movement of oxygen and carbon dioxide.
Type II pneumocyteProduces, stores, secretes, and recycles surfactant.Surfactant helps keep alveoli open and improves lung compliance.

Surfactant makes the lung easier to open and keep open

Inside the alveoli, a thin liquid layer creates surface tension. Without enough surfactant, that surface tension is high. Small alveoli then require more pressure to open and are more likely to collapse at the end of expiration. The lung becomes less compliant, meaning it is harder to inflate.

Adequate surfactant lowers surface tension. This improves alveolar stability, improves compliance, lowers the pressure needed to keep small alveoli open, and supports a more stable functional residual capacity. As a result, the newborn does less work to breathe.

Less surfactant→ Higher surface tension→ Alveolar collapse→ Lower compliance→ More work of breathing & oxygen need

For a very preterm newborn with poor compliance, repeated alveolar collapse, and increased work of breathing, immature surfactant function is a key explanation. The primary problem is not that surfactant directly adds oxygen to the blood. Its main effect is mechanical: it changes surface tension and therefore affects how easily the lung opens and remains open.

Fetal lung fluid supports growth before birth

Before birth, the fetal lungs are fluid-filled. This fluid is not simply a problem waiting to be removed. It helps keep the developing lungs expanded and supports normal lung growth. At birth, however, fluid must be absorbed and displaced as air enters the lungs and functional residual capacity is established. If too much fluid remains, it can contribute to transient tachypnea of the newborn.

Gestational age predicts respiratory risk

Earlier gestational age generally means less mature gas-exchanging structure and less mature surfactant function. This creates a predictable clinical pattern. The lungs are more likely to be difficult to inflate, alveoli are more likely to collapse, work of breathing rises, and the infant may need more oxygen or respiratory support.

  • Earlier birth: more structural immaturity and less mature surfactant function.
  • Higher surface tension: greater tendency toward alveolar collapse.
  • Lower compliance: more pressure and effort are needed to expand the lungs.
  • Clinical result: increased work of breathing, oxygen need, and possible respiratory support.

C2: Trace fetal circulation and explain the cardiopulmonary changes required for successful transition at birth.

Before birth, the placenta is the gas-exchange organ

A fetus does not use the lungs for normal gas exchange. The lungs are fluid-filled, and pulmonary blood flow is relatively low. Instead, oxygen and nutrients move from maternal blood across the placental interface into fetal blood. Carbon dioxide and other wastes move in the opposite direction toward maternal circulation. Maternal and fetal blood normally remain in separate vascular systems.

The umbilical vessels often cause confusion because their names seem opposite to what students expect. Remember that arteries and veins are named by the direction of blood flow relative to the heart, not by how much oxygen they carry.

  • Umbilical vein: carries the most oxygenated fetal blood from the placenta toward the fetus and heart.
  • Umbilical arteries: carry less-oxygenated blood away from the fetus and back toward the placenta.

The three fetal shunts redirect blood

Because the placenta provides gas exchange and the fetal lungs have high pulmonary vascular resistance, fetal circulation uses three important shunts. Each shunt allows blood to bypass an organ or route that does not yet need full blood flow.

ShuntDirectionMain purpose in fetal circulation
Ductus venosusUmbilical vein toward the inferior vena cavaBypasses much of the liver.
Foramen ovaleRight atrium → left atriumDirects relatively well-oxygenated blood toward the left heart and ascending aorta.
Ductus arteriosusPulmonary artery → aortaDiverts much of the right-ventricular output away from the high-resistance fetal lungs.

Trace the most oxygenated fetal blood

The most oxygenated blood entering the fetus is preferentially streamed toward the heart and brain. Being able to trace this pathway is more useful than memorizing the shunts as isolated vocabulary words.

Placenta→ Umbilical vein→ Ductus venosus→ IVC→ Right atrium→ Foramen ovale→ Left atrium→ Left ventricle→ Ascending aorta→ Heart & brain

Why fetal pulmonary vascular resistance is high

Before birth, the fetal lungs are fluid-filled, unexpanded, and relatively hypoxic. These conditions keep pulmonary vascular resistance, or PVR, high. High PVR limits blood flow through the lungs. Because resistance through the pulmonary circulation is high, much of the right-ventricular output is diverted through the ductus arteriosus into the aorta instead of traveling through the lungs.

Fetal pattern: fluid-filled lungs + low oxygen tension → high PVR → low pulmonary blood flow → greater use of fetal shunts.

Birth requires two major system changes

The transition at birth can be understood as two major events happening together: the lungs open and the placental circulation is removed. The first effective breaths expand the alveoli and raise oxygen tension. This causes pulmonary vascular resistance to fall. As PVR falls, pulmonary blood flow rises.

More blood returning from the lungs raises left-atrial pressure. That pressure change helps functionally close the foramen ovale. At the same time, clamping the umbilical cord removes the low-resistance placental vascular bed. Systemic vascular resistance, or SVR, therefore rises, and aortic pressure increases.

Higher oxygen tension and a lower prostaglandin effect also promote constriction and functional closure of the ductus arteriosus.

C3: Recognize how prematurity, pulmonary hypoplasia, and abnormal transition can impair neonatal oxygenation and ventilation.

Prematurity can limit both structure and lung mechanics

Prematurity increases respiratory risk because the lungs may not have completed the structural and functional changes needed for stable breathing. A very preterm newborn may have immature gas-exchanging structures and inadequate surfactant function at the same time. These problems can combine to make the lungs difficult to inflate and less stable between breaths.

  • Compliance may be poor, so more pressure or effort is needed to inflate the lungs.
  • Alveoli may be unstable and collapse more easily.
  • Work of breathing may rise quickly.
  • Oxygen need may increase.
  • Respiratory support may be needed to maintain adequate gas exchange.

When you see prematurity in a clinical question, connect gestational age to what the lung has had time to develop. Do not treat prematurity as a diagnosis by itself. Use it as a clue to likely problems with lung structure, surfactant-related stability, and respiratory reserve.

Pulmonary hypoplasia means incomplete lung growth

Pulmonary hypoplasia is more than having “small lungs.” It means lung development is incomplete. The infant may have reduced lung volume, fewer or smaller airways and alveoli, and a reduced pulmonary vascular bed. This limits the available area for ventilation and gas exchange and may also contribute to high pulmonary vascular resistance.

The lesson connects pulmonary hypoplasia with conditions that reduce space for lung growth, reduce the normal fluid environment needed for lung expansion, or reduce fetal breathing movement.

  • Reduced intrathoracic space: congenital diaphragmatic hernia, thoracic masses, or skeletal restriction.
  • Reduced fetal lung or amniotic fluid: prolonged oligohydramnios or early membrane rupture.
  • Reduced fetal breathing movement: neuromuscular or central disorders.

Clinical connection: oligohydramnios

Fetal lung fluid and the surrounding fluid environment help support normal lung expansion and growth. Prolonged oligohydramnios is therefore an important history finding because it can interfere with normal lung development and increase the risk of pulmonary hypoplasia.

Why pulmonary hypoplasia can cause severe respiratory failure

A hypoplastic lung has less functional lung tissue and a smaller pulmonary vascular bed. The newborn may have difficulty moving enough air, oxygenating the blood, and establishing the expected fall in pulmonary vascular resistance. Severe respiratory failure and pulmonary hypertension can occur at birth.

This is an important distinction from surfactant deficiency alone. Surfactant deficiency mainly affects alveolar surface tension and compliance. Pulmonary hypoplasia reflects incomplete development of the lung itself, including the pulmonary blood vessels.

Abnormal transition keeps the circulation too fetal

Successful transition requires several changes to occur in the correct direction. The lungs must expand, oxygen tension must rise, PVR must fall, pulmonary blood flow must increase, and pulmonary venous return must raise left-atrial pressure. The placental circulation also has to be removed so SVR rises.

If the newborn does not aerate the lungs effectively, this sequence is disrupted at the beginning. PVR does not fall as expected. Pulmonary blood flow therefore does not increase normally. Less blood returns from the lungs to the left atrium, and fetal circulatory patterns may persist. The result is impaired pulmonary gas exchange and continued oxygenation problems.

Failed-transition chain

Ineffective lung aeration → PVR remains elevated → pulmonary blood flow fails to rise normally → pulmonary venous return remains limited → fetal circulatory patterns persist → oxygenation remains impaired.

Use the history to predict the physiology

Clinical questions often give you a history before they give you the diagnosis. The goal is to use that history to predict what is likely happening in the lungs and circulation.
