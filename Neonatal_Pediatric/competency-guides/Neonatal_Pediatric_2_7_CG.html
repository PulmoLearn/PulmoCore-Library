C1: Evaluate readiness for home monitoring and discharge for technology-dependent neonatal and pediatric patients.

Discharge is a whole-system test

A technology-dependent child is not ready for discharge simply because vital signs are stable today. Hospital discharge removes continuous bedside supervision, so the entire care system must be able to function safely outside the hospital.

Readiness includes the child, caregivers, home equipment, power and backup systems, supplies, home environment, school or daycare plan, durable medical equipment support, follow-up contacts, and emergency pathways.

The child must be stable on the support that will actually be used at home

  • Stable oxygen and ventilation needs on the planned home support
  • Manageable secretion burden
  • A workable nutrition and feeding plan
  • Medication plan understood and deliverable
  • Clear thresholds for deterioration and escalation

Home monitoring needs a defined purpose

A home monitor should answer a specific clinical need. Caregivers must know what the alarm means, what the monitor cannot tell them, how to assess the child before reacting to a number, and when the written plan says to escalate.

Readiness domainQuestions to answer before discharge
ClinicalIs the child stable on the planned home respiratory support?
CaregiverCan caregivers independently perform routine and emergency skills?
EquipmentAre devices installed, tested, supplied, and backed up?
Home systemAre reliable electricity, communication, storage, fire safety, and emergency access available?
ContinuityAre DME/vendor support, follow-up contacts, school/daycare plans, and escalation pathways clear?
Clinical reasoning: Equipment delivery is not the same as discharge readiness. The home system must prove that it can sustain both routine care and predictable emergencies.

C2: Select, troubleshoot, and teach the safe use of home oxygen, positive-airway-pressure therapy, ventilation, humidification, apnea monitoring, pulse oximetry, suction, and medication-delivery equipment.

Teach every home device by function

Caregivers do not need a list of machines without context. They need to understand what each device is supposed to accomplish, what can fail, what the backup is, and what action comes first when the device alarms or appears ineffective.

Home troubleshooting starts with the child

Child → power/source → settings → tubing/circuit → interface or airway patency → humidification → alarms/sensors → backup equipment

A high-pressure ventilator alarm, for example, does not tell the caregiver exactly what is wrong. The child may have thick secretions, a kinked circuit, an airway obstruction, or another problem. Assess the child first, then work through the equipment systematically.

Clinical reasoning: An alarm is information. It should trigger patient assessment and troubleshooting—not automatic setting changes or reflexive silencing.

C3: Teach caregivers artificial-airway management, emergency response, CPR, safe sleep, infection or distress recognition, and other discharge needs.

Home airway care includes routine care and emergency rescue

Caregivers of a child with an artificial airway must be able to maintain the airway on an ordinary day and respond when the airway suddenly becomes unsafe. Teaching therefore includes patency, humidification, secretion management, suction, securement, routine tracheostomy care, and the child-specific plan for obstruction or accidental decannulation.

Emergency thresholds must be explicit

Caregivers should know when a problem can be addressed with the home plan, when to contact the clinical team, and when to activate EMS. Airway support and CPR expectations should follow the child’s discharge plan and the training the caregiver has received.

Teach early signs of deterioration

  • Fever or a new infection concern
  • Change in secretion amount, color, or character
  • Increasing work of breathing
  • Color or mental-status change
  • Falling saturation
  • Repeated alarms
  • Increasing oxygen or ventilatory support needs

Safe sleep and positioning are part of discharge teaching

Safe-sleep practices and positioning instructions should be individualized to the prescribed plan and explained clearly to every caregiver. The same emergency and positioning instructions should follow the child across home, school/daycare, respite, transport, and emergency settings.

Written and visual plans improve consistency

A child-specific written emergency plan helps different caregivers respond in the same way. It should travel with the child so that school staff, respite caregivers, transport teams, and emergency clinicians can quickly understand the baseline support and escalation plan.

Clinical reasoning: Home airway safety depends on predictable responses. Caregivers should not have to invent the obstruction or decannulation plan during the emergency.

Be ready to identify the essential routine and emergency airway skills caregivers need before discharge and the deterioration signs that should trigger escalation.
C4: Verify caregiver ability to set up, operate, clean, disinfect, and troubleshoot respiratory equipment.

Competency must be demonstrated

Hearing instructions is not the same as performing a skill. A caregiver may say “I understand” but still be unable to assemble a circuit, identify a blocked suction device, or respond safely to a power failure. The strongest evidence of readiness is independent performance.

Return demonstration verifies hands-on skill

The caregiver should independently assemble, operate, and verify each required device. This includes changing disposable supplies, cleaning and disinfecting reusable components according to the plan, and storing equipment correctly after cleaning.

Troubleshooting competency includes knowing when home correction is no longer enough

Caregivers should know which problems can be corrected with the written home plan, which require DME or clinical support, and which require emergency activation.

Teaching methodWhat it verifies
DemonstrationShows the caregiver how the skill is performed
Return demonstrationVerifies the caregiver can perform the skill independently
Teach-backVerifies understanding of purpose, response, warning signs, and escalation logic

Practice predictable emergencies before discharge

  • Power failure
  • Oxygen interruption
  • Ventilator alarm
  • Suction failure
  • Airway obstruction or accidental decannulation when applicable
  • Monitor alarm
  • Device disconnection
Clinical reasoning: Attendance verifies exposure to teaching. Return demonstration and scenario rehearsal verify whether the caregiver can actually keep the child safe.
Quiz-connected review
Be ready to distinguish demonstration, return demonstration, teach-back, and scenario rehearsal and to explain why each provides different evidence of caregiver readiness.
C5: Verify safe medication-administration technique and understanding.

A medication list is not proof of medication competency

Caregivers must know more than the medication name. They should identify the purpose of each prescribed respiratory medication, use the correct delivery device and interface, perform the full administration sequence, and understand what response or warning signs matter afterward.

Observe the complete sequence

  1. Identify the correct medication and its purpose.
  2. Prepare the prescribed device correctly.
  3. Position the child and interface appropriately.
  4. Complete the treatment correctly.
  5. Recognize the intended effect and warning signs.
  6. Follow the written plan for a missed dose or unexpected response.
  7. Clean the device as instructed.

Teach-back verifies decision understanding

A caregiver may perform a familiar routine correctly but still be unsure what to do when the child responds unexpectedly. Teach-back helps verify that the caregiver understands the expected effect, warning signs, and the written next-step plan.

Clinical reasoning: Safe home medication administration requires both technical skill and decision understanding. Knowing the drug name alone proves neither.
Quiz-connected review
Be ready to identify what must be observed and taught before a caregiver is considered competent to administer respiratory medications at home.
C6: Implement safety practices that reduce preventable respiratory-care complications and equipment-related harm.

Home respiratory safety is built around predictable failure

The goal of home safety planning is not to pretend equipment never fails. It is to reduce preventable harm by preparing for predictable interruptions, device problems, infection risk, and human error.

Redundancy creates safety

  • Backup oxygen and power
  • Spare airway supplies
  • Emergency bag
  • Portable suction when needed
  • Written contingency plans

Oxygen introduces a fire and cylinder hazard

Home oxygen safety includes no smoking or open flames, secure storage of cylinders, avoidance of unsafe products or equipment around oxygen, and adequate portable reserve for emergencies or transport.

Infection prevention continues at home

Caregivers need a consistent plan for hand hygiene, cleaning and disinfection, replacement of disposable supplies, drying and storage of reusable equipment, and recognition of infection signs.

Alarm safety means preserving meaningful alarms

Frequent nuisance alarms can create alarm fatigue. The solution is not to disable monitoring indiscriminately. Verify sensor reliability, use appropriate prescribed alarm limits, correct avoidable nuisance causes, and teach caregivers to assess the patient before silencing the alarm.

Prevent interface and equipment injury

Protect skin, secure tubing and artificial airways, avoid unapproved changes to settings, and replace damaged equipment through the designated clinical or DME pathway.

Clinical reasoning: A safe home system assumes that power, oxygen, suction, tubing, sensors, or caregivers can encounter problems—and creates a backup before the problem occurs.
Quiz-connected review
Be ready to identify redundancy, oxygen fire safety, infection prevention, alarm management, skin/device protection, and backup planning as core elements of home respiratory safety.
C7: Participate in interdisciplinary decision-making, escalation, palliative respiratory care, and family-centered end-of-life support.

Respiratory care should serve the child’s goals

Palliative care does not mean “stop all treatment.” It focuses on symptom relief, communication, quality of life, family support, and care that matches the child’s and family’s goals. It can occur alongside disease-directed treatment.

Respiratory comfort measures can remain appropriate

Depending on the plan and goals, comfort-focused respiratory care may include positioning, airflow, secretion management, oxygen when it relieves symptoms, noninvasive respiratory support when it improves comfort, and medication-assisted symptom relief directed by the care plan.

Goal-concordant NIV can coexist with palliative care

If noninvasive ventilation reduces dyspnea and helps the child rest, it may remain appropriate even when the overall goals have shifted toward comfort—provided that it remains tolerable and consistent with the agreed escalation limits.

Withdrawal of ventilatory support should be planned around comfort

  • Prepare the family and interdisciplinary team.
  • Ensure symptom-treatment measures are ready before support is changed.
  • Reduce unnecessary alarms and monitoring burden.
  • Follow the agreed device and withdrawal plan.
  • Continuously reassess comfort.
  • Respect family presence, culture, rituals, and bereavement needs.

Interdisciplinary communication remains essential

When the child deteriorates or goals change, communicate objective findings, trends, current respiratory support, response to interventions, and a specific recommendation. The care team needs to understand not only what the respiratory technology can do, but what the treatment is trying to accomplish now.

QuestionReasoning focus
What is the treatment trying to accomplish now?Disease modification, life prolongation, symptom relief, comfort, or a combination
Does the respiratory support improve comfort?Continue or modify support according to tolerance and goals
What burdens does the technology create?Consider alarms, interfaces, distress, communication limitations, and family priorities
What are the agreed escalation limits?Prevent unwanted escalation that conflicts with the care plan
Clinical reasoning: The question is not simply “Can this technology be continued?” Ask whether it improves comfort, matches the stated goals, and creates an acceptable burden for the child and family.

Be ready to explain how palliative care can coexist with respiratory treatment, how goal-concordant NIV may support comfort, and how withdrawal of ventilatory support should be prepared and monitored.
