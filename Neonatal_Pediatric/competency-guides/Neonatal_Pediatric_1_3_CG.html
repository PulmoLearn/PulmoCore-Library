C1: Establish and maintain a patent neonatal airway and select appropriate airway equipment.

Airway management begins with the least invasive action that solves the problem

A newborn airway can become ineffective because of poor position, obstructing secretions, or failure to breathe. The first goal is to create a patent path for air. In the course, initial airway care starts with neutral or slightly extended positioning. Suction is selective rather than routine because unnecessary suction can delay the ventilation that may be most important.

Choose the airway tool for the clinical need

Clinical situationAirway / ventilation choiceWhy
Newborn breathing with no obstructing secretionsPosition and observe; suction only if obstruction is present.Routine suction is not needed when air is moving effectively.
Apnea, gasping, or heart rate below 100/min after initial stepsFace-mask positive-pressure ventilation.Assisted ventilation is the first major intervention for inadequate breathing.
Mask ventilation remains ineffective after corrective steps or prolonged ventilation is expectedPrepare an endotracheal tube.A secured airway can provide more reliable ventilation.
Mask ventilation fails and intubation is not feasible or unsuccessful in an appropriately sized infantConsider a laryngeal mask.It provides an alternative route for ventilation.
Suspected lower-airway obstruction or compressions requiredAdvanced airway should be considered/prepared.More reliable access and ventilation may be needed during advanced resuscitation.

Airway selection should follow the resuscitation problem rather than a memorized device order. The question is always: Is the airway open? Are the lungs actually being ventilated? If not, what is the next least invasive step that can make ventilation reliable?

Meconium does not change the basic priority

Meconium-stained fluid does not automatically mean routine tracheal suctioning. Standard initial steps and PPV are still used when indicated. Intubation and tracheal suction may be considered if airway obstruction prevents effective ventilation.

C2: Perform or assist with neonatal intubation and verify placement using carbon dioxide detection and clinical assessment.

Intubation is useful only if the tube is actually ventilating the lungs

An endotracheal tube may be needed when effective mask ventilation cannot be achieved, prolonged ventilation is expected, suspected lower-airway obstruction must be addressed, or chest compressions are required. Placement is not considered successful simply because the tube passed through the mouth or a pressure number appears on the device. Verification must show that gas is reaching the lungs and that the infant is responding.

Use multiple signs to verify placement

  • Exhaled carbon dioxide: helps show that exhaled gas is returning through the tube.
  • Visible bilateral chest movement: supports that ventilation is reaching the lungs.
  • Bilateral breath sounds: help evaluate whether ventilation is reaching both lungs.
  • Rising heart rate: is an important physiologic sign that ventilation is becoming effective.

No single sign should be treated as the only proof. Exhaled CO₂ is valuable, but the course emphasizes clinical assessment and physiologic response together. A tube that appears technically placed but does not produce chest movement or heart-rate improvement must be questioned.

Recognize signs that require immediate reassessment

FindingWhat it should make you thinkNext priority
Unexpected or worsening bradycardiaVentilation may still be ineffective.Reassess tube and lung inflation immediately.
Absent chest movementTube may be misplaced, obstructed, or ventilation may be inadequate.Check depth, patency, and position.
Asymmetric breath soundsTube depth or one-sided ventilation may be a concern.Reassess tube position and clinical response.

Physiology confirms the procedure

A correctly placed airway should allow effective lung inflation. During neonatal resuscitation, the clinical response—especially improvement in heart rate—matters as much as the technical confirmation steps.

C3: Select neonatal resuscitation equipment and apply the neonatal resuscitation sequence.

Resuscitation readiness should mirror the possible escalation sequence

The safest time to gather neonatal resuscitation equipment is before it is needed. Delivery risk should guide readiness. A low-risk delivery may remain routine, while a high-risk delivery should have equipment available for thermal stabilization, positive-pressure ventilation, oxygen and monitoring, an advanced airway, and emergency vascular or medication support.

Emergency escalationUmbilical venous access and medication capabilitySupport drug or volume administration when severe bradycardia persists despite effective ventilation and compressions.

The sequence is driven by breathing and heart rate

The course sequence can be remembered as a decision pathway rather than a long checklist: provide warmth, position the airway, clear secretions only when obstructing, dry and stimulate; then assess breathing and heart rate. If apnea, gasping, or heart rate below 100/min persists, begin PPV. If ventilation is ineffective, correct it. If an advanced airway is needed, place and verify it. Chest compressions come only after effective lung inflation has been established and severe bradycardia remains.

APGAR is not the trigger

APGAR describes the infant’s condition at defined times. It should never delay PPV or another resuscitation step that is already indicated by breathing and heart rate.

Preductal oxygenation and heart-rate monitoring

During neonatal resuscitation, the right hand or wrist is used for preductal pulse oximetry. In advanced resuscitation, ECG provides the fastest reliable heart-rate assessment. These tools support the team, but the resuscitation sequence still depends on integrating the monitor with the infant’s breathing, chest movement, color, and response to ventilation.

C4: Provide effective positive-pressure ventilation, recognize ineffective ventilation, and systematically correct ventilation problems.

Ventilation is the central intervention in neonatal resuscitation

Most severe newborn bradycardia during transition is closely related to inadequate ventilation. That is why PPV is started for apnea, gasping, or heart rate below 100/min after initial steps. The lesson uses 30–60 inflations per minute and emphasizes adjusting pressure to produce gentle visible chest movement and a physiologic response.

How do you know PPV is working?

A rising heart rate is the strongest indicator that lung inflation is effective. Gentle bilateral chest movement is the key visual sign. A pressure value on a device, oxygen flowing through the circuit, or a mask that feels secure does not prove that the lungs are being ventilated.

Persistent bradycardia during PPV

Assume ventilation may be ineffective until you have evidence that the lungs are inflating. Correct ventilation before moving to chest compressions.

MR SOPA is a troubleshooting sequence

C5: Escalate care appropriately for persistent apnea, bradycardia, or inadequate ventilation and communicate concerns to the team.

Escalation depends on the response to effective ventilation

The newborn should not move to chest compressions simply because PPV was attempted. The course requires at least 30 seconds of ventilation that visibly inflates the lungs before severe persistent bradycardia is judged to require compressions. If the heart rate remains below 60/min despite effective ventilation, begin coordinated compressions and ventilation.

Advanced resuscitation sequence

  • Use an advanced airway during compressions as taught in the lesson.
  • Use a 3:1 compression-to-ventilation ratio.
  • Use 100% oxygen during compressions as taught in the lesson.
  • Reassess heart rate after approximately 60 seconds of coordinated compressions and ventilation.
  • If heart rate remains below 60/min, the lesson directs epinephrine through an intravascular route, preferably umbilical venous access in delivery-room resuscitation.
  • Consider volume expansion when blood loss or shock is suspected.

Communication is part of escalation

Persistent apnea, bradycardia, absent chest movement, or inadequate response to ventilation are “speak up now” findings. The respiratory therapist should communicate both the problem and the next needed action. A useful statement identifies what is happening, what has already been attempted, and what should be prepared next.

Example communication pattern

“Heart rate remains below 60 despite visible chest movement with effective ventilation. Prepare the advanced airway and begin coordinated compressions.” The exact words can vary; the important feature is clear physiologic information linked to the next action.

Postresuscitation care continues after the heart rate improves

Recovery of heart rate does not end assessment. The infant still needs reassessment of respiratory status and broader stability, along with documentation, communication with the family, and team debriefing. A newborn who required significant resuscitation may still deteriorate after the immediate emergency has passed.

Meconium: keep priorities in the correct order

A nonvigorous newborn with meconium-stained fluid should still receive standard initial steps and PPV when breathing and heart rate indicate it. Routine endotracheal suctioning is not recommended solely because meconium is present. Intubation and tracheal suction may be considered when airway obstruction is preventing effective ventilation.
