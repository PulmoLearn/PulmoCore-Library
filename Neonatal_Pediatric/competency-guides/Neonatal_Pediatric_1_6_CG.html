C1: Select and troubleshoot neonatal oxygen-delivery, noninvasive-support, ventilator, humidification, and monitoring equipment.

Start with the problem the infant actually has

Respiratory support is not one treatment. It is a ladder of different tools that solve different problems. Some infants mainly need more oxygen. Others need help keeping the lungs open. Some need backup breaths because respiratory drive is unreliable. The sickest infants may need a protected airway and full mechanical ventilation.

Before selecting a device, ask a simple question: What is failing—oxygenation, lung volume, ventilation, respiratory drive, or airway protection? The answer guides the level of support.

OxygenRaises inspired oxygen concentration when oxygenation is the main problem and ventilation is otherwise adequate.
HFNCProvides heated humidified flow, reduces upper-airway dead space, and may create variable distending pressure.
CPAP / NIPPVCPAP holds the lungs open; NIPPV adds intermittent positive-pressure breaths when more ventilatory assistance is needed.
Invasive ventilationUsed when oxygenation, ventilation, airway protection, or respiratory drive cannot be maintained with noninvasive support.

Why humidification matters

A newborn’s airway is small and easily affected by drying, thick secretions, and heat loss. Heated humidification is therefore part of the respiratory-support system, not an optional accessory. When gas is inadequately heated or humidified, secretions can become more difficult to clear and airway resistance can increase.

Treat the device as a complete system

When support appears to be failing, do not assume the infant suddenly needs a more aggressive mode. First verify that the equipment is actually delivering what you think it is delivering. A loose interface, blocked cannula, disconnection, circuit leak, condensate, disabled alarm, or misplaced sensor can mimic worsening disease.

SystemVerifyCommon problems
Oxygen / HFNCSource, flow, FiO₂, cannula fit, humidification, SpO₂ signalDisconnection, occlusion, poor fit, dry/cool gas, unreliable oximetry
CPAP / NIPPVInterface seal, pressure, circuit, humidification, backup breaths, alarmsLeak, nasal obstruction, interface injury, gastric distention, poor synchrony
Invasive ventilatorETT, circuit, humidifier, settings, exhaled volume, pressure, monitoring, alarmsDisconnection, leak, obstruction, condensate, malposition, mechanics change
Clinical reasoning: If an infant suddenly deteriorates after being stable, verify the infant, interface or airway, circuit, device, humidification, alarms, and monitoring before assuming the disease itself has progressed.

C2: Assess the neonatal airway and manage endotracheal tube size, positioning, securement, and verification.

An endotracheal tube is not “finished” once it is inserted

Neonatal airways are small, and small changes in tube position can have major effects. Safe ETT management includes four separate tasks: selecting an appropriate tube, placing it to a reasonable starting depth, securing it without injuring fragile skin, and repeatedly verifying that it remains in the correct position.

The course does not teach one universal tube-size formula. Instead, select the ETT using gestational age and weight with the approved neonatal airway reference for the clinical setting. Backup airway equipment should be available before the procedure begins.

Initial depth is a starting estimate, not proof

A planned insertion depth and lip or gum marking help guide and document placement, but a number at the lip does not prove that the distal tube tip is correctly located. Head position, movement, retaping, transport, and procedures can all change tube position.

ETT taskWhat to do
SizeUse gestational age/weight with the approved neonatal airway reference; prepare backup equipment.
DepthUse the planned insertion reference and visible lip/gum mark as a starting point.
SecurementProtect fragile skin, reduce circuit traction, and document the external marking.
VerificationUse exhaled CO₂, chest movement, bilateral breath sounds, physiologic response, depth mark, ventilator data, and radiograph.

Multimodal verification is safer than one sign

No single sign should be treated as the entire verification process. Exhaled carbon dioxide helps confirm that ventilation is reaching the airway. Bilateral chest movement and breath sounds help identify major asymmetry. Improving heart rate and oxygenation suggest effective ventilation. Ventilator pressures and exhaled volumes help show whether gas is moving as expected. A chest radiograph confirms the tube’s radiographic position.

From Lesson 1.4, the expected radiographic position is in the mid-trachea with the head neutral and safely above the carina.

Know the clues that demand immediate reassessment

  • Sudden low exhaled tidal volume or low-pressure alarm
  • Loss of the documented lip/gum marking
  • Asymmetric breath sounds or chest movement
  • Unexpected bradycardia or desaturation
  • New difficulty delivering pressure or volume

These changes should trigger assessment for obstruction, displacement, mainstem position, or accidental extubation before ventilator settings are increased.

Clinical reasoning: A tube that was correctly positioned an hour ago may not be correctly positioned now. Reassess after retaping, repositioning, transport, procedures, or unexplained changes in oxygenation, pressure, volume, or breath sounds.

C3: Select appropriate initial ventilator settings for neonatal respiratory failure.

Ventilator settings are tools for changing physiology

There is no single set of neonatal ventilator settings that fits every infant. Initial settings should match the infant’s size, disease physiology, current gas exchange, and the specific problem you are trying to correct. The goal is to achieve adequate oxygenation and ventilation with the lowest effective support.

A useful way to learn ventilator controls is to ask, “What physiologic variable does this setting change?” That question is more useful than memorizing a number without knowing its purpose.

ControlMain effectImportant caution
FiO₂Changes inspired oxygen concentration.Titrate to the ordered saturation target rather than leaving unnecessary oxygen in place.
PEEPMaintains end-expiratory lung volume and recruitment.Too little promotes collapse; too much can overdistend lung and impair venous return.
PIP / inspiratory pressureAffects the pressure gradient and delivered VT in pressure ventilation.Delivered VT changes when compliance, resistance, or leak changes.
Target VTSets the desired exhaled tidal volume in volume-targeted ventilation.Monitor the pressure required to reach the target.
RateChanges mandatory breath frequency and contributes to minute ventilation.Excessive rate can shorten expiratory time and worsen air trapping.
Inspiratory timeChanges filling time and contributes to mean airway pressure.Match timing to the disease time constants and allow enough exhalation.
Trigger sensitivityDetermines how easily patient effort starts a supported breath.Too insensitive can cause missed efforts; too sensitive can contribute to auto-triggering.

Match the settings to the disease

A low-compliance infant with RDS tends to have unstable, poorly recruited lung units. The strategy therefore includes adequate recruitment and PEEP while limiting excessive pressure and volume. By contrast, MAS or another higher-resistance problem needs enough time for gas to move in and, especially, enough time to move back out so air trapping does not worsen.

An infant whose main problem is apnea may have relatively normal lung mechanics but unreliable respiratory drive. That infant needs dependable backup ventilation while avoiding unnecessary pressure and oxygen.

Low complianceThink recruitment, adequate PEEP, and careful control of pressure and volume.
High resistanceThink filling and exhalation time, obstruction, and avoiding gas trapping.
Low respiratory driveThink reliable backup breaths with the minimum pressure and oxygen needed.
Clinical reasoning: Choose the setting after naming the problem. FiO₂ cannot correct hypercapnia, and a higher pressure is not automatically the answer to every low oxygen saturation.

C4: Differentiate and apply invasive and noninvasive ventilation modes.

Modes differ by what they provide and what the infant must still do

The support ladder becomes easier to understand when you ask what work remains the infant’s responsibility. Oxygen can improve inspired oxygen concentration but does not create reliable positive pressure. CPAP helps keep the lungs open but requires spontaneous breathing. NIPPV keeps a baseline distending pressure while also adding intermittent positive-pressure breaths. Invasive ventilation provides support through an endotracheal tube when noninvasive support is not enough.

ModeWhat it providesBest conceptual use
Low-flow oxygenSupplemental oxygen concentrationOxygenation deficit without major ventilatory failure or need for distending pressure
HFNCHeated humidified flow, dead-space washout, variable distending pressureIntermediate noninvasive support when controlled pressure is not the main goal
CPAPContinuous distending pressureRecruitment and FRC support in a spontaneously breathing infant
NIPPVBaseline CPAP/PEEP plus intermittent positive-pressure breathsAdditional ventilatory assistance, including recurrent apnea
Pressure-controlled ventilationControlled pressure; VT variesInvasive support where pressure is set and delivered volume must be watched closely
Volume-targeted ventilationTarget exhaled VT; required pressure variesInvasive support where pressure adapts to achieve the volume target

Pressure-controlled and volume-targeted ventilation respond differently to changing mechanics

In pressure-controlled ventilation, the set pressure stays the same, but the delivered tidal volume changes if compliance, resistance, or leak changes. If compliance suddenly improves, the same pressure may deliver a larger VT than before. If compliance worsens, the delivered VT may fall.

In volume-targeted ventilation, the desired exhaled tidal volume is the target, and the pressure required to achieve that volume can change. The clinician must watch both the volume and the pressure needed to deliver it.

Escalation does not have to follow every step in order

The support ladder is a reasoning tool, not a rigid sequence. A mildly hypoxemic infant may only need oxygen. A spontaneously breathing infant with low lung volume may benefit from CPAP. Recurrent apnea may require NIPPV. Severe apnea, bradycardia, shock, inability to protect the airway, or failure of noninvasive support may require immediate invasive ventilation.

Clinical reasoning: CPAP is not a backup-breath mode. If the infant’s problem changes from poor recruitment to recurrent apnea with rising CO₂, the support choice should change too.
Quiz-connected review
Be ready to distinguish oxygen, HFNC, CPAP, NIPPV, pressure-controlled ventilation, and volume-targeted ventilation by what each mode actually provides.
C5: Monitor gas exchange, airway pressures, volumes, pulmonary mechanics, and patient-ventilator interaction.

A ventilator number only matters when you know what it means

Mechanical ventilation is dynamic. The infant’s lung disease can improve or worsen, the airway can become obstructed, leak can change, and the infant may begin fighting or missing the ventilator. That means monitoring must include both the patient and the machine.

Use trends rather than isolated values. A single blood gas, pressure reading, or tidal volume is less useful than seeing how those measurements move together over time and after an intervention.

Gas exchangeSpO₂, oxygen requirement, blood gases, and when used ETCO₂ or transcutaneous CO₂.
Ventilator dataAirway pressure, exhaled VT, rate, minute ventilation, leak, and alarm patterns.
MechanicsCompliance and resistance help explain why the same settings may deliver different results over time.

Compliance and resistance change what the ventilator delivers

Compliance describes how easily the respiratory system expands. When compliance worsens, more pressure may be needed to produce the same volume, and in pressure ventilation the delivered VT may fall. Resistance describes how difficult it is for gas to move through the airways. Increased resistance can slow filling and exhalation and may contribute to air trapping.

Use the blood gas with ventilator data, not by itself

ProblemWhat to assess
High PaCO₂ + low exhaled VTAirway patency, leak, pressure or target VT, rate, synchrony, and dead space.
Hypoxemia + atelectasisRecruitment, PEEP/mean airway pressure, position, airway/interface, and FiO₂.
Hypoxemia + hyperinflationAir trapping, overdistention, PPHN, air leak, and perfusion before reflexively raising PEEP.

Patient-ventilator interaction: is the machine responding to the infant correctly?

A ventilator should support patient effort rather than ignore it or create extra breaths that the patient did not request. Two important patterns are missed efforts and auto-triggering.

Missed effortsThe infant tries to breathe but the ventilator does not recognize the effort. Consider insensitive triggering, weak respiratory drive, obstruction, excessive work to trigger, or leak.
Auto-triggeringThe ventilator delivers breaths without true patient effort. Consider excessive sensitivity, leak, condensate, or circuit movement.

Alarm patterns are clues

A high-pressure alarm may point toward obstruction, secretions, worsening compliance, pneumothorax, or patient conflict. A low-pressure or low-volume alarm may suggest disconnection, leak, accidental extubation, or inadequate support. The safest response is to assess the infant first, then the airway, circuit, ventilator, and data before changing settings.

Clinical reasoning: If PaCO₂ rises while exhaled VT falls, do not immediately increase pressure. First determine whether the problem is airway obstruction, leak, synchrony, changing mechanics, or an actual need for more support.

C6: Modify respiratory support while minimizing ventilator-induced lung injury and preparing for liberation.

The goal is the lowest effective support, not the biggest improvement in a number

Mechanical ventilation can be lifesaving, but it can also injure immature lungs. The clinician must balance two risks: too little support can cause hypoxemia, hypercapnia, fatigue, and collapse, while too much support can overdistend or repeatedly injure fragile lung units.

That is why ventilator management is a cycle: support the infant, measure the response, identify what can be reduced, and reassess. Improvement should lead to thoughtful reduction of unnecessary pressure, volume, oxygen, and support—not to leaving the infant on the same settings indefinitely.

Three major patterns of ventilator-related lung injury

Injury patternWhat it meansPrevention principle
VolutraumaInjury from excessive tidal volume and alveolar stretch.Monitor exhaled VT and avoid unnecessary overdistention.
BarotraumaInjury associated with excessive pressure and air leak.Use the lowest effective pressure and evaluate sudden air-leak patterns.
AtelectraumaInjury from repeated opening and collapse of unstable lung units.Maintain enough recruitment to avoid repeated collapse without causing overdistention.

Lung protection includes more than ventilator pressure

  • Use heated humidification appropriately.
  • Protect nasal tissue and fragile skin from interface injury.
  • Reduce unnecessary circuit dead space.
  • Avoid unnecessary circuit disconnections.
  • Reassess sedation and synchrony when patient-ventilator interaction is poor.
  • Use exhaled VT, pressure trends, compliance, blood gases, oxygen need, chest movement, and imaging to find the lowest effective support.

Liberation starts before extubation day

An infant is not ready for extubation simply because the ventilator settings look low. Readiness requires evidence that the infant can continue to succeed when the artificial airway and mandatory support are removed.

Disease / mechanicsImproving lung disease and compliance with less need for pressure or oxygen.
Gas exchangeStable oxygenation and ventilation on reduced support.
Respiratory driveReliable breathing effort with manageable apnea.
Airway / secretionsA patent airway and secretion burden the infant can manage with planned support.
Systemic stabilityThe infant is stable enough that respiratory work will not be undermined by broader deterioration.
Postextubation planCPAP, NIPPV, HFNC, oxygen, positioning, suction, monitoring, and criteria for re-escalation are planned in advance.

Step-down support should match the remaining deficit

A baby who still needs distending pressure after extubation may transition to CPAP. An infant who remains at risk for apnea may need NIPPV. A baby who mainly needs flow, humidification, or modest oxygen support may transition to HFNC or oxygen. The postextubation plan should be chosen before the tube is removed, not after the infant begins to struggle.

Clinical reasoning: “Low settings” are only one piece of readiness. Stable gas exchange, reliable respiratory drive, manageable secretions, airway readiness, systemic stability, and a postextubation plan are also required.
