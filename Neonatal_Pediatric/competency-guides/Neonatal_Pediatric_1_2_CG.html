C1: Evaluate maternal history and pregnancy factors that increase neonatal respiratory risk.

Maternal history is part of the respiratory assessment before birth

Respiratory care at a high-risk delivery begins before the infant is born. Maternal and pregnancy information gives the team clues about what may happen during transition. The goal is not to memorize a list of obstetric conditions. The goal is to translate each risk factor into a likely neonatal problem, then decide what equipment, monitoring, and personnel should already be available.

A useful handoff includes gestational age and estimated fetal weight, prenatal care, maternal disorders, medication or anesthesia exposure, infection status, membrane rupture and duration, amniotic fluid amount, fetal monitoring, fetal presentation, and planned delivery method. Each item can change the newborn’s respiratory reserve or the chance that resuscitation will be needed.

Think like the delivery-room RT

For every risk factor, ask three questions: What neonatal problem is more likely? What finding after birth would confirm that problem? What respiratory equipment or personnel should be ready before delivery?

C2: Interpret fetal and neonatal assessment information including biophysical profile, APGAR score, and fetal lung maturity information.

Antenatal testing shows how the fetus is tolerating pregnancy

Maternal history tells you what risks exist. Fetal assessment tells you whether the fetus may already be showing effects of those risks. The respiratory therapist does not need to manage obstetric testing, but should understand what the findings mean for delivery-room readiness.

Nonstress test and biophysical profile

A nonstress test looks for fetal heart-rate accelerations associated with movement. A biophysical profile combines several observations: fetal movement, fetal tone, fetal breathing movement, amniotic fluid, and heart-rate testing. A concerning fetal assessment should increase preparation because the newborn may arrive with less physiologic reserve.

Amniotic fluid index

The amniotic fluid index estimates fluid volume and helps identify oligohydramnios or polyhydramnios. Low fluid is especially important in this course because early prolonged oligohydramnios can be associated with impaired lung growth.

Deceleration patterns connect timing to mechanism

PatternTimingMost important interpretation
EarlyMirrors the contraction.Usually fetal head compression.
VariableAbrupt and variable in timing.Suggests umbilical cord compression.
LateBegins after contraction starts and recovers after it ends.Suggests uteroplacental insufficiency and fetal hypoxemia.

Late decelerations are especially important when combined with prematurity, preeclampsia, or other evidence of placental insufficiency. The tracing should not be viewed as an isolated fact; it changes how aggressively the neonatal team prepares.

Fetal lung maturity estimates respiratory readiness

Gestational age remains the strongest overall clue to respiratory readiness because lung structure and surfactant production develop over time. The course also carries forward historical fetal lung maturity indicators from Lesson 1.1. The lecithin-to-sphingomyelin, or L/S, ratio rises as surfactant phospholipid production increases. An L/S ratio around 2:1 has historically been associated with pulmonary maturity. The presence of phosphatidylglycerol, or PG, is another historical sign of more advanced surfactant maturation.

Important limit

A “mature” fetal lung profile estimates surfactant readiness. It does not prove that the entire newborn is mature, eliminate other delivery risks, or guarantee that respiratory support will not be needed.

APGAR describes adaptation; it does not drive resuscitation

APGAR is assigned at 1 and 5 minutes and can be repeated if the score remains low. The five components are Appearance, Pulse, Grimace, Activity, and Respiration. Each receives 0, 1, or 2 points. The score is useful for describing how the newborn is adapting, but breathing and heart rate determine whether immediate respiratory support is needed.

Component0 points1 point2 points

Clinical priority

Never delay needed ventilation while calculating an APGAR score. APGAR describes the newborn; it is not the resuscitation algorithm.

C3: Perform an initial neonatal assessment using physical findings, oxygenation, work of breathing, and available laboratory information.

The first assessment is a pattern, not a single number

A newborn can look stable or unstable before a complete set of laboratory values is available. The initial assessment should describe the whole infant: temperature, color, level of consciousness, tone, posture, respiratory rate and pattern, chest movement, breath sounds, heart rate, perfusion, oxygenation, and obvious congenital abnormalities.

The most useful approach is to look for clusters. A single fast respiratory rate may have several explanations. Tachypnea combined with grunting, nasal flaring, retractions, and central cyanosis is much more concerning because several findings point toward increased work of breathing and impaired gas exchange.

Recognizing respiratory distress

  • Increased work of breathing: tachypnea, grunting, nasal flaring, retractions, head bobbing, or seesaw movement.
  • Gas-exchange compromise: central cyanosis, low oxygen saturation, apnea, altered mental status, or worsening blood-gas values.
  • Systemic instability: temperature instability, poor perfusion, hypotonia, weak feeding, or abnormal glucose may accompany serious illness.

Thermoregulation is respiratory care

Cold stress increases metabolic demand and oxygen consumption. A cold newborn must use more energy to maintain body temperature. That extra demand can worsen hypoglycemia, acidosis, and respiratory distress. For this reason, warming and temperature control are part of cardiopulmonary stabilization, not a separate nursing task.

Use laboratory data to support the clinical picture

Lesson 1.2 does not expect detailed blood-gas interpretation; that comes later. Here, the key skill is recognizing that an abnormal glucose value or a worsening blood-gas trend adds evidence to what you are seeing at the bedside. Laboratory information should be interpreted alongside appearance, work of breathing, oxygenation, perfusion, temperature, and maternal history.

Avoid tunnel vision

Do not assign a diagnosis or respiratory plan from one laboratory value. A low glucose, low saturation, poor perfusion, and increased work of breathing together tell a much more meaningful story than any one result alone.

C4: Integrate maternal, fetal, and newborn findings to anticipate respiratory support needs at a high-risk delivery.

Integration begins before delivery

The highest-level skill in this lesson is to combine information rather than treating each assessment as a separate list. Start with the maternal or pregnancy risk. Add fetal evidence of compromise. Predict the newborn problem that is most likely. Prepare the delivery room for that problem. Then reassess after birth and decide whether the prediction was confirmed, contradicted, or needs to be changed.

Reasoning chain

Maternal/pregnancy risk → fetal evidence → predicted newborn problem → delivery-room preparation → newborn findings → level of support and escalation.

Representative high-risk combinations

Finding clusterWhat to anticipateWhat should already be ready
Prematurity + severe preeclampsia + recurrent late decelerationsPlacental insufficiency, fetal compromise, and immature respiratory reserve.Skilled neonatal personnel, warming, monitoring, airway and ventilation equipment, oxygen support, escalation capability.
Maternal diabetes + large fetusBirth trauma risk, hypoglycemia, delayed surfactant maturation, respiratory distress.Ventilation/oxygenation assessment, thermoregulation, monitoring for systemic instability.
Chorioamnionitis + newborn distressSerious infectious illness pattern, pneumonia/sepsis risk, temperature instability, respiratory failure.Prompt cardiopulmonary stabilization with awareness that the problem may be systemic as well as pulmonary.
Early prolonged rupture + oligohydramniosInfection plus possible pulmonary hypoplasia and severe respiratory compromise.Higher-level airway and ventilation readiness and NICU communication.
Post-term pregnancy + meconium/fetal compromiseAspiration and delivery-room respiratory risk.Appropriate neonatal airway and ventilation support ready before birth.

Preparation should scale with risk. A clearly high-risk delivery should not be approached as routine care with a plan to “see what happens.” The purpose of prenatal information is to allow the respiratory team to be ready for the next likely step before the infant needs it.

Reassess after birth

Predictions are not diagnoses. A high-risk pregnancy can still produce a stable newborn, and an infant can deteriorate despite reassuring prenatal information. After delivery, return to the infant in front of you: breathing, heart rate, oxygenation, temperature, perfusion, tone, and response to support. The prenatal history tells you what to watch for; the newborn assessment tells you what to do now.
