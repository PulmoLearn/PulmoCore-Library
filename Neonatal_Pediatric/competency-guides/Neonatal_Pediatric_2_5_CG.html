C1: Recognize mechanisms of respiratory compromise caused by asthma, pneumonia, croup, sleep-disordered breathing, and other pulmonary or airway diseases.

Start with the mechanism before naming the disease

Many pediatric respiratory diseases produce overlapping symptoms such as cough, increased work of breathing, hypoxemia, or noisy breathing. A safer way to organize the differential is to first identify the physiologic category: upper-airway obstruction, lower-airway obstruction, small-airway disease, parenchymal/infectious disease, chronic secretion-retention disease, structural airway disease, aspiration-related injury, or sleep-related obstruction.

Once the mechanism is identified, use age, history, onset, breath sounds, secretion pattern, gas exchange, imaging, and treatment response to narrow the diagnosis.

Disease / patternPrimary mechanismHigh-yield clues
AsthmaLower-airway obstruction from inflammation, bronchospasm, mucosal edema, and mucusEpisodic wheeze/triggers, prolonged exhalation, air trapping; severe disease may progress to rising CO₂
CroupUpper-airway obstructionBarking cough, hoarseness, inspiratory stridor
PneumoniaInfectious/inflammatory parenchymal disease causing alveolar filling and V/Q mismatchFever, crackles, hypoxemia, focal or diffuse imaging changes
BronchiolitisInfant small-airway edema, mucus, and debris with uneven obstructionViral prodrome, wheeze/crackles, feeding difficulty, air trapping, atelectasis, possible apnea/fatigue

Severe asthma can become quieter as it becomes more dangerous

Wheezing requires airflow. If obstruction becomes extreme, air movement may fall so much that wheezing becomes faint. A quieter chest with worsening air movement, drowsiness, and rising CO₂ is a sign of deterioration, not improvement.

Upper-airway noise and lower-airway noise are not interchangeable

Inspiratory stridor directs attention toward the upper airway. Expiratory wheeze and prolonged exhalation point more toward lower-airway obstruction. The distinction matters because the mechanisms and treatment priorities are different.

Clinical reasoning: Before naming a treatment, say what is failing: upper airway, lower airway, small airways, parenchyma, secretion clearance, or sleep-related patency.

C2: Differentiate obstructive, infectious, secretion-related, and chronic pulmonary disease using clinical and diagnostic data.

Use the same comparison sequence for every disease

Mechanism → age/history → bedside pattern → diagnostics → therapy → response/failure. This sequence prevents overreliance on one clue. A child can wheeze for more than one reason, have a positive culture without active infection, or have an imaging abnormality that does not fully explain current severity.

Age and onset matter

An infant with a viral prodrome and diffuse small-airway findings fits bronchiolitis better than an older child with episodic trigger-associated wheeze. A sudden choking event points toward foreign material or aspiration. A long history of daily wet cough and recurrent infections suggests a chronic secretion-clearance disorder rather than isolated asthma.

Response to treatment is diagnostic information too

Improved airflow after bronchodilation supports reversible lower-airway obstruction. Improvement after aspiration risk is addressed supports recurrent injury from feeding dysfunction. Persistent hypoxemia or fatigue despite supportive care may indicate progression or a different diagnosis.

Clinical reasoning: Avoid “one finding = one disease.” Build the diagnosis from the whole pattern and then ask whether the treatment response supports your reasoning.

C3: Select airway-clearance techniques for disorders such as cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis.

Airway clearance should solve a secretion problem

Airway-clearance therapy should not be prescribed simply because a child has a familiar disease label. It is most appropriate when secretions are retained, the cough is ineffective, or the disease mechanism supports assisted secretion clearance.

Cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis create a chronic clearance burden

In CF and bronchiectasis, thick or infected secretions can remain in the airway, contributing to obstruction, inflammation, recurrent infection, and further structural damage. Regular clearance strategies are therefore matched to the child’s secretion pattern, ability to cooperate, tolerance, and cough effectiveness.

StrategyConceptual role
PEPUses expiratory resistance to support airway patency and secretion movement
Oscillatory PEPAdds vibration to expiratory resistance to support mobilization
HFCWOMobilizes secretions over repeated chest-wall oscillation
Exercise / directed coughSupports independent secretion movement and removal when the child can participate
Cough augmentation / suctionAdds removal support when cough is too weak to clear mobilized secretions

Moving mucus is not the same as removing mucus

A child may mobilize large amounts of mucus during HFCWO or PEP but still be unable to expel it. This is especially important when neuromuscular weakness reduces cough force. In that situation, cough augmentation or suction may be required.

Reassessment tells you whether the technique is useful

  • Was sputum mobilized or removed?
  • Did breath sounds improve?
  • Did work of breathing change?
  • Was oxygenation stable?
  • Did the child tolerate the technique?
  • Did symptoms improve enough to justify continuing the plan?
Clinical reasoning: The best clearance device is the one that solves the child’s actual problem. If cough is weak, adding more mobilization alone may leave secretions in the central airway.
Quiz-connected review
Be ready to choose PEP, oscillatory PEP, HFCWO, exercise/directed cough, cough augmentation, or suction according to secretion burden and cough effectiveness.
C4: Anticipate medication management for asthma and cystic fibrosis and evaluate response.

Every respiratory medication should have a target

For every medication, ask four questions: What physiologic problem is the medication supposed to change? What finding should improve? What adverse effect should be monitored? What would indicate treatment failure or disease progression?

Therapy categoryTargetResponse / monitoring
Asthma bronchodilatorReversible bronchospasmAir entry, WOB, speaking/participation, peak flow/FEV₁ when feasible; monitor tachycardia, tremor, agitation
Systemic corticosteroidAirway inflammationJudge over the expected clinical course rather than expecting immediate bronchodilation
CF mucus-modifying inhaled therapyThick/dehydrated secretionsSecretion mobilization, cough/sputum trend, tolerance, and whether mucus can be removed
CF inhaled antimicrobial therapyDisease-specific airway infection managementTechnique, airway tolerance, organism/history, symptoms, exacerbation trajectory

Asthma response must include airflow, not just sound

Improved air entry and reduced work of breathing are more meaningful than simply hearing less wheeze. If the child is becoming drowsy and ETCO₂ is rising while the chest becomes quieter, airflow may be critically reduced.

CF treatment is judged by the intended target

For CF, treatment response may be reflected in sputum mobilization, cough pattern, spirometry, oxygenation, exercise tolerance, organism history, adverse effects, and the overall exacerbation trajectory. No single measure should replace the full clinical picture.

Clinical reasoning: A medication can be delivered correctly and still fail because the disease is progressing. It can also appear to fail when the wrong response is being measured.

C5: Obtain or interpret respiratory specimen, microbiology, sweat-test, hematologic, and imaging information when appropriate.

A diagnostic test should answer a clinical question

Tests are most useful when the team knows what question they are trying to answer. Is the goal diagnosis, organism identification, severity assessment, recognition of a complication, or monitoring treatment response?

Diagnostic informationWhat it contributes
Respiratory specimen / microbiologyHelps identify organisms when infection or chronic colonization is clinically relevant; interpret specimen quality and symptoms
Sweat testingSupports CF diagnosis when history and phenotype raise concern; not an acute severity test
Hematologic informationCBC trends may support infection/inflammation; hemoglobin and sickle-cell context affect respiratory interpretation
ImagingHelps characterize focal pneumonia, diffuse disease, aspiration injury, bronchiectasis/structural disease, and new infiltrates

Specimen quality changes how much confidence you place in a culture

A positive respiratory culture does not automatically mean active infection requiring treatment. The result should be interpreted with symptoms, specimen quality, chronic colonization history, and the reason the specimen was collected.

Sweat testing answers a diagnostic question, not an exacerbation-severity question

When a child has a phenotype concerning for cystic fibrosis—such as chronic wet cough, recurrent infection, poor growth, or bronchiectasis—sweat testing supports the diagnostic evaluation. It does not tell you how severe today’s pulmonary exacerbation is.

Hematologic context can change respiratory interpretation

CBC trends can support infection or inflammation assessment. Hemoglobin matters when evaluating oxygen delivery. In sickle cell disease, a new pulmonary infiltrate with fever or respiratory symptoms should raise concern for acute chest syndrome and the possibility of rapid deterioration.

Clinical reasoning: A test result is not a diagnosis by itself. Ask what the test was meant to answer and whether the result fits the child’s symptoms, specimen quality, imaging, and clinical trajectory.

Be ready to explain when microbiology, sweat testing, hematologic information, and imaging are useful and why none should be interpreted without clinical context.
C6: Recognize respiratory complications related to aspiration, feeding, obesity, and sleep-disordered breathing.

Not every respiratory problem begins in the lungs

Feeding dysfunction, aspiration, obesity, and upper-airway obstruction during sleep can all produce significant respiratory disease. These problems may be missed when the assessment focuses only on wheeze, infection, or chest imaging.

Aspiration may be recurrent rather than dramatic

Aspiration does not always present as a single witnessed choking event. Clues can include coughing, choking, desaturation, or a wet-sounding voice during feeds, recurrent pneumonia, poor growth, or otherwise unexplained bronchiectasis.

Possible evaluationClinical question
Feeding observationDo respiratory symptoms occur during feeding?
Swallow evaluationIs swallowing dysfunction contributing to aspiration risk?
BronchoscopyIs there airway injury, secretion burden, or another direct airway finding?
pH-impedance testingCould reflux-related events be contributing when clinically indicated?
ImagingIs there recurrent dependent injury or another pulmonary complication?

Obesity changes respiratory reserve but does not explain every symptom

Obesity can increase upper-airway collapsibility, increase work of breathing, worsen sleep-disordered breathing, reduce respiratory reserve, and complicate gas exchange during acute illness. However, dyspnea or hypoxemia should not simply be attributed to body habitus. Evaluate for OSA, hypoventilation, asthma, deconditioning, and other cardiopulmonary disease using the full clinical picture.

Recognize the OSA pattern

  • Snoring
  • Witnessed breathing pauses or gasping
  • Restless or fragmented sleep
  • Enuresis
  • Morning headache
  • Behavior change or poor school performance

Polysomnography is the diagnostic study emphasized in this lesson for suspected obstructive sleep apnea.

Clinical reasoning: Recurrent pneumonia plus coughing or desaturation during feeds should shift the differential toward aspiration. Snoring plus witnessed pauses and morning symptoms should shift it toward sleep-disordered breathing.
