C1: Interpret oxygenation index or oxygen saturation index, mean airway pressure, ventilation, pulmonary mechanics, waveforms, and trends during severe neonatal respiratory failure.

Severe respiratory failure is a trend problem, not a single-number problem

When a newborn is critically ill, the question is not simply, “What is the oxygen saturation?” The larger question is, how much support is required to produce that oxygenation, and is the overall cardiopulmonary condition improving or worsening? Two infants can have the same saturation while needing very different amounts of oxygen and airway pressure.

That is why advanced monitoring combines oxygenation, ventilation, pressure, lung volume, perfusion, imaging, mechanics, and the direction of change over time.

Oxygenation Index: how hard are we working to oxygenate the infant?

OI = (FiO₂ × Mean Airway Pressure × 100) ÷ PaO₂

The oxygenation index, or OI, relates the amount of inspired oxygen and mean airway pressure being used to the arterial oxygen level achieved. A rising OI means the infant is requiring a greater oxygen-and-pressure burden to achieve oxygenation. It is therefore most useful as a trend rather than as an isolated number.

OI can worsen because FiO₂ rises, mean airway pressure rises, PaO₂ falls, or more than one of these happens together. Before responding by simply adding more support, reassess whether the lungs are under-recruited, overdistended, affected by air leak, or whether perfusion and cardiovascular function are deteriorating.

OSI: a noninvasive companion when PaO₂ is unavailable

OSI = (FiO₂ × Mean Airway Pressure × 100) ÷ SpO₂

The oxygen saturation index, or OSI, uses pulse oximetry instead of PaO₂. It can provide a noninvasive oxygenation-burden trend when an arterial PaO₂ is not available. It should be interpreted cautiously when SpO₂ is very high because saturation can change very little even when PaO₂ changes substantially.

Mean airway pressure is not the same thing as PEEP

Mean airway pressure, or MAP, is the average pressure applied to the airway throughout the respiratory cycle. In high-frequency ventilation, it strongly influences lung volume, recruitment, and oxygenation. It reflects more than the baseline pressure alone and should not be treated as simply another name for PEEP.

Too little recruitmentLung units remain collapsed, oxygenation worsens, and more FiO₂ may be required.
Appropriate recruitmentEnough lung is open to support gas exchange without excessive distention.
OverdistentionExcess pressure can over-expand lung, impair venous return, and worsen air leak or hemodynamics.

Integrate oxygenation with ventilation and perfusion

A “better” oxygen number does not prove the infant is improving if the PaCO₂ is rising, pH is worsening, blood pressure is falling, lactate is increasing, or the lungs are becoming overdistended. Advanced interpretation therefore combines OI or OSI with PaCO₂ and pH, blood pressure, lactate and perfusion, imaging, chest expansion, lung volume, compliance, resistance, and device performance.

Waveforms and synchrony are clinical clues

Unexpected waveform or interaction changes may signal gas trapping, poor exhalation, airway obstruction, secretion burden, changing resistance or compliance, or problems with the support device itself. A rising support requirement should always trigger reassessment of the airway, lungs, circulation, and equipment before the next ventilator change is made.

Clinical reasoning: A rising OI is not an instruction to automatically raise pressure. It is a warning that the oxygenation burden is worsening and the cause must be identified.

C2: Apply advanced ventilation and adjunctive strategies when conventional support is inadequate.

Why move beyond conventional ventilation?

Conventional ventilation may become inadequate when the infant cannot maintain gas exchange without pressures or volumes that risk further lung injury. Advanced high-frequency strategies can provide ventilation while changing the way pressure and volume are delivered to the lung.

The decision to escalate should not be based on the name of the disease alone. Look at the actual problem: severe oxygenation failure, inadequate ventilation, persistent air leak, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, or a support burden that is becoming increasingly injurious.

HFOV: very small oscillatory volumes around a maintained lung volume

High-frequency oscillatory ventilation, or HFOV, delivers very small oscillatory volumes around a relatively constant mean airway pressure. The broad concept is to separate lung recruitment and oxygenation from the rapid oscillatory movement used for carbon dioxide clearance.

HFOV controlPrimary meaning
Mean airway pressureMainly influences lung volume, recruitment, and oxygenation.
AmplitudeInfluences oscillatory movement and CO₂ clearance.
FrequencyAlso influences CO₂ clearance and must be interpreted with the overall ventilatory response.

HFJV: rapid jet pulses with passive exhalation

High-frequency jet ventilation, or HFJV, delivers rapid high-velocity pulses while exhalation remains passive. Jet PIP, rate, inspiratory time, and background PEEP contribute to ventilation and lung volume. Because exhalation is passive, adequate time for gas to leave the lung is especially important when air trapping is a concern.

FeatureHFOVHFJV
Basic patternSmall oscillations around maintained MAPRapid high-velocity pulses with passive exhalation
Oxygenation focusStrongly linked to MAP and recruitmentInfluenced by lung volume/background support and overall strategy
Ventilation focusAmplitude/frequency and resulting CO₂ trendJet PIP, rate, inspiratory time, and passive exhalation

Air leak and PIE change the goal

When pulmonary interstitial emphysema or another air-leak problem develops, simply increasing conventional inspiratory pressure can worsen the injury. The strategy shifts toward reducing unnecessary distending pressure and tidal volume, allowing adequate exhalation, confirming ETT position and patency, clearing secretions, and reassessing whether unilateral disease or trapped gas is present.

A high-frequency strategy may be considered when it can maintain gas exchange while reducing the conventional pressure and volume burden. The exact mode is less important than matching the strategy to the infant’s physiology and reassessing the response after every major change.

Clinical reasoning: Advanced ventilation is not “more aggressive ventilation.” It is a different strategy used when conventional support is inadequate or becoming too injurious.

C3: Prevent and recognize ventilator-associated lung injury, air leak, and other complications of advanced respiratory support.

Respiratory support can save the lung and injure the lung

Neonatal respiratory support must be strong enough to maintain gas exchange but gentle enough to limit treatment-related injury. This balance is especially important in extremely premature infants whose lungs, blood vessels, brain, eyes, and gastrointestinal system are all vulnerable to rapid physiologic changes.

Three familiar lung-injury mechanisms still matter

InjuryMechanismPrevention direction
VolutraumaExcessive tidal volume and alveolar stretchAvoid unnecessary VT and reassess support as mechanics improve
BarotraumaExcessive pressure and air-leak injuryUse the least pressure needed for effective support
AtelectraumaRepeated opening and collapse of unstable lung unitsMaintain appropriate recruitment without overdistention

Air leak is often a sudden deterioration problem

Pneumothorax and pulmonary interstitial emphysema are important air-leak syndromes. Sudden desaturation, bradycardia, hypotension, asymmetric breath sounds or chest movement, or an abrupt increase in support need should be treated as an emergency pattern rather than as routine progression of lung disease.

In a deteriorating ventilated infant, immediate threats such as airway displacement, airway obstruction, and pneumothorax remain high priorities. Increasing pressure before checking these possibilities can worsen the situation.

Advanced respiratory care is multisystem care

BPDRisk reflects extreme prematurity plus inflammation, oxygen exposure, pressure/volume injury, infection, and prolonged support.
ROPRisk increases with prematurity and excessive or unstable oxygen exposure.
IVHAbrupt swings in hemodynamics, oxygenation, or CO₂ can worsen risk in a vulnerable premature infant.
NECMay present with abdominal distention, apnea, worsening acidosis, and increasing ventilation difficulty.

Not every increase in support is worsening lung disease

Ventilator-associated infection, accidental extubation, retained secretions, skin injury, circuit breaks, and equipment problems can all increase oxygen and ventilation needs. Deterioration troubleshooting should therefore include the patient, airway, circuit, equipment, and multisystem condition.

Clinical reasoning: Prevention is cumulative. There is no single “BPD prevention setting.” The goal is the least injurious effective respiratory support over time while avoiding unnecessary oxygen, pressure, volume, instability, and prolonged invasive support.
Quiz-connected review
Be ready to connect sudden deterioration with air-leak or airway emergencies and to explain how respiratory support can contribute to BPD, ROP, IVH, NEC, infection, and other treatment-related complications.
C4: Assess readiness for ventilator liberation and extubation and recognize failure of liberation.

Extubation readiness is a bundle, not a ventilator number

Low ventilator settings are encouraging, but they do not prove that a neonate is ready to maintain breathing after the artificial airway is removed. Successful liberation depends on respiratory drive, gas exchange, lung mechanics, secretion management, airway stability, overall physiologic stability, and the plan for support after extubation.

Six readiness domains

1. Respiratory driveSpontaneous effort is reliable and apnea burden is manageable; consider sedation and caffeine therapy.
2. Gas exchangeOxygen need and blood-gas trend are stable on modest support.
3. Mechanics / secretionsCompliance is improving, secretions are manageable, and no major active air leak is present.
4. AirwayAirway factors support safe removal of the ETT, and no near-term procedure clearly requires the artificial airway.
5. Systemic stabilityPerfusion, temperature, hemoglobin/electrolytes, and neurologic status are sufficiently stable.
6. Postextubation planCPAP, NIPPV, HFNC/oxygen, caffeine, positioning, suction, monitoring, and escalation criteria are planned before extubation.

Why respiratory drive can override “good settings”

An infant may have stable gases and low ventilator settings but still have frequent apnea or weak spontaneous effort. Recent sedation can temporarily depress respiratory drive. A premature infant may also need caffeine support. If the infant cannot reliably initiate and sustain breathing, low settings do not make extubation safe.

Recognize liberation failure early

After extubation, increasing apnea, work of breathing, FiO₂ requirement, hypercapnia, acidemia, recurrent bradycardia, fatigue, or inability to maintain airway and gas exchange suggest that the current level of support is failing. These findings should prompt timely escalation rather than prolonged waiting for the infant to “declare” failure.

Clinical reasoning: Successful liberation is planned before the ETT is removed. The postextubation mode and failure criteria should already be clear.

C5: Plan ongoing monitoring, respiratory technology, and discharge needs for infants with chronic respiratory disease.

Chronic respiratory care extends far beyond the ventilator

An infant with BPD or another chronic respiratory condition may remain medically fragile even after the acute respiratory crisis has passed. Long-term planning must address oxygenation, work of breathing, growth, feeding, aspiration risk, pulmonary hypertension, equipment needs, caregiver skills, emergency readiness, and specialty follow-up.

A clinically stable infant is not automatically ready for discharge. The home system must also be safe, reliable, and sustainable.

What should continue to be monitored?

  • Oxygen requirement and ordered saturation goals
  • Work of breathing and respiratory exacerbations
  • Growth and nutrition
  • Feeding tolerance and aspiration risk
  • Pulmonary hypertension risk and surveillance
  • Developmental progress and specialty follow-up

Match respiratory technology to the infant

NeedPossible home respiratory support
Supplemental oxygenHome oxygen system with ordered saturation goals and monitoring plan
MonitoringPulse oximetry and appropriate alarm response plan

Teaching is not the same as competency

Caregivers must be able to demonstrate the skills required to safely care for the infant at home. Verbalizing a procedure is not enough. Return demonstration may include airway and emergency skills, suction, equipment setup and troubleshooting, alarm response, medication delivery, emergency ventilation or CPR, and knowing when to seek help.

Discharge readiness is a system check

Infant stabilityRespiratory plan is stable enough for home care.
Equipment readinessEquipment has been delivered, functions correctly, and backup systems are available.
Caregiver competencyCaregivers can demonstrate routine and emergency respiratory care skills.
Follow-up planEmergency contacts, medications, appointments, pulmonary hypertension surveillance, developmental care, nutrition, hearing, vision, and immunization plans are coordinated.

BPD care is multidisciplinary

Chronic lung disease affects more than oxygenation. Growth may be difficult because breathing itself consumes energy. Feeding may increase aspiration risk. Pulmonary hypertension may develop. Developmental follow-up, immunization, hearing and vision care, nutrition, and specialty appointments all become part of the respiratory discharge plan.

Clinical reasoning: A stable infant with unprepared caregivers or missing backup equipment is not discharge-ready. Physiologic stability and home-system readiness are both required.
