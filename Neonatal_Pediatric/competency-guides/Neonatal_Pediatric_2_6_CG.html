C1: Integrate cardiovascular, neurologic, musculoskeletal, renal or metabolic, and hematologic findings into the respiratory assessment.

Respiratory status depends on more than the lungs

A child can have a normal oxygen saturation and still have inadequate tissue oxygen delivery. SpO₂ describes the percentage of available hemoglobin binding sites occupied by oxygen; it does not tell you how much hemoglobin is present or how much blood the heart is delivering to the tissues.

This is why respiratory assessment in critically ill children must include perfusion, neurologic status, respiratory muscle performance, renal/metabolic data, and hematologic information rather than treating oxygen saturation as a stand-alone endpoint.

Think in paired data:
SpO₂ + perfusion • ventilation + blood pressure • strength + CO₂ • hemoglobin + oxygen delivery • medication change + respiratory drive

Normal SpO₂ can coexist with shock

A child with SpO₂ 99% but cool extremities, weak pulses, delayed refill, low urine output, and rising lactate may still have inadequate oxygen delivery because cardiac output is poor. Increasing oxygen concentration alone does not correct a circulation problem.

Normal SpO₂ can also coexist with anemia

If less hemoglobin is available to carry oxygen, oxygen content can fall even when the remaining hemoglobin is highly saturated. The respiratory therapist should therefore consider hemoglobin and perfusion when the clinical picture suggests inadequate oxygen delivery.

Clinical reasoning: Ask: Is this primarily a lung problem, a pump/perfusion problem, a drive problem, a muscle problem, or a mixed problem?

C2: Recognize respiratory compromise caused by congenital heart disease, pulmonary hypertension, neurologic injury, neuromuscular weakness, and thoracic injury.

Different mechanisms can create the same respiratory symptom

Hypoxemia, tachypnea, fatigue, or poor gas exchange can originate from the lungs, the heart, the brain, the respiratory muscles, or mechanical injury to the chest. Treatment is safest when the mechanism is identified before support is intensified.

Congenital heart disease changes how oxygen and ventilation affect circulation

Congenital heart disease may involve excessive pulmonary blood flow, reduced pulmonary blood flow, ductal-dependent systemic blood flow, mixing dependence, or single-ventricle physiology. Respiratory support changes pulmonary vascular tone and therefore can change how blood is distributed between the lungs and the systemic circulation.

Pulmonary hypertension is a cardiopulmonary problem

Elevated pulmonary vascular resistance can reduce pulmonary blood flow, increase right-heart workload, and worsen hypoxemia. The therapist should assess oxygenation, ventilation, acid-base status, perfusion, and hemodynamic response together instead of treating pulmonary hypertension as an oxygen-saturation problem alone.

Neurologic injury changes drive and airway protection

Brain injury can reduce respiratory drive, weaken protective reflexes, increase aspiration risk, and create a delicate relationship between ventilation and cerebral blood flow. A neurologically injured child may therefore need respiratory support even when primary lung mechanics are not the main problem.

Neuromuscular weakness can fail silently at first

A child with progressive neuromuscular weakness may maintain normal oxygen saturation while tidal ventilation becomes less effective and carbon dioxide rises. Weak cough and bulbar dysfunction can create a second danger: retained secretions and loss of airway protection.

C3: Evaluate respiratory muscle strength and recognize impending ventilatory failure in neuromuscular disease.

Neuromuscular failure is a trend-recognition problem

Children with neuromuscular disease may deteriorate gradually. Oxygen saturation can remain normal while respiratory muscle strength, tidal ventilation, and cough effectiveness worsen. Serial measures are therefore more useful than a single reassuring number.

MeasureWhat it tells you
Vital capacityGlobal respiratory reserve; serial decline is more important than one isolated value
Maximal inspiratory pressureInspiratory muscle strength
Cough strength / peak cough flowAbility to clear secretions effectively
ETCO₂ / blood gasReveals evolving hypoventilation before SpO₂ necessarily falls
Bulbar functionSwallowing, gag, secretion handling, aspiration risk, and airway-protection ability

The dangerous pattern is a combination

Declining strength/reserve + rising CO₂ + weak cough or bulbar dysfunction + increasing fatigue = impending ventilatory and airway-protection failure

Waiting for cyanosis or a low SpO₂ can delay intervention. The child should be evaluated for airway and ventilatory support while the team still has time to prepare deliberately.

Bulbar weakness changes the risk

Difficulty swallowing, a weak gag, secretion retention, or aspiration risk means the problem is no longer ventilation alone. Airway protection may be failing even before oxygenation becomes abnormal.

Clinical reasoning: Normal saturation is not a reason to wait when VC, inspiratory strength, cough, bulbar function, and CO₂ are all worsening.

Be ready to use serial VC, MIP, cough strength, CO₂, bulbar function, and fatigue together to recognize impending neuromuscular respiratory failure.
C4: Anticipate the respiratory consequences and interactions of sedatives, analgesics, vasoactive agents, and other critical-care medications.

Medication effects become part of the respiratory assessment

In critical care, a medication may improve one problem while creating a new respiratory concern. The important skill is not memorizing doses here; it is recognizing how a medication can change drive, airway protection, ventilator interaction, perfusion, and oxygen delivery.

Medication categoryPotential benefitRespiratory consequence to reassess
SedativesReduce agitation and may improve toleranceReduced drive, mental status, airway tone, spontaneous ventilator interaction
AnalgesicsReduce pain-related splinting and may improve ventilationExcessive CNS/respiratory depression, hypoventilation, loss of airway protection
Vasoactive agentsSupport vascular tone, cardiac output, or blood pressureInterpret respiratory response with perfusion, lactate, urine output, and hemodynamics
Neuromuscular blockadeMay facilitate ventilation in selected situationsRemoves spontaneous effort and masks examination findings; requires complete ventilatory support and objective monitoring

Analgesia can improve breathing—but still requires surveillance

A child with rib or chest-wall pain may splint, breathe shallowly, and avoid coughing. Effective analgesia can improve chest movement. But if the child becomes somnolent and CO₂ rises, the medication effect must be reassessed because excessive depression may now be worsening ventilation.

After every medication change, ask five questions

DriveDid spontaneous breathing change?
AirwayCan the child still protect the airway?
InteractionDid synchrony with support improve or worsen?
PerfusionDid BP, pulses, lactate, or urine output change?
Gas exchangeDid oxygenation or CO₂ change?
Clinical reasoning: A medication change is a physiologic intervention. Reassess what changed in drive, airway protection, gas exchange, ventilator interaction, and perfusion.

C5: Modify ventilation and oxygenation to support the lungs while protecting cerebral and cardiovascular function.

Respiratory support can help one organ system while stressing another

Mechanical ventilation changes intrathoracic pressure, carbon dioxide, oxygenation, and circulation. The safest respiratory plan therefore protects the lungs while also protecting cerebral perfusion and cardiovascular function.

Brain injury: avoid both hypoxemia and hypotension

Secondary brain injury can worsen when oxygenation or perfusion falls. A ventilator strategy that improves one number but lowers blood pressure may still threaten cerebral oxygen delivery.

Do not use routine aggressive hyperventilation after traumatic brain injury

Lowering PaCO₂ excessively can reduce cerebral blood flow. The lesson therefore avoids routine aggressive hyperventilation after TBI. Brief controlled hyperventilation is reserved for specific acute herniation situations within the course framework.

Positive pressure is also a cardiovascular intervention

Higher intrathoracic pressure can reduce venous return and cardiac output. This becomes especially important in shock or preload-dependent physiology. If blood pressure and urine output fall after an airway-pressure increase, reconsider whether the respiratory change is compromising circulation.

Congenital heart physiology may require different respiratory targets

In single-ventricle or mixing-dependent circulation, changes in oxygen and ventilation can alter pulmonary/systemic blood-flow balance. More oxygen or more ventilation is not automatically better if it shifts blood flow away from where it is needed.

Mechanical trauma requires correction of the mechanical problem

A tension pneumothorax is a classic example. Increasing ventilator pressure does not fix trapped pleural pressure and may worsen the situation. The mechanical cause requires immediate decompression.

Clinical reasoning: After every major respiratory change, reassess oxygenation, ventilation, blood pressure/perfusion, neurologic status, and the mechanism you were trying to treat.
Quiz-connected review
Be ready to explain why hypoxemia, hypotension, PaCO₂, airway pressure, congenital heart physiology, and thoracic trauma must be considered together when modifying respiratory support.
C6: Differentiate venovenous and venoarterial extracorporeal support and apply respiratory management principles during extracorporeal life support.

ECMO is chosen by the type of organ support needed

The core distinction is simple: does the child primarily need extracorporeal gas-exchange support, or does the child also need major circulatory support?

SupportPrimary functionNative heart role
VV ECMORespiratory / gas-exchange supportNative cardiac output still provides systemic circulation
VA ECMOCardiac plus respiratory supportExtracorporeal circuit provides major circulatory support in addition to gas exchange

Severe respiratory failure with preserved cardiac function points toward VV physiology

If the primary problem is refractory gas exchange but cardiac output is adequate, the support need is respiratory rather than circulatory. If the child also has major cardiac failure or cardiogenic shock, VA physiology is more appropriate.

The lungs and airway still need care after cannulation

  • Continue lung-protective ventilation.
  • Maintain humidification and secretion management.
  • Maintain airway security and infection prevention.
  • Continue respiratory assessment even when extracorporeal gas exchange is carrying much of the workload.

Interpret the patient and circuit together

Important complications include bleeding, thrombosis, hemolysis, differential hypoxemia, and circuit failure. A change in oxygenation or perfusion may arise from the child, the lungs, or the extracorporeal circuit.

Clinical reasoning: ECMO does not eliminate the respiratory therapist’s role. It changes the goal of conventional support from “do all the gas exchange” to “protect the lungs while the circuit helps.”

Be ready to distinguish VV from VA ECMO by support need and to identify the respiratory-care responsibilities that continue during extracorporeal life support.
C7: Communicate deterioration and recommended care-plan changes with the interdisciplinary team.

Communication should convert assessment into action

Saying “the patient is worse” is not enough during a rapidly changing pediatric emergency. High-quality escalation communication should summarize what changed, provide objective evidence, name the likely mechanism, state what has already been done, describe the response, and make a specific recommendation.

What changed → objective evidence → likely mechanism → actions taken → response → specific recommendation

Mechanism-based recommendations are stronger than vague alerts

For example, sudden unilateral breath-sound loss with hypotension and worsening oxygenation after trauma should be communicated as suspected tension pneumothorax physiology with a recommendation for immediate decompression rather than merely reporting “low saturation.”

Trend communication is especially important in neuromuscular decline

For a child with progressive neuromuscular weakness, communicate the serial decline in VC or inspiratory strength, rising CO₂, worsening cough, bulbar changes, fatigue, and what you recommend for airway or ventilatory escalation. Waiting until saturation falls can lose valuable preparation time.

Transport readiness is part of communication and safety

Before transport, verify airway security, ventilator and chest-drain function, oxygen/gas reserve, battery reserve, perfusion, temperature, line security, emergency roles, and disease-specific risks. Unresolved instability should be addressed before transport begins.

Weak communicationStronger communication
“The patient looks worse.”“VC has fallen serially, cough is weak, dysphagia is new, and ETCO₂ is rising; this suggests impending neuromuscular ventilatory and airway-protection failure. Recommend preparing escalation now.”
“The saturation dropped.”“After chest trauma, the child has new unilateral breath-sound loss, hypotension, and worsening hypoxemia; tension pneumothorax is suspected. Recommend immediate decompression.”
Clinical reasoning: The RT’s role is not only to recognize deterioration. It is to translate the pattern into a clear recommendation that helps the team act before the child decompensates further.
