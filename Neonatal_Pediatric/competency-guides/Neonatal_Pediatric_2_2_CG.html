C1: Select and troubleshoot age-appropriate oxygen administration and aerosol delivery devices.

Start with the child’s need, not the device name

Oxygen therapy and aerosol therapy are both delivery problems. The clinician must first identify what needs to be delivered, then choose a system that can deliver it reliably to this child. Age, size, cooperation, inspiratory demand, airway anatomy, comfort, humidification needs, and ventilation status all influence the best choice.

Oxygen should be treated like a medication. The goal is not “the most oxygen possible.” The goal is to provide enough oxygen to meet the ordered saturation target while recognizing when the child’s problem is no longer oxygenation alone.

Troubleshoot the whole oxygen system

If oxygen delivery seems ineffective, first reassess the child. Then verify the oxygen source and settings, tubing and circuit connections, interface position and seal, reservoir inflation when present, humidification and temperature, and the reliability of the pulse-oximeter signal. A correct device can still fail when the setup is wrong.

A simple troubleshooting sequence
Child → source/settings → tubing/connections → interface → humidification/temperature → signal quality → physiologic response

Aerosol delivery depends on both device and patient

Aerosol therapy is only useful if enough medication reaches the intended airway. The best device depends on age and cooperation, the medication and device combination, inspiratory flow, and whether the child uses a mask, mouthpiece, or ventilator circuit.

Aerosol deviceKey points
pMDI + spacer/VHCPortable and effective with correct technique; young children need a well-fitted mask and calm tidal breathing
Jet nebulizerFamiliar, but may have residual volume, longer treatment time, noise, and variable output
Vibrating meshLow residual volume and efficient output; cleaning and medication compatibility matter
Dry-powder inhalerRequires enough patient-generated inspiratory flow

When aerosol response is poor, ask why

Poor response does not automatically mean the medication failed. Check the drug/device pairing, device assembly, medication placement, power or driving-gas source, interface seal, patient technique, inspiratory flow, and device cleanliness. Only then can you separate delivery failure from true pharmacologic failure or disease progression.

Clinical reasoning: A toddler screaming around a loose pMDI mask may receive far less lower-airway medication than expected. Correct the delivery problem before concluding that the drug did not work.

C2: Select airway-clearance therapy based on secretion burden, cough effectiveness, and underlying disease.

Airway clearance has two jobs: move mucus and remove mucus

A common mistake is to treat airway-clearance techniques as interchangeable. They are not. Some methods primarily move secretions toward larger airways. Others help the child actually expel those secretions. The child’s cough strength may be just as important as the amount of mucus present.

Before choosing a therapy, assess secretion volume and thickness, cough strength, bulbar function, airway patency, oxygen reserve, pain or reflux risk, disease mechanism, and the child’s ability to cooperate.

Why cough strength can change the entire plan

A child with cystic fibrosis may have large amounts of thick mucus but enough respiratory muscle strength to cough effectively after mobilization. A child with neuromuscular weakness may have fewer secretions yet be unable to clear them because the cough is weak. In the second child, more mobilization without a removal strategy can leave mucus sitting in the central airway.

Mobilization and removal should be planned together

MobilizationMoves secretions from smaller or peripheral airways toward larger central airways.
RemovalUses an effective cough, assisted cough, MI-E, or suction when necessary to get secretions out.
Clinical reasoning: If HFCWO mobilizes secretions but the child has a weak cough, the treatment is incomplete until a removal strategy is added.

C3: Anticipate therapeutic effects and adverse effects of aerosolized and disease-specific respiratory medications.

Delivery technique and medication effect are two different questions

Aerosol therapy has two separate success points. First, the medication must be delivered correctly. Second, the medication must produce the intended physiologic effect. A perfectly assembled nebulizer cannot make the wrong medication solve the wrong problem, and an effective medication cannot help if little of it reaches the airway.

Medication / therapy categoryExpected therapeutic effectReassessment / adverse-effect thinking
Inhaled bronchodilatorReduce reversible bronchial smooth-muscle constriction and improve airflowReassess air movement, wheeze, WOB, peak flow when feasible, heart rate, tremor, and overall response
Anti-inflammatory therapyReduce airway inflammationDo not expect the same immediate effect as bronchodilation; monitor medication-specific effects over the expected course
CF mucus-modifying inhaled therapySupport management of dehydrated or thick secretions as part of airway clearanceMonitor mobilization, cough, bronchospasm/tolerance, and whether secretions can actually be removed
Inhaled antimicrobial therapyDeliver disease-specific antimicrobial therapy to the respiratory tractMonitor technique, airway tolerance, and the broader infection response; does not replace cultures, systemic assessment, or clearance

Bronchodilator response is more than “less wheeze”

After a bronchodilator, look for improved air movement and reduced work of breathing. Wheeze may change, but it should be interpreted with overall airflow. Peak flow may be useful when the child is cooperative enough to perform it. Also monitor adverse effects such as increased heart rate or tremor.

Not every medication should work immediately

Anti-inflammatory therapy targets airway inflammation, not immediate bronchial smooth-muscle relaxation. If a student expects the same rapid response as a bronchodilator, an appropriate delayed effect may be misclassified as treatment failure.

More mobile mucus can be a sign of therapeutic effect

With CF mucus-modifying therapy, increased secretion mobilization may be expected. The next question is whether the child can remove those secretions. A medication can be doing its intended job while the airway-clearance plan still needs adjustment.

When the expected response does not occur

Poor deliveryWrong device pairing, bad seal, poor technique, inadequate inspiratory flow, assembly or cleaning problem
Inadequate medication responseDrug was delivered but did not produce enough physiologic benefit
Disease progressionThe underlying respiratory problem is worsening and may require escalation
Clinical reasoning: Before saying “the medication did not work,” determine whether the medication reached the child correctly and whether the expected effect should have occurred yet.

C4: Select and safely administer helium-oxygen mixtures based on airway physiology.

Heliox changes gas flow resistance—it does not cure the obstruction

Heliox is a mixture of helium and oxygen. Helium is less dense than the nitrogen-rich gas it replaces. Lower gas density can reduce turbulent resistance and make breathing easier in selected children with narrowed airways.

This makes heliox a physiology-driven therapy. It is most useful when turbulent flow through a narrowed airway is contributing significantly to work of breathing and the child does not require so much oxygen that the helium fraction becomes too small to provide meaningful density benefit.

What heliox can and cannot do

Can helpReduce gas-density-related turbulent resistance and work of breathing in selected upper-airway or severe obstructive conditions.
Cannot replaceForeign-body removal, treatment of edema, bronchodilation, definitive airway stabilization, or other treatment of the underlying cause.

Why increasing oxygen requirement reduces benefit

Heliox only works as a helium-oxygen mixture. If the child requires a very high oxygen concentration, there is less room for helium in the mixture. As the helium fraction falls, the density advantage decreases. Heliox should not be chosen if maintaining the mixture would prevent the child from receiving enough oxygen.

Safe administration requires compatible equipment and ongoing reassessment

Use a compatible delivery system, verify the oxygen being delivered, and monitor the child’s work of breathing and gas exchange. Continue definitive airway or disease treatment at the same time. Heliox is a bridge—not a reason to delay escalation.

Know when the bridge is failing

  • Increasing oxygen requirement
  • Increasing fatigue
  • Rising CO₂
  • Altered mental status
  • Worsening obstruction or inability to maintain the airway
Clinical reasoning: A child with stridor and modest oxygen need may benefit from heliox while definitive treatment continues. A child with rapidly rising oxygen need, fatigue, and hypercapnia may have moved beyond heliox’s useful role.
Quiz-connected review
Be ready to explain why heliox helps selected obstructive physiology, why its benefit falls as oxygen requirement rises, and why it must never delay definitive airway care.
C5: Select, administer, and monitor inhaled nitric oxide when physiologically indicated.

iNO is a targeted pulmonary-vascular therapy

Inhaled nitric oxide, or iNO, is not simply “another oxygen therapy.” Its target is pulmonary vascular resistance. When inhaled into ventilated lung regions, nitric oxide can produce selective pulmonary vasodilation and improve blood flow through areas of lung that are receiving gas.

The key reasoning step is therefore to identify pulmonary hypertension or elevated pulmonary vascular resistance as part of the problem. Hypoxemia alone is not enough to justify the therapy.

Think physiology before therapy

Pulmonary hypertension / elevated PVR → selective pulmonary vasodilation in ventilated regions → reassess oxygenation and hemodynamic response

Available echocardiographic or hemodynamic information helps support the physiologic rationale. At the same time, optimize the underlying lung problem, lung recruitment, ventilation, oxygenation, acid-base status, and circulation. iNO does not replace these basic steps.

Administration is also a delivery-system competency

The therapist must verify the delivery system and uninterrupted gas supply. A therapy that depends on continuous delivery can become dangerous if the gas supply or device is interrupted unexpectedly.

Why iNO should be weaned deliberately

Abrupt interruption can cause rebound pulmonary hypertension and clinical deterioration. As the child improves, the therapist should follow the planned weaning process while watching oxygenation, hemodynamics, and signs that pulmonary vascular stress is returning.
