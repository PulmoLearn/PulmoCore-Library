Competency Priorities

What You Must Be Able to Apply

Recognize airway threat
Differentiate croup, epiglottitis, anaphylaxis, foreign body, and postextubation edema.
Oxygenate before and during intervention
Use positioning, two-person bag-mask ventilation, airway adjuncts, and backup devices.
Verify every airway
Use exhaled CO₂, chest movement, breath sounds, depth, and imaging rather than a single sign.
Know when NIV is failing
Escalate before fatigue, apnea, severe hypercapnia, or altered mental status makes the airway more difficult.
Airway Threat

Stridor Is a Sign—The Cause Determines the Response

ConditionTypical cluesPriority
CroupBarking cough, hoarseness, inspiratory stridor, viral prodrome.Keep calm, provide oxygen, administer prescribed therapy, and monitor for fatigue.
Epiglottitis or deep neck infectionDrooling, tripod position, muffled voice, fever, toxic appearance, refusal to lie down.Avoid agitation and repeated throat examination; prepare a controlled airway with expert support.
Foreign body aspirationSudden choking, asymmetric breath sounds, focal wheeze, abrupt cough or cyanosis.Use age-appropriate choking intervention when complete obstruction is present; prepare bronchoscopy for persistent obstruction.
AnaphylaxisRapid airway edema, wheeze, urticaria, hypotension, gastrointestinal symptoms.Immediate intramuscular epinephrine and airway/oxygenation support.
Postextubation edemaStridor after tube removal, increased effort, voice change.Provide humidified oxygen, prescribed medication, and prepare reintubation if worsening.

Open the Airway First

Positioning and Bag-Mask Technique Often Determine Success

Infants may require a small shoulder roll to align the airway because the occiput is relatively large. Older children are generally positioned in a neutral or slight sniffing position unless trauma is suspected.

One-person technique
May be adequate when seal and airway position are easy.
Two-person technique
One clinician maintains a two-handed mask seal and airway position while the second squeezes the bag.

Use only enough pressure and volume to produce visible chest rise. Excessive ventilation increases gastric distention, regurgitation, and intrathoracic pressure.

Bridge Devices

Airway Adjuncts Reduce Soft-Tissue Obstruction

AdjunctAppropriate useImportant caution
Oropharyngeal airwayUnconscious child without a gag reflex.May trigger gagging, vomiting, or laryngospasm in a responsive child.
Nasopharyngeal airwayChild with reduced tone who still has a gag reflex.Avoid or use caution with facial trauma, basilar skull injury, or severe coagulopathy.
Supraglottic airwayRescue ventilation when bag-mask ventilation is difficult or intubation is delayed or unsuccessful.Does not provide the same aspiration protection or airway access as an ETT.
Endotracheal tubeDefinitive airway for prolonged ventilation, airway protection, severe failure, or procedures.Requires skilled placement, confirmation, securement, and ongoing monitoring.

Prepare for First-Pass Success

Pediatric Intubation Requires a Complete Primary and Backup Plan

Preparation domainKey elements
PatientIndication, hemodynamics, aspiration risk, difficult-airway predictors, preoxygenation, and positioning.
EquipmentPrimary and backup ETT sizes, cuff check, laryngoscope, stylet, suction, CO₂ detector, bag-mask device, supraglottic airway, and securement.
TeamClear roles, medication plan, maximum attempt duration, backup operator, and failed-airway pathway.
Post-placementVentilator connection, exhaled CO₂, depth, breath sounds, pressure/volume response, imaging, and sedation plan.
Size formulas are estimates, not guarantees. Prepare one tube size smaller and larger than the predicted size, and confirm with resistance, leak, cuff pressure, and clinical performance.

Never Trust a Single Sign

Verify Placement Immediately and Reconfirm After Every Move

Immediate confirmation
Sustained exhaled CO₂ waveform, bilateral chest rise, improving oxygenation, and appropriate breath sounds.
Ongoing confirmation
Depth marking, tube security, ventilator volumes and pressures, chest imaging, and reassessment after movement.

Sudden low exhaled volume, absent CO₂, gastric sounds, asymmetric breath sounds, or loss of the depth mark requires immediate evaluation for dislodgement, obstruction, mainstem placement, or circuit failure.

Support Without an Artificial Airway

CPAP and Bilevel Ventilation Address Different Deficits

ModePrimary roleTypical use
CPAPContinuous distending pressure improves FRC and upper-airway patency.Obstructive sleep apnea, atelectasis, cardiogenic pulmonary edema, or hypoxemic disease with preserved ventilation.
Bilevel ventilationInspiratory pressure support above expiratory pressure assists tidal volume and reduces work of breathing.Hypercapnia, respiratory muscle weakness, severe obstructive disease, or mixed oxygenation/ventilation failure.
HFNCHeated humidified flow reduces dead space and may reduce work of breathing.Mild-to-moderate hypoxemic distress when reliable ventilatory assistance is not yet required.

Interface fit, leak, synchrony, humidification, gastric distention, anxiety, and skin pressure all influence success.

Do Not Delay Intubation

NIV Failure Is a Clinical Trend, Not a Single Number

Failure domainWarning signs
VentilationRising PaCO₂, worsening acidemia, lower tidal volume, fatigue, or apnea.
OxygenationPersistent hypoxemia despite increasing FiO₂ and pressure.
NeurologicAgitation progressing to somnolence, poor airway protection, or inability to cooperate.
HemodynamicShock, arrhythmia, worsening perfusion, or pressure-related compromise.
InterfaceUncontrollable leak, severe skin injury, vomiting, aspiration risk, or inability to tolerate the mask.

Prevent Avoidable Harm

Airway and NIV Complications Are Often Mechanical

ComplicationPrevention or response
Gastric distentionUse appropriate pressure and volume, consider gastric decompression, and monitor abdominal expansion.
Skin or nasal injuryUse the correct interface, protective barriers, regular skin checks, and alternating pressure points.
Air leakAvoid excessive pressure and assess sudden asymmetry, desaturation, or hemodynamic change.
Unplanned extubationSecure the tube, document depth, manage agitation, support during movement, and reassess after procedures.
Ventilator-associated infectionHand hygiene, oral care, secretion management, minimizing circuit breaks, and early liberation when safe.

Key Takeaways

Airway emergencies demand cause-specific care
Stridor from croup, epiglottitis, foreign body, or anaphylaxis does not follow one universal pathway.
Bag-mask ventilation is a core airway skill
Position, seal, two-person technique, and gentle chest rise matter more than force.
Verification is continuous
Exhaled CO₂, depth, breath sounds, volumes, and imaging must be reassessed after every movement.
NIV should not postpone a needed airway
Worsening CO₂, acidemia, fatigue, apnea, or mental status signals failure.
