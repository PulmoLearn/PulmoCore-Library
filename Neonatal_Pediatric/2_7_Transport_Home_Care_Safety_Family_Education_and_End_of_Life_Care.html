Move Only After Stabilization

Transport Converts Small Problems Into Limited-Access Emergencies

Before departure, the child must be as stable as the clinical situation allows. Confirm that the plan can survive movement, vibration, limited access, delays, and equipment failure.

Plan for Delays

Gas and Battery Calculations Require a Safety Reserve

Never plan for the expected travel time alone. Include loading, handoff, traffic, weather, elevator delays, and emergency detours.

Approximate cylinder duration = available gas volume ÷ total flow demand

For devices that consume both continuous flow and additional breath-delivery gas, use the manufacturer’s transport consumption guidance. Battery estimates should account for device condition, heater use, alarms, and aging batteries.

Airway Emergency Readiness

Every Tracheostomy Caregiver Must Manage Obstruction and Decannulation

Possible obstruction
Assess the child, attempt suction, remove an obstructed inner cannula when applicable, provide oxygen, and replace the tube if ventilation remains ineffective.
Accidental decannulation
Call for help, position the child, insert the same-size tube or one size smaller, confirm ventilation, and use the established emergency pathway.

The airway plan must state whether the upper airway is patent, how mature the stoma is, which rescue techniques are allowed, and when emergency services should be activated.

Prevent Predictable Harm

Home Respiratory Safety Depends on Redundancy and Clear Action Thresholds

Fire safety
No smoking or open flame near oxygen, secure cylinders, and post emergency instructions.
Power failure
Charged batteries, backup device, utility notification when available, and a relocation plan.
Alarm fatigue
Use clinically appropriate limits, prevent nuisance alarms, and teach assessment before silencing.

Families need a written decision tree for low saturation, high or low ventilator pressure, power loss, suction failure, thick secretions, fever, increased work of breathing, and accidental device disconnection.

Quality of Life and Symptom Relief

Palliative Care Can Be Provided Alongside Disease-Directed Treatment

Palliative care focuses on symptom relief, communication, quality of life, family support, and alignment of treatment with goals. It does not require stopping disease-directed care.

Respiratory symptom support
Positioning, airflow, secretion management, oxygen when it relieves symptoms, noninvasive support when consistent with goals, and medication-assisted dyspnea relief.
Family-centered communication
Clarify hopes, worries, acceptable burdens, emergency preferences, and what a good day looks like for the child.
Comfort-Focused Respiratory Care

Withdrawal of Support Requires Preparation, Symptom Control, and Presence

When life-sustaining ventilation is no longer consistent with the child’s goals, the team should create a clear plan for symptom treatment before, during, and after withdrawal. The family should understand what may happen and who will remain present.

ResponsibilityRespiratory care role
PreparationConfirm goals, code status, device plan, medication readiness, family wishes, and team roles.
ComfortMinimize dyspnea, secretion distress, noise, alarms, unnecessary monitoring, and visible equipment burden.
Withdrawal processAdjust or remove respiratory support according to the agreed plan while continuously assessing comfort.
Family supportUse clear language, permit presence and rituals, respect culture, and avoid rushing the process.
After deathProvide respectful device removal when appropriate, memory-making support, and bereavement resources.

Neonatal and Pediatric Respiratory Care: Integrated Practice

Assess the whole patient
Age, anatomy, disease, perfusion, neurologic status, family context, and care setting all shape respiratory decisions.
Use the least harmful effective support
Escalate promptly when needed, but minimize pressure, oxygen, sedation, and invasive technology when lower-burden care is effective.
Prepare for transitions
Delivery room, ICU, transport, home, school, and end-of-life care each require different safety systems.
Family competency is a clinical outcome
Safe pediatric respiratory care depends on communication, return demonstration, emergency rehearsal, and shared goals.
Final exam connection: Expect integrated cases that require recognizing the patient’s stage of illness, selecting a support strategy, troubleshooting equipment, reassessing response, and adapting the plan to the setting and goals of care.
