Reversible Airflow Obstruction

Asthma Severity Is Judged by Air Movement, Work, and Response

Asthma combines airway inflammation, bronchial smooth-muscle constriction, mucosal edema, and mucus production. Severe obstruction increases expiratory time and can cause dynamic hyperinflation.

Early severe signs
Tachypnea, retractions, prolonged exhalation, wheeze, anxiety, and reduced peak flow.
Late danger signs
Poor air movement, fatigue, altered mental status, rising PaCO₂, cyanosis, or bradycardia.
Core treatment
Oxygen as needed, inhaled bronchodilator, systemic corticosteroid, hydration, and escalation for failure.

Recognize Severe Asthma Deterioration

A 7-year-old with status asthmaticus has less wheezing after repeated treatment, but air movement is now minimal. The child is drowsy and ETCO₂ is rising.

Small-Airway Viral Disease

Bronchiolitis Is Managed Primarily With Supportive Care

Bronchiolitis typically affects infants and causes edema, mucus, and cellular debris in small airways. The result is uneven obstruction, air trapping, atelectasis, increased work of breathing, and feeding difficulty.

Helpful care
Nasal suction when obstructed, hydration, oxygen for hypoxemia, and escalating respiratory support when needed.
Reassessment priorities
Apnea, feeding tolerance, hydration, work of breathing, oxygenation, CO₂ trend, and fatigue.
Do not over-treat the label. Routine bronchodilator, corticosteroid, antibiotic, or chest physiotherapy use is not appropriate for every child with bronchiolitis.
Infection and Inflammation

Pneumonia May Be Focal, Diffuse, Viral, Bacterial, or Aspiration-Related

DisorderKey clues
Community-acquired pneumoniaFever, cough, tachypnea, focal crackles, pleuritic pain, and focal or multifocal infiltrates.
Viral pneumoniaDiffuse symptoms, wheeze or crackles, bilateral interstitial pattern, and viral prodrome.
Aspiration pneumoniaFeeding or neurologic risk, dependent infiltrates, recurrent episodes, and poor airway protection.
PertussisParoxysmal cough, inspiratory whoop, post-tussive emesis, apnea, or cyanosis in infants.
TuberculosisChronic cough, exposure risk, weight loss, fever, lymphadenopathy, or persistent radiographic abnormality.

Chronic Suppurative Disease

Cystic Fibrosis Requires Daily Airway, Infection, Nutrition, and Adherence Management

Abnormal chloride transport causes dehydrated secretions, impaired mucociliary clearance, chronic infection, inflammation, bronchiectasis, pancreatic insufficiency, and high nutritional demand.

Care domainTypical priorities
Airway clearancePEP, oscillatory PEP, chest wall oscillation, exercise, directed cough, and individualized sequencing.
Inhaled therapyBronchodilator when indicated, mucus-modifying therapy, and inhaled antibiotic according to plan.
Infection managementRespiratory cultures, targeted antibiotics, and strict infection-prevention practices.
NutritionPancreatic enzyme replacement, high-calorie intake, fat-soluble vitamins, salt, and growth monitoring.
MonitoringSymptoms, sputum, weight, spirometry, exercise tolerance, oxygenation, and exacerbation frequency.

Sleep and Feeding

Sleep-Disordered Breathing and Aspiration May Present Outside the Lungs

Obstructive sleep apnea
Snoring, witnessed pauses, gasping, restless sleep, enuresis, morning headache, behavior change, or poor school performance.
Chronic aspiration
Coughing or desaturation with feeds, wet voice, recurrent pneumonia, poor growth, or unexplained bronchiectasis.

Polysomnography evaluates sleep-disordered breathing. Swallow evaluation, feeding observation, bronchoscopy, pH-impedance testing, and imaging may help define aspiration risk.

Hematologic Pulmonary Emergency

Acute Chest Syndrome Can Progress Rapidly

In a child with sickle cell disease, new pulmonary infiltrate plus fever and/or respiratory symptoms should raise concern for acute chest syndrome. Causes may include infection, fat embolism, hypoventilation, or pulmonary infarction.

Priorities: oxygen for hypoxemia, adequate but careful hydration, pain control without excessive hypoventilation, incentive spirometry or lung expansion, antibiotics when indicated, bronchodilator for bronchospasm, transfusion when appropriate, and escalation for respiratory failure.

Recognize Acute Chest Syndrome

A 12-year-old with sickle cell disease develops fever, chest pain, tachypnea, hypoxemia, and a new lower-lobe infiltrate.

Key Takeaways

Air movement matters more than wheeze volume
A quiet chest with fatigue and rising CO₂ is an emergency.
Bronchiolitis is primarily supportive care
Treat hypoxemia, dehydration, obstruction, and respiratory failure rather than the viral label alone.
Chronic wet cough needs explanation
Consider CF, bronchiectasis, PCD, aspiration, and airway malacia.
Some pulmonary diseases are multisystem
Nutrition, infection control, sleep, swallowing, hematology, and family adherence shape respiratory outcomes.
