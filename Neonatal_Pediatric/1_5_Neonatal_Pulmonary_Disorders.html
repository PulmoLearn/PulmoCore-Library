Differential Diagnosis

Neonatal Respiratory Distress Has a Limited Set of Common Patterns

Start with six questions: How premature is the infant? What happened before and during delivery? When did symptoms begin and what does the bedside examination show? What do oxygenation and the blood gas show? What does the chest image show? How does the infant respond to oxygen and pressure?

Mechanism first. Neonatal respiratory compromise may be primarily pulmonary (parenchymal disease, retained fluid, obstruction, infection, air leak, or hypoplastic lungs), cardiovascular/pulmonary vascular (PPHN or congenital heart disease causing shunt physiology), or central-control (apnea of prematurity). The mechanism determines which findings matter most and which support is likely to help.
Use the blood gas to define severity, not to name the disease by itself. Ask whether the infant has primarily an oxygenation problem, a ventilation problem with rising carbon dioxide, acidemia from respiratory failure or poor perfusion, or a mixed pattern. Interpret the result together with the current FiO₂/support, work of breathing, trend, and imaging.

Prematurity

RDS Is a Disease of Surfactant Deficiency and Immature Lung Structure

Respiratory distress syndrome is most common in premature infants. High surface tension promotes alveolar collapse, low functional residual capacity, decreased compliance, V/Q mismatch, and increased work of breathing.

Presentation
Immediate or early grunting, retractions, tachypnea, cyanosis, and increasing oxygen need.
Imaging
Low lung volume, diffuse reticulogranular or ground-glass opacity, and air bronchograms.

Management emphasizes thermal support, oxygen titration, early noninvasive respiratory support, selective early surfactant, and lung-protective ventilation when invasive support is required. Current practice favors keeping spontaneously breathing preterm infants on CPAP or another noninvasive strategy when feasible.

Delayed Fluid Clearance

TTN Usually Improves as Fetal Lung Fluid Is Absorbed

Transient tachypnea of the newborn is caused by delayed clearance of fetal lung fluid. It is more common after cesarean delivery without labor, in late-preterm infants, and in infants of diabetic mothers.

FeatureTTN pattern
OnsetWithin the first hours after birth
Dominant signTachypnea, sometimes with mild retractions or oxygen need
ImagingHyperinflation, perihilar streaking, fluid in fissures, and small pleural effusions
CourseSupportive care with improvement commonly within 24–72 hours

Support may include oxygen, CPAP, careful feeding decisions, and monitoring. Worsening illness, high oxygen need, fever, shock, or persistent symptoms should prompt reassessment for another diagnosis.

Obstruction, Inflammation, and Air Trapping

Meconium Aspiration Produces Uneven Lung Disease

Meconium may obstruct small airways, create ball-valve air trapping, cause chemical pneumonitis, inactivate surfactant, and trigger PPHN. Lung regions may show both atelectasis and hyperinflation.

Imaging
Coarse, patchy bilateral opacities with hyperinflation and possible air leak.
Complications
Pneumothorax, severe hypoxemia, pulmonary hypertension, and need for advanced ventilation.

Treatment is based on severity: oxygen, CPAP or invasive ventilation, careful PEEP, surfactant in selected severe cases, pulmonary vasodilator therapy when PPHN is present, and ECMO for refractory cardiorespiratory failure.

Failure of Pulmonary Transition

PPHN Maintains Fetal-Style Right-to-Left Shunting

Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn occurs when pulmonary vascular resistance remains abnormally high after birth. Right-to-left shunting across the ductus arteriosus and/or foramen ovale causes severe, often labile hypoxemia.

Assessment clueWhy it matters
Preductal saturation higher than postductalSuggests right-to-left ductal shunting, although absence of a difference does not exclude PPHN.
Hypoxemia out of proportion to radiographic diseaseRaises concern for shunt physiology or congenital heart disease.
Labile SpO₂ with handling or agitationPulmonary vascular tone may worsen abruptly.
EchocardiographyConfirms pulmonary hypertension, shunt direction, ventricular function, and excludes structural heart disease.

Treatment targets the cause and the pulmonary vascular environment: optimize lung recruitment and ventilation, correct hypoxemia, acidosis, hypothermia, hypoglycemia, and hypotension, minimize agitation, and consider inhaled nitric oxide when appropriate. Refractory disease may require ECMO.

Infection and Aspiration

Pneumonia May Resemble Other Neonatal Lung Diseases

Neonatal pneumonia can be acquired before, during, or after birth. Risk factors include prolonged membrane rupture, maternal fever, chorioamnionitis, prematurity, invasive support, and aspiration.

Respiratory signs
Tachypnea, grunting, apnea, increased oxygen need, poor compliance, or diffuse infiltrates.
Systemic signs
Temperature instability, lethargy, poor feeding, hypotension, glucose instability, or abnormal perfusion.
Evaluation
Cultures, CBC trends, inflammatory markers, blood gas, imaging, and response to therapy.

Respiratory support is paired with antimicrobial treatment when bacterial infection is suspected. Aspiration risk may arise from meconium, blood, milk, reflux, swallowing dysfunction, or congenital gastrointestinal-airway connections.

Sudden Deterioration

Air Leak Syndromes Can Progress in Minutes

Pneumothorax, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, pneumomediastinum, and pneumopericardium may occur spontaneously or with positive-pressure support. Excess pressure, uneven obstruction, and fragile premature lungs increase risk.

Pneumothorax clues
Sudden desaturation, bradycardia, asymmetric chest movement, decreased breath sounds, hypotension, and increased support need.
Immediate priorities
Assess airway position and patency, reduce unnecessary pressure, rapidly confirm the diagnosis, and decompress a tension pneumothorax without delaying for imaging.

Respond to an Air Leak

A ventilated premature infant suddenly becomes cyanotic and hypotensive. The right chest is hyperexpanded with markedly decreased breath sounds, and transillumination is strongly positive.

Choose the urgent action.

Key Takeaways

Escalation framework: do not escalate from a diagnosis label alone. Escalate when the infant shows a worsening trend such as increasing FiO₂ requirement, persistent or worsening hypoxemia, rising carbon dioxide with fatigue or acidemia, increasing work of breathing, recurrent apnea or bradycardia, hemodynamic compromise, or failure of the current support strategy. Move from oxygen to noninvasive support, invasive ventilation, specialty gas therapy, or advanced rescue support according to the physiology and response while urgently treating reversible causes such as airway malposition, obstruction, or air leak.
Use the whole pattern
Gestation, delivery history, examination, oxygenation, blood gases, imaging, and response to support are more useful together than any single finding.
RDS collapses; TTN retains fluid
RDS produces low-volume surfactant-deficient lungs, while TTN usually produces a transient wet-lung pattern.
MAS is uneven and injury-prone
Obstruction, inflammation, air trapping, surfactant dysfunction, air leak, and PPHN may coexist.
Sudden change is an emergency
Always consider air leak, airway malposition, obstruction, hemorrhage, or abrupt pulmonary vascular worsening.
