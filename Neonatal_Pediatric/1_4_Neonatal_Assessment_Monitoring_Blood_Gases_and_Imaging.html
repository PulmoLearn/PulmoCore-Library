Start With the Patient

Monitoring Supports the Examination; It Does Not Replace It

Begin with general appearance and then move through airway, breathing, circulation, neurologic status, temperature, and equipment. Compare current findings with the infant’s baseline and recent trend.

Oxygenation Monitoring

Pulse Oximetry Measures Saturation, Not Ventilation

Pulse oximetry estimates arterial oxyhemoglobin saturation. It does not measure PaO₂, PaCO₂, ventilation, oxygen content, or tissue perfusion directly.

Preductal
Right hand or wrist; reflects blood reaching the brain and coronary circulation before ductal mixing.
Postductal
Either foot; reflects blood after potential mixing through the ductus arteriosus.
Interpret the signal before the number: Motion, low perfusion, edema, ambient light, poor probe contact, venous pulsation, and dyshemoglobins can produce misleading values.

A meaningful preductal-postductal difference may suggest ductal shunting, but the pattern must be interpreted with the clinical examination, blood pressure, echocardiography, and other data.

Continuous Trends

ETCO₂ and Transcutaneous Monitoring Answer Different Questions

MethodBest useImportant limitation
End-tidal CO₂Confirm airway placement, trend exhaled CO₂, detect disconnection, obstruction, apnea, and changes in ventilation or perfusion.Small tidal volumes, leaks, dead space, low pulmonary blood flow, and severe lung disease may widen the PaCO₂–ETCO₂ difference.
Transcutaneous CO₂Continuous estimate of CO₂ trend without repeated blood sampling.Requires calibration, heated-sensor site changes, and correlation with a blood gas.
Transcutaneous O₂Continuous oxygen trend, especially when arterial access is limited.Affected by skin thickness, perfusion, temperature, edema, and sensor condition.
NIRSRegional tissue oxygenation trend, commonly cerebral or somatic.It is a regional trend, not a direct measurement of systemic arterial saturation or cardiac output.

Match the Monitoring Method

Continuous estimate of arterial oxyhemoglobin saturation
Exhaled CO₂ waveform used to confirm ventilation and detect disconnection
Heated skin sensor that trends oxygen or carbon dioxide

Sampling Strategy

Choose the Least Invasive Sample That Can Answer the Question

SampleBest informationKey limitation
ArterialMost direct assessment of pH, PaCO₂, PaO₂, and arterial oxygenation.Invasive; repeated puncture may injure vessels and tissue.
CapillaryUseful for pH and PCO₂ trends in many stable infants.Capillary PO₂ is not a reliable substitute for arterial oxygenation.
VenousUseful for systemic acid-base and metabolic assessment; central venous samples may help evaluate oxygen extraction.Venous PO₂ does not represent arterial oxygenation, and PCO₂ is usually higher than arterial.
Umbilical arterial cord gasReflects fetal condition and placental gas exchange near delivery.Must be correctly identified and interpreted with delivery events.
Umbilical arterial catheterAllows repeated arterial sampling and continuous blood-pressure monitoring in selected neonates.Requires careful placement, sterile care, and monitoring for vascular complications.

Systematic Review

Read the Entire Image Before Naming the Disease

A portable neonatal chest radiograph should be reviewed in a consistent order:

Image quality
Patient identity, projection, rotation, exposure, inspiration, and body position.
Airway and mediastinum
Trachea, carina, cardiac silhouette, mediastinal shift, and thymus.
Lungs and pleura
Volume, symmetry, opacities, lucencies, air bronchograms, effusion, and air leak.
Everything else
Diaphragm, abdomen, bones, soft tissues, lines, tubes, and devices.
Pattern examples: Low-volume diffuse granular lungs with air bronchograms suggest RDS; hyperinflation with coarse patchy opacities may occur with meconium aspiration; unilateral hyperlucency with absent markings and mediastinal shift suggests a tension pneumothorax.
Position Before Interpretation

Confirm Every Line and Tube on Every Image

DeviceDesired positionImportant hazard
Endotracheal tubeMid-trachea with the head neutral, safely above the carina.Too deep may enter a main bronchus; too high increases accidental extubation risk.
Orogastric or nasogastric tubeTip and side holes below the diaphragm within the stomach.Esophageal or airway placement can cause aspiration, ineffective decompression, or injury.
Umbilical arterial catheterHigh position in the descending thoracic aorta or an accepted low abdominal position according to policy.Placement near major arterial branches increases thrombosis and organ ischemia risk.
Umbilical venous catheterAt the inferior vena cava–right atrial junction, outside the cardiac silhouette.Portal, hepatic, intracardiac, or pericardial malposition can cause serious complications.

Key Takeaways

Assess before reacting
Confirm the infant’s condition and signal quality before treating a monitor number.
Know what each monitor measures
SpO₂, ETCO₂, transcutaneous values, NIRS, and blood gases are related but not interchangeable.
Sample site changes interpretation
Capillary PO₂ and venous PO₂ do not represent arterial oxygenation.
Imaging includes devices
Always inspect line, tube, and airway position before focusing only on the lung pattern.
