Before the Delivery Call: Read the Maternal Story

Incoming delivery

29 weeks · severe preeclampsia · urgent cesarean anticipated

The neonatal team is called before delivery. Your first job is not to wait for the baby—it is to use the maternal and pregnancy history to predict what may happen next.

Clinical rule: gestational age, maternal disorders, medications, infection status, membrane rupture, amniotic fluid, fetal monitoring, and the delivery plan can all change what the neonatal team should anticipate.

Gestational agePredicts airway size, surfactant maturity, temperature risk, respiratory drive, and likely support needs.

The Fetal Monitor Changes the Urgency

New information

Recurrent late decelerations

The obstetric team reports recurrent late decelerations. The cesarean is now urgent.

What antenatal tests tell you

Nonstress test: fetal heart-rate accelerations associated with movement.

Biophysical profile: fetal movement, tone, breathing movement, amniotic fluid, and heart-rate testing.

Amniotic fluid index: estimates fluid volume and helps identify oligohydramnios or polyhydramnios.

Deceleration patterns

Early: mirrors the contraction → usually fetal head compression.

Variable: abrupt, variable timing → suggests umbilical cord compression.

Late: begins after the contraction starts and recovers after it ends → suggests uteroplacental insufficiency and fetal hypoxemia.

Prepare Before the Infant Arrives

Decision point

29 weeks + severe preeclampsia + recurrent late decelerations

You have minutes before delivery. Decide what level of preparation is appropriate.

Preparation must match predicted risk. At least one qualified person must be assigned to the newborn and able to begin ventilation. Higher-risk births require additional personnel and advanced stabilization capability.

Environment

Warm room, preheated radiant warmer, warm linens, hat, thermal wrap or mattress when appropriate.

Ventilation

Flow-inflating or self-inflating device, T-piece when used, masks, blender, oxygen source, suction, pulse oximetry.

Airway

Appropriate laryngoscope blades, ETT sizes, securement, CO₂ detector, supraglottic airway when appropriate.

Escalation

Medication access, chest-compression space, transport incubator, communication with NICU or referral center.

The Baby Arrives: Describe Gestational Age and Growth Separately

Birth

Infant delivered at 29 weeks

The baby reaches the warmer. Before interpreting the rest of the assessment, classify gestational age correctly.

PretermBefore 37 completed weeks.
Term37 weeks through 41 weeks and 6 days.
Post-termAt or after 42 weeks.
Growth is a different question. Small for gestational age generally means birth weight below the expected range for gestational age, commonly below the 10th percentile. Large for gestational age is commonly above the 90th percentile.

APGAR Describes Adaptation—It Does Not Decide When to Resuscitate

Do not wait for an APGAR score to start needed resuscitation. Breathing and heart rate drive immediate resuscitation decisions. APGAR is assigned at 1 and 5 minutes and may be repeated when low.

Recognize the Pattern and Act

Respiratory distress is a pattern. Combine work of breathing, gas exchange, temperature, perfusion, tone, and the surrounding history rather than reacting to one isolated number.

Work of breathing

Grunting, nasal flaring, retractions, head bobbing, seesaw movement, tachypnea.

Gas exchange

Central cyanosis, low saturation, apnea, altered mental status, worsening blood gases.

Systemic adaptation

Temperature instability, poor perfusion, weak feeding, hypotonia, abnormal glucose may accompany serious illness.

Thermoregulation

Cold stress increases metabolic demand and oxygen consumption and can worsen hypoglycemia, acidosis, and respiratory distress.

Independent case

A second newborn arrives

A 36-week newborn is 20 minutes old. Temperature is 35.7°C, RR 72/min, with nasal flaring, intercostal retractions, expiratory grunting, and central cyanosis.
