Scenario 2.7 · Beyond the Hospital

The Plan Has to Work When the ICU Is No Longer Around.

Take a technology-dependent child from interfacility transport through home ventilation, tracheostomy emergencies, caregiver competency, school and home readiness, and comfort-focused respiratory care. Safe discharge is not a paperwork event—it is a system test.

Move Only After Stabilization

Transport case

Ventilator-dependent child scheduled for transfer

The child is stable in the PICU, but transport will involve elevators, loading, road travel, and a handoff at another facility.

Transport converts small problems into limited-access emergencies. Before departure, confirm airway, breathing, circulation, environment, equipment, gas, power, communication, and contingency plans.

Plan for the Delay, Not the Perfect Trip

Approximate cylinder duration = available gas volume ÷ total flow demand
Gas planning

Usable oxygen = 900 L · estimated total oxygen consumption = 15 L/min

Calculate approximate duration before adding a safety reserve

Do not plan to use the cylinder to empty. Add margin for loading, traffic, weather, elevator delays, handoff time, increased patient demand, and emergency detours. Battery planning also needs a reserve.

Every Tracheostomy Caregiver Needs an Emergency Plan

Home emergency

Accidental decannulation

A tracheostomy-dependent child becomes distressed after the tube is accidentally removed. The caregiver has the emergency bag and has been trained in tube replacement.

Emergency bag: same-size and one-size-smaller tracheostomy tubes, obturator, ties, suction supplies, oxygen/manual ventilation equipment, and the written emergency pathway.
Obstruction: assess the child, attempt suction, remove an obstructed inner cannula when applicable, provide oxygen, and replace the tube if ventilation remains ineffective.

Education Is Not Complete Until the Caregiver Can Do It

Competency, not attendance. Use plain language, demonstration, return demonstration, teach-back, scenario rehearsal, and written/visual plans.
Emergency rehearsal should include power loss, ventilator alarms, thick secretions, suction failure, accidental decannulation, equipment disconnection, increased work of breathing, and when to activate EMS.

Independent synthesis

Sometimes the Goal Changes From Prolonging Life to Relieving Burden

Palliative care can occur alongside disease-directed treatment. It focuses on symptom relief, communication, quality of life, family support, and aligning treatment with the child’s and family’s goals.
Goals-of-care case

Progressive neuromuscular disease

The child uses nocturnal noninvasive ventilation. The family chooses comfort-focused care and does not want future intubation, but wants NIV continued when it relieves dyspnea and improves comfort.

Comfort-focused respiratory care may include positioning, airflow, secretion management, oxygen when it relieves symptoms, noninvasive support when consistent with goals, and medication-assisted dyspnea relief.
Withdrawal of support requires preparation. Confirm goals, code status, medications, device plan, family wishes, team roles, symptom-control measures, and who will remain present. Minimize unnecessary alarms, noise, monitoring, and visible equipment burden when they no longer serve the goals of care.
