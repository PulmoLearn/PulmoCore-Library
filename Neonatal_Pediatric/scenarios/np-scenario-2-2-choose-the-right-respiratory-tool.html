Scenario 2.2 · Choose the Right Respiratory Tool

The Best Therapy Is the One That Solves the Actual Problem.

Move through a pediatric respiratory shift where each patient needs something different. Choose oxygen support, HFNC, aerosol delivery, airway-clearance therapy, cough augmentation, suction, heliox, or inhaled pulmonary vasodilation based on the physiologic problem—not simply the device you know best.

Match Oxygen Delivery to Demand

Oxygen is a medication. Choose the device based on the target saturation, oxygen requirement, inspiratory flow demand, airway anatomy, comfort, and humidification needs.
DeviceBest fitImportant limitation
Low-flow nasal cannulaMild oxygen need in a stable child.Delivered FiO₂ varies with patient demand and breathing pattern.
Nonrebreather maskRapid high-concentration oxygen during acute hypoxemia.Needs adequate flow and reservoir inflation.
Oxygen hoodControlled humidified oxygen around an infant’s head.Requires enough flow to prevent CO₂ accumulation and careful temperature monitoring.
HFNCHeated humidified high flow for hypoxemia with increased work of breathing.Does not replace ventilation when apnea, fatigue, or severe hypercapnia is present.

HFNC Works—Until It Doesn’t

Patient 1

6-month-old with bronchiolitis

SpO₂ 86%, RR 58/min, moderate retractions, copious nasal secretions. The infant is alert and has no apnea.

HFNC delivers heated humidified gas at flows intended to meet or exceed inspiratory demand. It may reduce nasopharyngeal dead space and generate variable distending pressure.
Reassuring response: lower respiratory rate, fewer retractions, improved comfort, lower oxygen need, stable CO₂. Failure: worsening retractions, apnea, rising CO₂, altered mental status, persistent hypoxemia, or hemodynamic instability.

Deliver the Medication to the Airway, Not the Room

Patient 2

2-year-old with wheeze

The child needs an inhaled bronchodilator but becomes upset by the mask and cries during treatment.

pMDI + spacer/VHCPortable, fast, reproducible, and effective when technique and seal are good.
Jet nebulizerFamiliar and useful, but noisy with residual volume and longer treatment time.
Mesh nebulizerLow residual volume and efficient output; useful in ventilator circuits.
DPIRequires adequate patient-generated inspiratory flow and is unsuitable for many young or acutely ill children.
For infants and young children: use a valved holding chamber with a properly fitted mask. Calm tidal breathing improves deposition; crying substantially reduces lower-airway delivery.

Mobilizing Secretions Is Not the Same as Removing Them

Choose airway-clearance therapy according to the secretion problem and the child’s ability to cooperate and cough.
PEPExhalation against resistance helps splint airways and move gas behind secretions.
Oscillatory PEPAdds expiratory oscillation to secretion mobilization.
Chest-wall oscillationMobilizes secretions in chronic secretion disorders but does not replace an effective cough.
Manual CPTSelected percussion and drainage positions for retained or focal secretions.
Mechanical insufflation-exsufflationPositive then negative pressure simulates a cough in respiratory muscle weakness.
SuctionRemoves secretions when the patient cannot clear them or obstruction is suspected; it should not be performed automatically on a schedule.

A Weak Cough Changes the Plan

Patient 3

12-year-old with muscular dystrophy

The child has a weak cough, falling vital capacity, and recurrent mucus plugging. Breath sounds improve briefly after suction, but secretions recur.

If secretions are mobilized but the child cannot generate an effective cough, the missing step is cough augmentation, not simply more scheduled suction.

Reduced support

Specialty Gases Are Bridges, Not Cures

Heliox

A helium-oxygen mixture with lower density that may reduce turbulent resistance and work of breathing in selected upper-airway obstruction or severe obstructive disease. Benefit decreases as required oxygen concentration rises.

Inhaled nitric oxide

Selective pulmonary vasodilation in ventilated lung regions. Monitor oxygenation response, NO/NO₂, methemoglobin, delivery-system function, and rebound during weaning.

Inhaled epoprostenol

Aerosolized prostacyclin that may reduce pulmonary vascular resistance. Monitor clinical response, aerosol delivery, systemic pressure, platelet effects, and interruption risk.

What none of them replace

Lung recruitment, ventilation, correction of acidosis, treatment of the underlying disease, or definitive airway stabilization.

Choose the Tool—and Define How You Will Know It Worked

Independent case

Severe postextubation upper-airway narrowing

A child has marked stridor and increased work of breathing despite standard medication. SpO₂ is adequate on a modest oxygen concentration while the airway team prepares definitive management.

Every therapy needs a reassessment plan: oxygen/HFNC → SpO₂, work, RR, mental status, CO₂; aerosol → technique and response; clearance → secretion movement and cough effectiveness; heliox → work/stridor and readiness for definitive treatment; inhaled vasodilator → oxygenation, hemodynamics, uninterrupted delivery, and safe weaning.
