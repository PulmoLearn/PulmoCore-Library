Scenario 2.3 · Protect the Airway

The Airway Can Change Faster Than the Plan.

Follow a child from upper-airway obstruction through bag-mask support, airway adjunct selection, intubation preparation, tube verification, noninvasive ventilation decisions, and sudden post-intubation deterioration. The goal is not just to place an airway—it is to keep oxygenation and ventilation reliable through every transition.

Bag-Mask Ventilation Begins With Position and Seal

Deterioration

3-year-old becomes apneic after progressive airway fatigue

SpO₂ is falling and the child needs immediate assisted ventilation.

Position

Use a neutral or slightly extended position appropriate to age. Avoid excessive flexion or extension. A shoulder roll may help infants and small children because of the relatively large occiput.

Two-person technique

When seal is difficult, one clinician uses two hands to hold the mask and open the airway while the second squeezes the bag and watches chest rise.

Ventilate to visible chest rise. Excessive pressure or volume increases gastric insufflation and lung injury risk.

Reduced support

Verify Placement Immediately—and Again After Every Move

No single visual cue is enough. Confirm tracheal ventilation with continuous waveform capnography when available, then integrate chest rise, bilateral breath sounds, tube depth, SpO₂ response, and imaging when appropriate.

Best immediate physiologic confirmation

Persistent exhaled CO₂ with an appropriate waveform strongly supports tracheal placement in a perfusing child.

Reconfirm after movement

Transport, repositioning, procedures, and circuit manipulation can displace the tube. Reassess depth and ventilation after every significant move.

CPAP and Bilevel NIV Solve Different Problems

CPAP

Provides one continuous distending pressure. Best when the child primarily needs lung recruitment, airway splinting, and improved oxygenation while ventilation remains adequate.

Bilevel NIV

Provides inspiratory pressure above expiratory pressure, adding pressure support that can reduce work of breathing and improve ventilation.

New patient

10-year-old with pneumonia

Increasing work of breathing and hypoxemia improve after NIV begins. Two hours later the child becomes drowsy, mask leak increases, PaCO₂ rises, and oxygenation worsens.

NIV failure is a trend: worsening mental status, escalating oxygen need, rising CO₂/acidosis, persistent severe work, hemodynamic instability, inability to protect the airway, or inability to maintain an effective interface.

The Child Suddenly Deteriorates After Intubation

Emergency

Immediately after transport

SpO₂ falls rapidly. Peak pressure rises. Breath sounds are markedly decreased on the left. The ETCO₂ waveform is still present but lower than before.

Think mechanical first. After intubation, sudden deterioration should trigger rapid evaluation for tube displacement, obstruction, pneumothorax, and equipment/circuit failure while oxygenation and ventilation are supported.
