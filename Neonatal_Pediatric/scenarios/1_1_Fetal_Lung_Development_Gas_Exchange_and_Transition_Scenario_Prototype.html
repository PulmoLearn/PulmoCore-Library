C1 Lung Maturity

Fetal Lung Development Builds Structure Before Full Function

Lung development is progressive and continues after birth. Early phases establish the conducting airways and pulmonary vessels. Later phases create thin gas-exchanging surfaces, surfactant-producing cells, and increasing numbers of alveoli.

EmbryonalDay 26–52
Lung bud, major airways, early pulmonary veins, diaphragm.
PseudoglandularDay 52–16 weeks
Conducting airway branching, cilia, glands, cartilage.
Canalicular17–26 weeks
Capillary growth, acinar formation, type I and II cells, early viability.
Saccular26–36 weeks
Terminal saccules thin and mature; gas-exchange surface expands.
Alveolar/PostnatalAbout 36 weeks onward
Alveoli mature and multiply rapidly after birth.
Clinical connection: A fetus may become potentially viable during the canalicular phase because capillaries approach the developing air spaces, but severe surfactant deficiency and immature lung structure remain major limitations.

C1 Lung Maturity

Type I Cells Exchange Gas; Type II Cells Stabilize the Alveoli

Type I pneumocytes
Extremely thin cells covering most of the alveolar surface. Their shape minimizes diffusion distance.
Type II pneumocytes
Produce, store, secrete, and recycle pulmonary surfactant. They can also proliferate after alveolar injury.

Surfactant lowers surface tension at the air-liquid interface. This improves compliance, reduces the opening pressure required for small alveoli, and helps prevent collapse at end-exhalation.

When surfactant is adequateWhen surfactant is deficient
Lower surface tensionHigh surface tension
Improved complianceStiff, poorly compliant lungs
More stable functional residual capacityRecurrent alveolar collapse and atelectasis
Lower work of breathingIncreased work of breathing and oxygen need
Fetal lung fluid is necessary before birth. It maintains lung expansion and supports normal growth. At birth, that fluid must be absorbed and displaced so air can establish functional residual capacity. Retained fluid contributes to transient tachypnea of the newborn.

Differentiate Alveolar Cells and Surfactant

Forms the thin surface used for diffusion
Produces and stores pulmonary surfactant
Lowers alveolar surface tension
Maintains lung expansion and supports development before birth

C1 Lung Maturity · Contrast Case

Pulmonary Hypoplasia Means Too Little Functional Lung

Pulmonary hypoplasia is incomplete lung development resulting in reduced lung volume, fewer or smaller airways and alveoli, and a reduced pulmonary vascular bed. The result may be severe respiratory failure and pulmonary hypertension at birth.

Low intrathoracic space
Congenital diaphragmatic hernia, thoracic masses, or skeletal restriction.
Low fetal lung fluid or amniotic fluid
Prolonged oligohydramnios or early membrane rupture can impair lung expansion.
Reduced fetal breathing movement
Neuromuscular or central disorders may reduce the mechanical stimulus for growth.

Placental Physiology

The Placenta Performs Fetal Gas Exchange

Maternal and fetal blood normally remain in separate vascular systems. Oxygen and nutrients diffuse across the placental interface into fetal blood, while carbon dioxide and waste products move toward maternal circulation.

Umbilical vein
Carries the most oxygenated blood from the placenta toward the fetus.
Umbilical arteries
Carry less-oxygenated blood from the fetus back to the placenta.
Remember the exception: Veins travel toward the heart and arteries travel away from the heart. Their names do not depend on oxygen content.
C2 Fetal Circulation

Three Shunts Bypass the Fetal Liver and Lungs

Placenta

Provides oxygen and removes carbon dioxide.

Umbilical vein → fetus
Ductus venosus
Bypasses much of the liver
Foramen ovale
Right atrium → left atrium
Ductus arteriosus
Pulmonary artery → aorta

Fetus

High pulmonary vascular resistance limits pulmonary blood flow.

Umbilical arteries → placenta

Oxygen-rich placental blood enters through the umbilical vein. Much of it bypasses the liver through the ductus venosus and enters the inferior vena cava. Preferential streaming sends the best-oxygenated blood across the foramen ovale to the left atrium, left ventricle, and ascending aorta, supporting the heart and brain. Blood entering the right ventricle faces high PVR, so most is diverted through the ductus arteriosus into the descending aorta.
