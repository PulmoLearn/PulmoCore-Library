Scenario 2.6 · More Than a Lung Problem

Normal SpO₂ Does Not Mean the Child Is Safe.

Follow critically ill pediatric patients whose respiratory problems begin in the heart, circulation, brain, respiratory muscles, or chest wall. Connect oxygenation to perfusion, detect silent ventilatory muscle failure, protect the brain and spine, recognize thoracic emergencies, and decide whether extracorporeal support must provide gas exchange, circulation, or both.

Respiratory Care Can Change Cardiac Physiology

Before aggressively changing oxygen or pressure in congenital heart disease, ask what the circulation depends on: pulmonary blood flow, systemic blood flow, mixing, or balance between pulmonary and systemic flow.
Excess pulmonary blood flowTachypnea, retractions, pulmonary edema, hepatomegaly, poor growth. Excessive oxygen may further lower pulmonary vascular resistance.
Reduced pulmonary blood flowCyanosis from low pulmonary blood flow; oxygen may help only when pulmonary vasoconstriction contributes.
Ductal-dependent systemic circulationSystemic perfusion can fall as the ductus closes even when SpO₂ looks acceptable.
Mixing / single-ventricle physiologySystemic oxygenation depends on adequate mixing and careful balance of pulmonary and systemic blood flow.

Shock Is an Oxygen-Delivery Problem

SpO₂ measures arterial saturation. It does not measure cardiac output, hemoglobin concentration, or tissue oxygen delivery.
Shock typeTypical clues
HypovolemicTachycardia, cool extremities, narrow pulse pressure, delayed refill, low urine output, fluid loss or bleeding.
Distributive / septicWarm or cool shock, fever or hypothermia, altered mental status, rising lactate.
CardiogenicPoor perfusion with hepatomegaly, pulmonary edema, gallop rhythm, rising venous pressure, or known heart disease.
ObstructiveTension pneumothorax, tamponade, or massive pulmonary vascular obstruction limiting filling or output.
Case A

Child with congenital heart disease after a large fluid bolus

Tachycardia, cool extremities, hepatomegaly, worsening crackles, delayed refill, and low urine output develop.

Neuromuscular Failure Can Hide Behind a Normal Saturation

Follow strength and ventilation, not SpO₂ alone. A child may maintain oxygen saturation until respiratory reserve is dangerously low.
Vital capacityGlobal respiratory reserve and trend toward ventilatory failure.
Maximal inspiratory pressureInspiratory muscle strength.
Peak cough flowAbility to generate expiratory flow for secretion clearance.
ETCO₂ / blood gasDetects evolving hypoventilation.
Bulbar assessmentIdentifies aspiration, secretion-retention, and airway-protection risk.
TrendRapid decline matters even before a single value becomes profoundly abnormal.

The Brain and Spine Need Oxygenation, Ventilation, and Perfusion

Case B

Traumatic brain injury

The child is intubated after severe head trauma. Blood pressure is marginal and oxygenation is being stabilized.

Avoid secondary injury from hypoxemia and hypotension. Maintain appropriate ventilation. Routine aggressive hyperventilation is avoided; brief controlled hyperventilation is reserved for specific acute herniation situations.
Cervical spine injury: maintain alignment, use jaw thrust when appropriate, and anticipate diaphragmatic weakness with high lesions. Neurogenic shock may present with hypotension, relative bradycardia, and warm skin.

Thoracic Trauma Demands Mechanism-Specific Action

Pulmonary contusionWorsening hypoxemia and infiltrates after blunt trauma; support oxygenation and use lung-protective ventilation when needed.
Tension pneumothoraxSudden unilateral decreased breath sounds with shock; decompress immediately.
HemothoraxDecreased breath sounds, dullness, shock, and pleural blood; restore volume and drain the chest.
Flail chestParadoxical chest-wall motion with underlying contusion; analgesia, pulmonary hygiene, and ventilatory support as needed.
Diaphragmatic injuryAbdominal contents enter chest; avoid mask overdistention and prepare definitive care.
PriorityAirway, breathing, and circulation problems may coexist—treat immediately life-threatening physiology first.

ECMO Must Match the Organ Failure

VV ECMO

Provides gas-exchange support while relying on the patient’s native cardiac output. Typical use: severe respiratory failure with adequate cardiac function.

VA ECMO

Provides both cardiac and respiratory support. Typical use: cardiogenic shock, cardiac arrest, or combined cardiorespiratory failure.

ECMO does not eliminate respiratory care. Continue lung-protective ventilation, humidification, secretion management, airway security, infection prevention, and monitoring for bleeding, thrombosis, hemolysis, differential hypoxemia, and circuit failure.

Stabilization Has to Survive the Move

Transport decision

Critically ill child scheduled for transfer

The airway is marginally secure, vasoactive support is still being adjusted, the chest tube is intermittently bubbling without a clear explanation, and portable oxygen reserve is barely enough for the expected trip.

Never transport an unresolved instability. Secure the airway, confirm ventilator and chest-drain function, verify gas and battery duration, stabilize perfusion, protect lines, control temperature, assign emergency roles, and communicate disease-specific risks before departure.
