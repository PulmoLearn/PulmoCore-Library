Scenario 1.4 · What Is the Baby Telling You?

When the Numbers Change, Start With the Baby.

A preterm infant in the NICU begins to deteriorate. Follow the clues from the bedside examination to continuous monitoring, blood-gas sampling, radiographic review, line and tube position, and a final integrated decision. Your job is to decide which data are real, which are limited, and what deserves immediate action.

Monitoring Supports the Examination—It Does Not Replace It

NICU bedside

Preterm infant on respiratory support

You are called because the bedside monitor has changed. Before reacting to the screen, organize what you will assess at the bedside.

Use a consistent neonatal assessment: general appearance → breathing → gas exchange → circulation → neurologic/metabolic status → equipment.

The SpO₂ Falls—But Is the Infant Actually Worse?

Alarm

SpO₂ 94% → 72%

The infant remains pink. Heart rate and chest movement are unchanged. The pleth waveform is weak and erratic while the foot is moving.

Pulse oximetry

Estimates arterial oxyhemoglobin saturation. It does not directly measure PaO₂, PaCO₂, ventilation, oxygen content, or tissue perfusion.

Check signal quality

Motion, low perfusion, edema, ambient light, poor probe contact, venous pulsation, and dyshemoglobins can mislead the reading.

Preductal: right hand or wrist. Postductal: either foot. A meaningful difference can suggest ductal shunting, but it must be interpreted with the rest of the clinical picture.

Choose the Monitor That Answers the Question

ETCO₂Confirms airway placement, trends exhaled CO₂, and helps detect disconnection, obstruction, apnea, and changes in ventilation or perfusion.
Transcutaneous CO₂Provides a continuous CO₂ trend without repeated blood sampling; requires calibration, heated-site changes, and blood-gas correlation.
Transcutaneous O₂Provides an oxygen trend when arterial access is limited; affected by skin, perfusion, temperature, edema, and sensor condition.
NIRSProvides a regional cerebral or somatic tissue oxygenation trend; it is not a direct arterial saturation or cardiac-output measurement.

Now You Need a Blood Gas—Which Sample?

Choose the least invasive sample that can answer the clinical question.
SampleBest useKey limitation
ArterialMost direct pH, PaCO₂, PaO₂, and arterial oxygenation.Invasive.
CapillaryUseful pH and PCO₂ trends in many stable infants.PO₂ is not a reliable substitute for arterial oxygenation.
VenousUseful systemic acid-base and metabolic information.Venous PO₂ does not represent arterial oxygenation; PCO₂ is usually higher.
Umbilical arterial cordReflects fetal acid-base status at delivery.Answers a delivery-status question rather than current arterial oxygenation later in the NICU course.

Interpret the Gas in the Same Order Every Time

1. pH→2. PCO₂→3. HCO₃⁻ / base deficit→4. Oxygenation→5. Clinical trend
Arterial blood gas

pH 7.24 · PaCO₂ 62 · HCO₃⁻ 26 · PaO₂ 58 on FiO₂ 0.35

The infant is intubated.

Always interpret the gas in the context of gestational age, FiO₂, ventilation mode, temperature, perfusion, sampling site, and recent interventions.

Read the Entire Image Before Naming the Disease

1. Image quality

Identity, projection, rotation, exposure, inspiration, body position.

2. Airway & mediastinum

Trachea, carina, cardiac silhouette, mediastinal shift, thymus.

3. Lungs & pleura

Volume, symmetry, opacities, lucencies, air bronchograms, effusion, air leak.

4. Everything else

Diaphragm, abdomen, bones, soft tissue, lines, tubes, devices.

Pattern examples: Low-volume diffuse granular lungs with air bronchograms suggest RDS. Hyperinflation with coarse patchy opacities may occur with meconium aspiration. Unilateral hyperlucency with absent markings and mediastinal shift suggests tension pneumothorax.

UACHigh descending thoracic aorta or accepted low abdominal position per policy.
UVCIVC–right atrial junction, outside the cardiac silhouette.

Now Put the Baby, Monitor, Ventilator, and Image Together

Sudden deterioration

Ventilated preterm infant

SpO₂ falls and bradycardia develops. Chest movement becomes asymmetric. Breath sounds are markedly decreased on the left. ETCO₂ falls, but a waveform remains present. Exhaled tidal volume is lower.

The safest response is not to chase one monitor. Integrate the bedside examination with airway position, ventilation, and the possibility of unilateral air leak.
