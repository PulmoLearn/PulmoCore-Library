Scenario 1.5 · Why Is This Newborn Struggling?

Same Symptom. Different Disease.

Tachypnea, retractions, hypoxemia, and an abnormal chest x-ray can come from very different neonatal problems. Work through a neonatal respiratory differential using the clues that matter most: gestational age, delivery history, timing, physical findings, oxygen response, blood gas, imaging, and the infant’s response to support.

Start With the Mechanism, Not the Disease Name

When a newborn is struggling, first ask whether the dominant problem is poor lung expansion/compliance, retained fluid, airway obstruction/inflammation, infection, abnormal pulmonary vascular transition, air leak, underdeveloped lungs, or impaired respiratory drive.

Premature and Grunting: RDS or Something Else?

Case A

28 weeks · minutes after birth

Increasing grunting, retractions, tachypnea, and oxygen requirement. Chest radiograph shows low lung volumes with a diffuse fine granular pattern and air bronchograms.

Respiratory distress syndrome

Surfactant deficiency increases surface tension, reduces compliance, promotes atelectasis, lowers functional residual capacity, and worsens V/Q matching.

What should fit?

Prematurity, early onset, increasing work of breathing, diffuse low-volume disease on imaging, and need for lung recruitment.

Term and Tachypneic: Use the Delivery History

Case B

39 weeks · elective cesarean without labor

RR 78/min shortly after birth, mild retractions, oxygen requirement is modest. Chest radiograph shows hyperinflation, prominent perihilar markings, and fluid in the fissures.

TTN clue pattern: term or near-term infant + cesarean without labor + early tachypnea + retained lung-fluid pattern + relatively rapid improvement.

Meconium, Infection, or PPHN?

Case C

41 weeks · thick meconium

Coarse breath sounds, respiratory distress, patchy bilateral opacities with areas of hyperinflation.

Case D

35 weeks · maternal fever + prolonged rupture

Temperature instability, lethargy, respiratory distress, diffuse infiltrates, worsening gas exchange.

Case E

Term infant · severe hypoxemia

Preductal SpO₂ 94%, postductal SpO₂ 82%. Hypoxemia is out of proportion to the radiographic lung findings.

PPHN is a circulation problem expressed as a respiratory problem. Persistently high pulmonary vascular resistance promotes right-to-left shunting across fetal channels, producing severe hypoxemia. A preductal-postductal difference can support the pattern when ductal shunting is present.

When Oxygen Alone Is Not the Answer

Case E evolves

Severe hypoxemia persists despite optimized ventilation and lung recruitment

Echocardiography supports pulmonary hypertension with right-to-left shunting and excludes a ductal-dependent congenital heart lesion.

Inhaled nitric oxide may be used in appropriate term or near-term infants with significant hypoxemic respiratory failure and PPHN physiology because it selectively reduces pulmonary vascular resistance in ventilated lung regions.
Do not treat the number alone. Before escalating to specialty-gas therapy, optimize lung inflation, ventilation, oxygen delivery, temperature, perfusion, acid-base status, and identify the underlying disease.

Three Different Reasons a Baby Can Suddenly or Persistently Fail

Case F

Ventilated preterm infant · sudden deterioration

Acute desaturation, bradycardia, asymmetric chest movement, markedly decreased breath sounds on one side.

Case G

Severe oligohydramnios history

Small bell-shaped chest, severe respiratory failure from birth, poor lung expansion despite support.

Case H

27 weeks · recurrent pauses in breathing

Episodes are associated with bradycardia and desaturation; infection and other acute causes are not evident.

The Diagnosis Is Only Useful If It Changes What You Do

Longitudinal case

Former 25-week infant, now 38 weeks postmenstrual age

The infant has required prolonged oxygen and positive-pressure support. Imaging shows chronic heterogeneous lung disease. Growth has been difficult, and oxygen requirement rises with handling and feeding.

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia is chronic lung disease after prematurity and prolonged respiratory support. Management is individualized and may include oxygen, careful ventilation, nutrition and growth support, fluid management, treatment of pulmonary hypertension when present, and planning for safe chronic respiratory care.
