Conventional Ventilation Is Reaching Its Limit

Day 2 of life

25-week infant with severe RDS

Despite surfactant and conventional ventilation, FiO₂ and pressure requirements continue to rise. Blood gases remain poor and the team is concerned about additional lung injury from larger pressure and volume swings.

High-frequency ventilation separates lung recruitment from very small-volume ventilation. It can be considered when conventional ventilation cannot provide acceptable gas exchange without increasingly injurious settings.

HFOV

Uses very rapid oscillatory pressure changes around a set mean airway pressure. Mean airway pressure is a major oxygenation/recruitment control. Amplitude strongly influences CO₂ removal.

HFJV

Delivers very short high-velocity jet pulses through a specialized circuit while allowing passive exhalation. It is often useful when limiting air trapping and managing severe air-leak physiology are important goals.

Quantify How Severe the Oxygenation Failure Has Become

Oxygenation Index (OI) combines the oxygen concentration, mean airway pressure, and arterial oxygen response. A rising OI means more support is required to achieve a given PaO₂.
OI = (FiO₂ × Mean Airway Pressure × 100) ÷ PaO₂

The X-Ray Changes: Now You Are Fighting an Air Leak

Several hours later

Increasing CO₂ retention · patchy cystic lucencies tracking through the lung

The radiographic pattern is concerning for pulmonary interstitial emphysema (PIE).

Air-leak strategy: minimize excessive distending pressure and trapped gas while preserving enough recruitment and ventilation to maintain acceptable gas exchange.
Reduce unnecessary pressure/volumeAvoid overdistention that drives gas into interstitial spaces.
Allow exhalationAir trapping worsens when expiratory time is inadequate.
Accept reasonable gasesPermissive hypercapnia may be preferable to increasingly injurious ventilation when clinically appropriate.
Consider HFJVShort jet pulses and passive exhalation can be useful in selected infants with significant air-leak disease.

The Lung Is Not the Only Organ at Risk

Extreme prematurity, oxygen exposure, hemodynamic instability, infection, and respiratory support interact with the brain, eyes, gut, circulation, and developing lung.
ROPAbnormal retinal vascular development is associated with prematurity and oxygen exposure; avoid unnecessary hyperoxemia and large saturation swings.
IVHFragile germinal-matrix vessels make very preterm infants vulnerable, especially with abrupt cerebral blood-flow changes.
NECPrematurity, intestinal immaturity, altered perfusion, and feeding factors contribute to bowel injury.
BPDInterrupted lung development plus oxygen, inflammation, and mechanical injury contribute to chronic lung disease.
Unplanned extubationA preventable airway emergency that can rapidly cause hypoxemia, bradycardia, aspiration, and traumatic reintubation.
Ventilator-associated injuryExcess pressure, volume, oxygen, and repeated recruitment loss can compound lung injury.

Weeks Later: Is This Infant Ready to Extubate?

Recovery phase

31 weeks postmenstrual age

FiO₂ 0.25, low ventilator pressures, spontaneous respiratory effort is consistent, blood gas is acceptable, secretions are manageable, and hemodynamics are stable.

Extubation readiness is more than a low ventilator setting. Assess respiratory drive, gas exchange, airway patency, secretion burden, hemodynamics, recent apnea, caffeine status when appropriate, and the planned postextubation support.

Stability Can Change During Cooling or Transport

Therapeutic hypothermia

Eligible term or near-term infants with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy require controlled cooling and careful monitoring. Temperature affects metabolism, gas interpretation, oxygen consumption, hemodynamics, and medication handling.

Transport

Before departure, secure the airway and lines, verify portable oxygen and ventilation capacity, confirm battery life, anticipate temperature loss, test monitors, calculate medication needs, and plan for deterioration during movement.

Transport check

The infant is scheduled for transfer

The Goal Changes From Rescue to Sustainable Growth

Months later

Former 25-week infant with BPD preparing for chronic respiratory care

The infant has stable but persistent oxygen needs, is growing, tolerates feeds, and may require ongoing respiratory support after NICU discharge.

Chronic respiratory care is not simply “more oxygen.” The goal is stable gas exchange with enough respiratory reserve for growth, feeding, sleep, development, and safe care by the family while minimizing additional lung and oxygen injury.
Family capability is a clinical variable. Equipment training, alarm response, airway or tracheostomy care when applicable, emergency planning, oxygen/fire safety, follow-up, and caregiver demonstration must be established before discharge.
