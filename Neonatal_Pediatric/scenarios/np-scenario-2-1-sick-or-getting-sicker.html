Scenario 2.1 · Sick or Getting Sicker?

Pediatric Assessment Starts Before You Touch the Child.

Follow a young child from the first doorway impression through respiratory assessment, monitoring, diagnostics, and escalation. Your job is to recognize not only whether the child is sick, but whether the child is compensating—or beginning to fail.

The Pediatric First Look

Emergency department

18-month-old child with cough and increased work of breathing

Before applying monitors, you pause at the doorway and observe the child.

Pediatric Assessment Triangle: Appearance → Work of Breathing → Circulation to Skin. It is a rapid visual impression, not a replacement for the primary assessment.
AppearanceTone, interactiveness, consolability, look/gaze, speech or cry.
Work of breathingAbnormal sounds, position, retractions, flaring, head bobbing.
Circulation to skinPallor, mottling, cyanosis.

Age Changes What “Normal” Looks Like

Pediatric airway size, respiratory rate, chest-wall compliance, oxygen consumption, and respiratory reserve differ by age. A finding that is expected in one age group may be concerning in another.

Smaller airway, bigger consequences

A small decrease in airway radius can cause a large increase in resistance. Children can deteriorate quickly with edema, secretions, or bronchospasm.

Higher metabolic demand

Infants and young children have higher oxygen consumption and less reserve, so apnea or severe obstruction can produce hypoxemia rapidly.

C2 · Supported

Distress or Failure?

Initial exam

RR 48/min · nasal flaring · intercostal retractions · SpO₂ 91% · alert and anxious

Breath sounds are coarse with scattered wheeze. The child is still interacting with the caregiver.

Respiratory distress

Tachypnea, retractions, flaring, abnormal sounds, increased effort, anxiety, and compensatory tachycardia may be present while mental status and respiratory drive remain intact.

Respiratory failure

Worsening fatigue, decreased effort, altered mental status, bradypnea/apnea, poor air movement, persistent hypoxemia, rising PaCO₂, or bradycardia indicate failing compensation.

C3 · Reduced support

The Monitor Is a Trend, Not the Whole Patient

Twenty minutes later

SpO₂ 93% on oxygen · HR 154/min · RR falls from 48 to 28/min

The child is now sleepy, has weaker breath sounds, and retractions are less dramatic.

Less visible work can be worse. When a child tires, respiratory rate and retractions may decrease because the child is losing the ability to compensate.

Choose the Diagnostic That Answers the Question

Blood gasUse when ventilation, acid-base status, or severity of gas-exchange failure must be clarified.
Chest radiographUse when focal disease, air leak, foreign body pattern, pneumonia, edema, or device position is suspected.
CapnographyUseful for airway/ventilation trending, intubation confirmation, and detecting disconnection or severe airflow change.
Pulse oximetryUseful for oxygenation trend, but does not directly measure ventilation or PaCO₂.

C4 + C5 · Reduced support

The Blood Gas Confirms What the Child Was Already Telling You

Venous blood gas + clinical trend

pH 7.27 · PCO₂ 58 mmHg

The child is increasingly somnolent with reduced air movement despite ongoing oxygen therapy.

A blood gas should confirm and refine clinical reasoning. Do not allow a single laboratory value to overrule a clearly deteriorating bedside assessment.

Recognize the Child Who Is About to Lose the Airway

Independent case

4-year-old with acute respiratory illness

The child initially had tachypnea and retractions. Now the child is quiet, difficult to arouse, has shallow respirations, poor air entry, SpO₂ 89% despite oxygen, and a falling heart rate.

This is not improvement. The pattern indicates respiratory failure with impending cardiopulmonary arrest unless ventilation is restored.
