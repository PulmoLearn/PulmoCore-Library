Build the Differential Before Naming the Disease

Start with the pattern: episodic obstruction, viral small-airway disease, focal infection, chronic suppuration, aspiration, structural airway collapse, sleep-disordered breathing, or hematologic lung injury.
Episodic wheeze + triggersThink asthma or another reversible obstructive process.
Infant + viral prodromeThink bronchiolitis when diffuse wheeze/crackles and feeding difficulty are present.
Fever + focal findingsThink pneumonia when focal crackles and infiltrate align.
Daily wet coughThink CF, bronchiectasis, PCD, or another chronic suppurative disorder.
Symptoms with feedingThink chronic aspiration or swallowing dysfunction.
Snoring + pausesThink obstructive sleep apnea.

A Quiet Chest Can Be More Dangerous Than Loud Wheeze

Case A

7-year-old with status asthmaticus

After repeated bronchodilator treatments, wheezing is quieter—but air movement is now minimal. The child is drowsy and ETCO₂ is rising.

Asthma combines airway inflammation, bronchospasm, mucosal edema, and mucus production. Severe obstruction increases expiratory time, air trapping, and dynamic hyperinflation.
Late danger signs: poor air movement, fatigue, altered mental status, rising PaCO₂, cyanosis, or bradycardia.

Supported

Bronchiolitis: Treat the Physiology, Not the Label

Case B

5-month-old with viral prodrome

Diffuse crackles and wheeze, increased work of breathing, copious nasal secretions, poor feeding, and intermittent desaturation.

Mechanism

Small-airway edema, mucus, and cellular debris create uneven obstruction, air trapping, atelectasis, and increased work of breathing.

Foundation of care

Nasal suction when obstructed, hydration, oxygen for hypoxemia, and escalating respiratory support when needed.

Routine bronchodilators, corticosteroids, antibiotics, or chest physiotherapy are not appropriate for every infant with bronchiolitis.

Not Every Infection Looks the Same

Case C

Fever + focal crackles

Lobar opacity and pleuritic pain.

Case D

Paroxysmal cough

Inspiratory whoop and post-tussive emesis.

Case E

Chronic cough + weight loss

Exposure risk and persistent radiographic abnormality.

Pneumonia may be bacterial, viral, or aspiration-related. Pertussis and tuberculosis require different diagnostic, treatment, and infection-control thinking.

Chronic Wet Cough Needs an Explanation

Case F

14-year-old with cystic fibrosis

Increased cough, thicker sputum, lower FEV₁, weight loss, and reduced exercise tolerance.

CF care is multidimensional: airway clearance, inhaled therapy, respiratory cultures and targeted antibiotics, infection prevention, nutrition, pancreatic support, and trend monitoring.
Chronic disorderCore clue
BronchiectasisIrreversible airway dilation with chronic wet cough, recurrent infection, and crackles.
Primary ciliary dyskinesiaImpaired ciliary motion with chronic sinus/ear disease, wet cough, and later bronchiectasis.
Airway malaciaDynamic airway collapse causing barking cough, monophonic wheeze, or expiratory obstruction.
Chronic aspirationSymptoms with feeding, recurrent dependent infiltrates, poor growth, or wet voice.

Sometimes the Lung Complaint Starts Outside the Lung

Obstructive sleep apnea

Snoring, witnessed pauses, gasping, restless sleep, enuresis, morning headache, behavior change, or poor school performance. Polysomnography evaluates sleep-disordered breathing.

Chronic aspiration

Coughing or desaturation with feeds, wet voice, recurrent pneumonia, poor growth, or unexplained bronchiectasis. Swallow evaluation and feeding observation may identify risk.

Case G

12-year-old with sickle cell disease

Fever, chest pain, tachypnea, hypoxemia, and a new lower-lobe infiltrate.

Acute chest syndrome can progress rapidly. New pulmonary infiltrate plus fever and/or respiratory symptoms in a child with sickle cell disease should trigger acute chest syndrome management and close monitoring.

Choose the Diagnosis That Changes What You Do

Independent cases

Four children, four different mechanisms

Use the history, age, breath sounds, secretion pattern, imaging, and response to treatment to decide which problem is most likely—and what the next respiratory action should be.

Mechanism drives treatment: bronchospasm → bronchodilation; bronchiolitis → supportive care; bacterial infection → targeted antimicrobial therapy; chronic suppuration → airway clearance + culture-guided management; aspiration → protect feeding/airway; OSA → sleep evaluation; acute chest syndrome → urgent multisystem treatment.
