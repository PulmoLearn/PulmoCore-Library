Scenario 1.6 · Climbing the Support Ladder

How Much Support Does This Baby Need?

Follow a premature infant as respiratory support escalates from supplemental oxygen to CPAP, noninvasive positive pressure, and invasive ventilation. At every step, decide whether the current support is working, what problem you are trying to fix, and whether the next move should change oxygenation, ventilation, or both.

C1 · Guided

Think in a Support Ladder

Escalate only as much as needed: use the least invasive support that can maintain acceptable oxygenation, ventilation, lung recruitment, and work of breathing.
1 · OxygenIncrease inspired oxygen when the main problem is hypoxemia without significant ventilatory failure.
2 · CPAPProvide distending pressure to improve FRC and keep unstable alveoli open.
3 · NIPPVAdd intermittent positive-pressure breaths when CPAP alone does not adequately support ventilation or apnea.
4 · Invasive ventilationUse an artificial airway when noninvasive support fails or the infant cannot maintain ventilation, oxygenation, or airway protection.

C2 · Supported

The Infant Is Working Hard—But Still Breathing

Case

30 weeks · spontaneous respirations · RR 68/min · moderate retractions · SpO₂ 88% on low-flow oxygen

Breath sounds are diminished but symmetric. The infant is not apneic and is protecting the airway.

What CPAP does

Raises mean airway pressure, helps establish and maintain functional residual capacity, reduces alveolar collapse, and can decrease work of breathing.

What CPAP does not guarantee

It does not directly guarantee adequate minute ventilation. A baby can remain hypercapnic or apneic despite an open lung.

3
C3 · Supported

CPAP Helps Oxygenation, but Ventilation Is Still Failing

After CPAP

SpO₂ improves to 92%, but apnea continues and PaCO₂ rises to 61 mmHg

The infant repeatedly requires stimulation and brief mask ventilation.

NIPPV adds intermittent positive-pressure breaths on top of baseline distending pressure. It may improve ventilation and reduce apnea when CPAP alone is insufficient.
4
C4 · Reduced support

Know When Noninvasive Support Has Failed

Two hours later

Frequent apnea · pH 7.19 · PaCO₂ 72 · increasing FiO₂ requirement · worsening fatigue

Despite optimized interface fit and noninvasive settings, the infant is no longer maintaining ventilation.

Do not wait for complete collapse. Failure criteria are physiologic: worsening acidosis/hypercapnia, recurrent apnea, escalating oxygen need, severe work of breathing, hemodynamic instability, or inability to protect the airway.

C5 · Reduced support

Now Separate Oxygenation From Ventilation

On invasive ventilation, make changes based on the physiologic problem.
ProblemPrimary variables to think about
HypoxemiaFiO₂ and mean airway pressure, including PEEP and inspiratory pressure/time depending on mode.
HypercapniaMinute ventilation: tidal volume/pressure and respiratory rate, while watching for overdistention and air leak.
AtelectasisRecruitment and adequate end-expiratory lung volume.
OverdistentionReduce excessive pressure/volume and reassess lung mechanics.

C5 + C6 · Reduced support

The Ventilator Is Set—Now Watch the Baby, Not Just the Numbers

After intubation

Pressure-controlled ventilation

Chest movement is visible. Exhaled tidal volume is higher than expected for the infant’s size. PaCO₂ falls to 29 mmHg and the chest radiograph shows hyperinflation.

Ventilator injury can come from excessive pressure, excessive volume, repeated collapse and reopening, oxygen toxicity, and patient-ventilator asynchrony. The goal is adequate gas exchange with the least injurious support.

C1–C6 · Independent synthesis

Which Direction Do You Move on the Ladder?

New case

32-week infant on CPAP 6 cmH₂O

SpO₂ 93% on FiO₂ 0.28, RR 62/min, mild retractions, no apnea, pH 7.32, PaCO₂ 48 mmHg. Over the next several hours the infant becomes less tachypneic and FiO₂ falls to 0.23.

Escalation is not the only decision. When the infant is stable with improving work of breathing and decreasing oxygen need, support should be reassessed for cautious weaning rather than automatically maintained or increased.
