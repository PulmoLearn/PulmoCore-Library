The Baby Is Here. What Happens Next?

Follow one newborn from routine transition into escalating resuscitation. At each decision point, choose the next action, watch the infant’s response, and learn why effective ventilation—not rushing ahead—is the central priority in neonatal resuscitation.

First Question: Routine Transition or Resuscitation Pathway?

Start with three observations: Is the newborn term? Is there good tone? Is the newborn breathing or crying?

Routine transition

A term infant with good tone who is breathing or crying can usually remain skin-to-skin with warmth, observation, and routine assessment. Deferred cord clamping for at least 60 seconds is appropriate for most newborns who do not need immediate resuscitation.

Resuscitation pathway

Move to the warmer when breathing is absent or ineffective, tone is poor, or immediate intervention is required.

Ventilation Is the Priority

After initial steps

Baby B remains apneic · heart rate 80/min

The chest is still, and spontaneous respirations have not begun.

Begin positive-pressure ventilation when the newborn is apneic, gasping, or has a heart rate below 100/min after initial steps.

PPV target

The 2025 framework used in this lesson permits 30–60 inflations/min. Adjust pressure to produce visible chest movement.

Best sign PPV is working

A rising heart rate is the most important response. Visible chest movement helps confirm that the lungs are being inflated.

Monitor the Response and Decide When an Advanced Airway Is Needed

After corrective steps

Chest movement improves, but mask ventilation remains difficult

The team prepares to secure a more reliable airway.

Preductal SpO₂

Place the pulse-oximetry sensor on the right hand or wrist.

Heart rate

ECG provides the fastest reliable heart-rate assessment during advanced resuscitation and is recommended before chest compressions.

Endotracheal tube

Use when prolonged ventilation is expected, mask ventilation remains ineffective, suction below the cords is needed for suspected obstruction, or compressions are required.

Laryngeal mask

An alternative when face-mask ventilation is unsuccessful and intubation is not feasible or unsuccessful in an appropriately sized infant.

Now the Heart Rate Is Below 60—But Only After Effective Ventilation

After intubation

Chest moves with each breath · HR 48/min after 30 seconds of effective ventilation

The lungs are now being inflated reliably, but severe bradycardia persists.

Begin chest compressions when the heart rate remains below 60/min despite at least 30 seconds of ventilation that visibly inflates the lungs.

If the heart rate remains below 60/min, give epinephrine through an intravascular route, preferably an umbilical venous catheter during delivery-room resuscitation. Consider volume expansion when blood loss or shock is suspected.

Do Not Let Meconium Distract You From the Priority

New delivery

Meconium-stained fluid · infant apneic · HR 90/min

The baby is nonvigorous after positioning and stimulation.

Meconium does not change the priority: establish ventilation. Routine endotracheal suctioning is not recommended solely because the infant is nonvigorous and meconium-stained. Intubation and tracheal suction may be considered when airway obstruction prevents effective ventilation.
Recovery is not the end of the resuscitation. Continue assessment of heart rate, breathing, oxygenation, temperature, perfusion, glucose/metabolic stability, and neurologic status, and communicate the course of resuscitation clearly during handoff.
