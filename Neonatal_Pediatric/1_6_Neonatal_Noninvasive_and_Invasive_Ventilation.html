Neonatal Noninvasive and Invasive Ventilation

Select the least invasive support that meets the infant’s needs, then adjust pressure, volume, rate, PEEP, inspiratory time, and oxygen using physiology rather than habit.

Competency Priorities

What You Must Be Able to Apply

Select the least invasive support
Match oxygen, HFNC, CPAP, and NIPPV to work of breathing, apnea, oxygen need, and disease pattern.
Choose initial controls
Set rate, PIP or target tidal volume, PEEP, inspiratory time, trigger, and FiO₂ based on size and physiology.
Separate oxygenation from ventilation
Use FiO₂ and mean airway pressure for oxygenation; use minute ventilation for CO₂ control.
Troubleshoot before turning knobs
Assess the infant, airway, circuit, device, and data before changing support.
Escalation Framework

Use the Lowest Level of Support That Achieves the Goal

Oxygen→ HFNC→ CPAP→ NIPPV→ Intubation→ Invasive ventilation

The pathway is not rigid. An apneic infant with severe bradycardia may need immediate intubation, while a spontaneously breathing premature infant with RDS may remain on CPAP and receive surfactant through a minimally invasive technique.

Reassess after every escalation: respiratory effort, chest movement, oxygen need, blood gases, apnea burden, perfusion, temperature, interface fit, and abdominal distention.
Preserve Spontaneous Breathing

Noninvasive Support Splints the Airway and Recruits Lung Volume

SupportPrimary roleImportant limitations
Low-flow oxygenRaises inspired oxygen when work of breathing and ventilation are otherwise adequate.Does not provide reliable distending pressure or ventilatory support.
HFNCProvides heated humidified flow, reduces nasopharyngeal dead space, and may create variable distending pressure.Pressure is not directly controlled and depends on flow, leak, cannula fit, and infant size.
CPAPProvides continuous distending pressure to maintain FRC, reduce atelectasis, and stabilize the chest wall.Does not deliver mandatory breaths; ineffective for persistent apnea without backup support.
NIPPVAdds intermittent positive-pressure breaths above PEEP/CPAP to support ventilation and reduce apnea.Leaks, gastric distention, interface injury, and poor synchrony can reduce effectiveness.

When Noninvasive Support Is Not Enough

Intubate for Failure of Ventilation, Oxygenation, Airway Protection, or Respiratory Drive

Common indications
Recurrent severe apnea, worsening acidosis, escalating oxygen/pressure need, exhaustion, shock, poor airway protection, or need for a procedure.
Before intubation
Prepare size-appropriate ETT, laryngoscope, suction, CO₂ detector, securement, monitoring, medications, and backup airway plan.

After placement, verify exhaled CO₂, chest movement, bilateral breath sounds, heart-rate response, depth marking, and radiographic position. Reassess after any movement or sudden deterioration.

Ventilator Language

Each Control Changes a Specific Physiologic Variable

ControlPrimary effectClinical caution
FiO₂Changes inspired oxygen concentration.Use the lowest concentration that meets the ordered saturation target.
PEEPMaintains end-expiratory lung volume and improves recruitment.Too low promotes atelectasis; too high may overdistend lung and impair venous return.
PIP or inspiratory pressureControls pressure gradient and influences delivered tidal volume in pressure ventilation.Tidal volume varies as compliance, resistance, and leak change.
Target tidal volumeSets the desired exhaled volume in volume-targeted ventilation.Monitor pressure required to achieve the target and avoid excessive volume.
RateChanges mandatory breath frequency and minute ventilation.Excessive rate may shorten exhalation and create gas trapping.
Inspiratory timeChanges time available for filling and contributes to mean airway pressure.Match disease time constants and allow adequate exhalation.
Trigger sensitivityDetermines how easily patient effort initiates a supported breath.Too insensitive causes missed efforts; too sensitive causes auto-triggering.

Start, Then Reassess

Initial Settings Should Match Size, Disease, and Clinical Goal

There is no single neonatal setting package. Begin with a lung-protective strategy, then promptly evaluate chest movement, exhaled tidal volume, pressure requirement, blood gases, oxygenation, hemodynamics, and synchrony.

Low-compliance disease
RDS often needs adequate PEEP and recruitment while limiting excessive tidal volume and pressure.
High-resistance or obstructive disease
MAS may require enough inspiratory time for filling and enough expiratory time to avoid air trapping.
Apnea with relatively normal lungs
Use the minimum pressure and oxygen needed while providing reliable backup ventilation.
Air leak risk
Reduce unnecessary pressure and volume while maintaining adequate gas exchange.

Separate the Problem

CO₂ Is Controlled by Alveolar Ventilation; Oxygenation Depends on FiO₂ and Lung Recruitment

ProblemPrimary controls to evaluate
High PaCO₂ with low exhaled tidal volumeAirway patency, leak, inspiratory pressure or target tidal volume, rate, synchrony, and dead space.
Low PaCO₂Reduce excessive minute ventilation by lowering rate, pressure, or tidal-volume target as appropriate.
Hypoxemia with atelectasisOptimize interface/airway, PEEP, mean airway pressure, recruitment, positioning, and FiO₂.
Hypoxemia with hyperinflationAvoid reflexively increasing PEEP; assess air trapping, overdistention, PPHN, air leak, and perfusion.

Prevent Injury

Neonatal Ventilation Is a Balance Between Recruitment and Overdistention

Volutrauma
Injury caused by excessive tidal volume and alveolar stretch.
Barotrauma
Injury associated with excessive pressure and air leak.
Atelectrauma
Injury caused by repetitive opening and collapse of unstable lung units.

Use exhaled tidal volume, pressure trends, compliance, chest movement, blood gases, oxygen need, and imaging to find the lowest effective support. Maintain heated humidification, protect the skin and nasal septum, reduce circuit dead space, avoid unnecessary disconnections, and reassess sedation and synchrony.

Key Takeaways

Support should match the deficit
Oxygen treats oxygen concentration, CPAP recruits lung volume, and NIPPV adds ventilatory assistance.
Use physiology to choose settings
PEEP supports FRC, pressure or tidal volume affects breath size, and rate changes minute ventilation.
Separate oxygenation and ventilation
Do not use FiO₂ to treat hypercapnia or increase ventilation blindly for hypoxemia.
Troubleshoot the system
Patient, airway, circuit, ventilator, and data must all be assessed before changing support.
