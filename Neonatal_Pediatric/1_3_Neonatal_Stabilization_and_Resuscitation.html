Newborn Chain of Care

Most Newborns Need Supportive Transition, Not Resuscitation

A newborn who is term, has good tone, and is breathing or crying can usually remain with the parent for skin-to-skin care, temperature support, ongoing observation, and routine assessment. Deferred cord clamping for at least 60 seconds is appropriate for most newborns who do not need immediate resuscitation.

Routine transition
Warmth, skin-to-skin contact, drying when needed, airway positioning, and observation.
Resuscitation pathway
Move to a warmer when breathing is absent or ineffective, tone is poor, or immediate intervention is required.
Do not delay ventilation for scoring. APGAR describes the infant’s condition but does not determine when PPV should begin.
First Response

Warm, Position, Clear Only When Needed, Dry, and Stimulate

Initial care focuses on temperature, airway position, and breathing. Suction is not routine. Clear the mouth or nose only when secretions obstruct breathing or ventilation.

StepPurpose
Provide warmthPrevent cold stress, increased oxygen consumption, hypoglycemia, and acidosis.
Position the airwayPlace the head in a neutral or slightly extended “sniffing” position.
Clear secretions only if neededRemove visible obstruction without delaying ventilation.
Dry and stimulatePromote breathing while limiting heat loss; use gentle rubbing of the back or soles.

Most Important Intervention

Effective Ventilation Is the Priority in Neonatal Resuscitation

Begin PPV when the newborn is apneic, gasping, or has a heart rate below 100/min after initial steps. The 2025 guideline permits a ventilation rate of 30–60 inflations per minute. Adjust pressure to achieve visible chest movement and a rising heart rate.

Best sign of success
Heart rate rises after the lungs are inflated.
Visual sign
Gentle bilateral chest movement with each inflation.
Persistent bradycardia
Assume ineffective ventilation until proven otherwise.

When the Chest Does Not Move

Correct Ventilation Before Escalating to Compressions

If PPV does not produce chest movement or a heart-rate increase, perform ventilation corrective steps. The familiar mnemonic is MR SOPA.

Titrate, Do Not Guess

Use Preductal Oxygen Saturation and Heart Rate to Guide Care

Place the pulse-oximetry sensor on the right hand or wrist to obtain a preductal measurement. ECG provides the fastest reliable heart-rate assessment when advanced resuscitation is underway and is recommended before chest compressions.

SituationOxygen approach
Term or late-preterm infant beginning PPVStart with room air and titrate to preductal saturation targets and clinical response.
Very preterm infant beginning respiratory supportUse blended oxygen according to local protocol and titrate carefully.
Chest compressionsUse 100% oxygen during compressions, then reduce promptly once the heart rate recovers.
Spontaneously breathing infant with labored breathing or persistent cyanosisApply pulse oximetry; consider CPAP and supplemental oxygen as indicated.

Secure Ventilation

An Advanced Airway Improves Reliability When Mask Ventilation Fails

Use an endotracheal tube when prolonged ventilation is expected, mask ventilation remains ineffective, suction below the vocal cords is needed for suspected obstruction, or chest compressions are required. A laryngeal mask is an alternative when face-mask ventilation is unsuccessful and intubation is not feasible or unsuccessful in an appropriately sized infant.

Verify placement
Use exhaled CO₂ detection with clinical assessment, chest movement, bilateral breath sounds, and rising heart rate.
Reassess depth and patency
Unexpected bradycardia, absent chest movement, asymmetric breath sounds, or low exhaled CO₂ requires immediate evaluation.
Advanced Resuscitation

Begin Compressions Only After Ventilation Inflates the Lungs

Start chest compressions when the heart rate remains below 60/min despite at least 30 seconds of ventilation that visibly inflates the lungs. Use an advanced airway, coordinate compressions and ventilations at a 3:1 ratio, and use 100% oxygen during compressions.

Technique
Two-thumb encircling-hands technique over the lower third of the sternum.
Rhythm
Three compressions followed by one ventilation, coordinated smoothly.
Reassess
Check the heart rate after approximately 60 seconds of coordinated compressions and ventilation.

If the heart rate remains below 60/min, give epinephrine through an intravascular route, preferably an umbilical venous catheter during delivery-room resuscitation. Consider volume expansion when blood loss or shock is suspected.

Special Situation

Meconium Does Not Change the Priority: Establish Ventilation

Routine endotracheal suctioning is not recommended solely because an infant is nonvigorous and meconium-stained. Begin the standard initial steps and PPV when indicated. Intubation and tracheal suction may be considered when airway obstruction prevents effective ventilation.

Key Takeaways

Ventilation comes first
Apnea, gasping, and bradycardia usually improve when the lungs are inflated effectively.
Correct before escalating
Use MR SOPA and an advanced airway before deciding ventilation has failed.
Compressions require confirmed lung inflation
Start at heart rate below 60/min after 30 seconds of effective ventilation.
Recovery is not the endpoint
Continue thermal, respiratory, circulatory, metabolic, neurologic, and family-centered care.
