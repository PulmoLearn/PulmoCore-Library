Cardiopulmonary Coupling

Respiratory Care Can Improve—or Destabilize—Cardiac Physiology

Congenital heart disease changes pulmonary blood flow, systemic blood flow, mixing, and oxygen delivery. The respiratory therapist must know whether the child’s circulation is pulmonary-flow dependent, systemic-flow dependent, or mixing dependent before aggressively changing oxygen or pressure. Pulmonary hypertension adds another cardiopulmonary mechanism: elevated pulmonary vascular resistance can limit pulmonary blood flow, increase right-heart strain, and worsen hypoxemia, so oxygenation, ventilation, acid-base status, perfusion, and hemodynamic response must be interpreted together.

PatternRespiratory relevance
Excess pulmonary blood flowTachypnea, retractions, pulmonary edema, hepatomegaly, and poor growth; excessive oxygen may further lower pulmonary vascular resistance.
Reduced pulmonary blood flowCyanosis and low pulmonary blood flow; oxygen may help only if pulmonary vasoconstriction is contributing.
Ductal-dependent systemic circulationClosing ductus can reduce systemic perfusion despite acceptable SpO₂.
Mixing lesionSystemic oxygenation depends on adequate mixing rather than normal saturation targets.
Single-ventricle physiologyBalance between pulmonary and systemic blood flow is critical; excessive ventilation or oxygen can disturb that balance.

Oxygen Delivery

Shock Is Inadequate Tissue Oxygen Delivery

TypeTypical clues
HypovolemicTachycardia, cool extremities, narrow pulse pressure, delayed refill, low urine output, history of fluid loss or bleeding.
Distributive / septicWarm or cool shock, vasodilation or vasoconstriction, fever or hypothermia, altered mental status, rising lactate.
CardiogenicPoor perfusion with hepatomegaly, pulmonary edema, gallop rhythm, rising venous pressure, or known heart disease.
ObstructiveTension pneumothorax, tamponade, or massive pulmonary embolic obstruction limiting cardiac filling or output.

Assess heart rate, pulse quality, capillary refill, blood pressure, urine output, lactate, mental status, temperature gradient, and NIRS when available. Hypotension is a late pediatric finding.

Integrate multisystem data into the respiratory assessment. Do not interpret SpO₂, blood gases, or ventilator data in isolation. Falling urine output may support worsening perfusion; glucose, electrolytes, acid-base status, or other metabolic abnormalities can alter respiratory drive and muscle performance; hemoglobin affects arterial oxygen content even when saturation looks acceptable; and bleeding or other hematologic problems can reduce oxygen delivery or complicate invasive respiratory care.

Differentiate Pediatric Shock

A child with congenital heart disease develops tachycardia, cool extremities, hepatomegaly, worsening crackles, delayed refill, and low urine output after a large fluid bolus.

Choose the best interpretation.
Protect the Brain and Airway

Neurologic Disease Can Impair Drive, Tone, and Airway Protection

Central drive failure
Brain injury, seizures, sedatives, infection, or metabolic disease may reduce respiratory effort.
Airway protection failure
Weak cough, poor gag, cranial nerve dysfunction, or altered consciousness increases aspiration risk.

During status epilepticus or severe brain injury, priorities include oxygenation, ventilation, glucose, temperature, seizure control, and avoidance of hypotension. Brief controlled hyperventilation is reserved for specific acute herniation situations—not routine management.

Medication classRespiratory-care implication to anticipate
SedativesMay reduce respiratory drive, mental status, airway tone, and patient-ventilator interaction. Reassess ventilation, airway protection, CO₂, and support needs after a change.
AnalgesicsAdequate pain control can improve splinting and ventilator tolerance, while excessive respiratory/CNS depression can worsen hypoventilation or airway protection. Reassess response rather than assuming distress is purely pulmonary.
Vasoactive agentsChanges in vascular tone, cardiac output, and blood pressure alter perfusion and oxygen delivery. Interpret SpO₂ and ventilator response together with hemodynamics, urine output, lactate, and bedside perfusion.
Neuromuscular blockade or other critical-care medicationsLoss of spontaneous movement or respiratory effort changes the assessment. Ensure adequate ventilatory support and continue monitoring gas exchange, hemodynamics, airway security, and the underlying disease process.

Thoracic Trauma

Airway, Breathing, and Circulation Problems May Coexist

InjuryKey clues and priority
Pulmonary contusionWorsening hypoxemia and infiltrates after blunt trauma; support oxygenation and use lung-protective ventilation if needed.
Tension pneumothoraxSudden unilateral decreased breath sounds, hypotension, distended neck veins, or tracheal shift; decompress immediately.
HemothoraxDecreased breath sounds, dullness, shock, and pleural opacity; restore volume and drain the chest.
Flail chestParadoxical chest movement with underlying contusion; provide analgesia, pulmonary hygiene, and ventilatory support when needed.
Diaphragmatic injuryAbdominal contents in the chest, respiratory distress, and reduced breath sounds; avoid mask overdistention and prepare surgery.

Brain and Spine Protection

Ventilation Must Support Cerebral and Spinal Perfusion

After traumatic brain injury, avoid hypoxemia and hypotension. Maintain appropriate ventilation without routine aggressive hyperventilation. Excessive positive pressure can reduce venous return and cerebral perfusion when circulation is unstable.

Cervical spine injury
Maintain alignment, use jaw thrust when appropriate, and anticipate diaphragmatic weakness with high lesions.
Neurogenic shock
Hypotension with relative bradycardia and warm skin may follow spinal cord injury.
Extracorporeal Support

ECMO Supports Gas Exchange, Circulation, or Both

ModePrimary supportTypical use
VV ECMOGas exchange supportSevere respiratory failure with adequate cardiac function.
VA ECMOCardiac and respiratory supportCardiogenic shock, cardiac arrest, or combined cardiorespiratory failure.

During ECMO, continue lung-protective ventilation, secretion management, humidification, airway security, infection prevention, and monitoring for bleeding, thrombosis, hemolysis, differential hypoxemia, and circuit failure.

Team-Based Critical Care

Stabilization Must Survive the Move

Before transport, secure the airway, confirm ventilator and chest-drain function, verify gas and battery duration, stabilize perfusion, control temperature, protect lines, define emergency roles, and communicate disease-specific risks.

Communicate deterioration as an assessment plus recommendation. Report what changed, the objective evidence, the likely physiologic mechanism, what has already been done, the response, and the specific care-plan change or escalation you recommend. Examples include calling for immediate decompression with tension physiology, requesting airway/ventilatory escalation for progressive neuromuscular failure, or recommending hemodynamic reassessment when respiratory interventions worsen perfusion.
Never transport an unresolved instability. A marginal airway, inadequate gas supply, uncontrolled shock, or unrecognized tension pneumothorax becomes more dangerous once access is limited.

Key Takeaways

Integrate the whole patient
Cardiac output, hemoglobin, perfusion, neurologic status, renal/metabolic findings, muscle strength, and gas exchange all change respiratory decisions.
Neuromuscular decline can be silent
Follow vital capacity, inspiratory pressure, cough flow, CO₂, bulbar function, and the trend—not SpO₂ alone.
Protect brain and circulation while supporting the lungs
Avoid hypoxemia, harmful hyperventilation, hypotension, and unnecessary positive pressure; reassess after medication or ventilator changes.
ECMO and team care remain respiratory care
Differentiate VV from VA support, continue lung protection and airway care, and clearly communicate deterioration plus the recommended next step.
