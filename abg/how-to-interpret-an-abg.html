How to Interpret an ABG

Arterial blood gas interpretation is one of the most important skills for respiratory therapists. Whether you are preparing for clinicals, studying for the TMC, or reviewing for the CSE, this guide walks you through a systematic process for interpreting any ABG.

Acid-base status
Identify acidotic, alkalotic, or normal pH.
Compensation
Recognize no, partial, or full compensation.
Oxygenation
Classify PaO₂ severity and build the final interpretation.

Step 1

Evaluate pH

Start by deciding whether the blood is acidotic, alkalotic, or normal. Do this before looking at PaCO₂ or HCO₃.

pH ValueInterpretation
Less than 7.35Acidemia (low blood pH)
7.35–7.45Normal pH range
Greater than 7.45Alkalemia (high blood pH)
Example: pH 7.28 is acidotic because it is below 7.35.
Coming in Learning Step 4: A pH within 7.35–7.45 does not necessarily mean the ABG is normal. If PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ are both abnormal, inspect the underlying pattern.
Step 2

Determine Respiratory or Metabolic Cause

After you know the pH direction, compare it to PaCO₂ and HCO₃. For straightforward single-disorder examples, identify the value whose change explains the pH direction. More complex ABGs can contain mixed disorders.

35–45 mmHg
Normal HCO₃:
22–26 mEq/L

Board Prep Shortcut

pH and PaCO₂ move opposite directions in respiratory disorders. pH and HCO₃ move the same direction in metabolic disorders.

Interpretation Decision 3 · Guided Learning Step 3

Assess Compensation

Compensation is the body's response to a primary acid-base disorder: the lungs change ventilation in metabolic disorders, while the kidneys adjust bicarbonate handling in respiratory disorders.

The pH +1 / pH +2 screening shortcut

pH +1: The pH and only one of PaCO₂ or HCO₃⁻ are outside their reference ranges. This suggests an uncompensated pattern in simple teaching examples.
pH +2: Both PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ are abnormal. Check the direction of the second change to see whether it fits compensation; two abnormal values alone do not prove compensation.

With an abnormal pH and appropriate opposing-system change, the introductory label is partial compensation. If pH returns to 7.35–7.45 with an appropriate pattern, the introductory label is full compensation. Real ABGs may have mixed disorders, so expected compensation calculations and clinical context are needed for definitive interpretation.

No evident compensation

The opposing system remains in range in a simple teaching example.

Partial compensation pattern

The opposing system changes in the expected direction; pH remains outside the reference range.

Full compensation pattern

The opposing system changes appropriately; pH falls within the reference range.

ABG (pH / PaCO₂ / HCO₃⁻)Teaching patternReason
7.23 / 60 / 24Respiratory acidosis without evident compensationPaCO₂ is elevated; HCO₃⁻ remains in range.
7.33 / 58 / 30Partially compensated respiratory acidosis patternElevated HCO₃⁻ offsets elevated PaCO₂, but pH remains low.
7.38 / 56 / 32Fully compensated respiratory acidosis patternpH is in range on the acidic side of 7.40; both values are elevated.
Clinical note: A pH +2 pattern can also reflect a mixed disorder. For metabolic disorders, compare measured PaCO₂ with the expected respiratory response; for respiratory disorders, compare bicarbonate with the expected acute or chronic renal response.
Guided Learning Step 4

When the pH Looks Normal: Use 7.40

For straightforward fully compensated teaching examples, use 7.40 as a midpoint to identify the underlying direction even when pH falls inside the normal range.

7.35–7.39: Acidic side of normal; look for an underlying acidosis pattern.
7.41–7.45: Alkaline side of normal; look for an underlying alkalosis pattern.
pH / PaCO₂ / HCO₃⁻Interpretation
7.40 / 40 / 24All three values normal
7.36 / 55 / 30Fully compensated respiratory acidosis teaching pattern
7.43 / 30 / 19Fully compensated respiratory alkalosis teaching pattern

Important: The midpoint is a beginner shortcut, not a diagnostic rule. At pH 7.40, or when findings conflict, do not force a single diagnosis. Evaluate expected compensation and possible mixed disorders.

Step 4

Evaluate Oxygenation

Acid-base status and oxygenation are related, but they are not the same thing. After interpreting pH, PaCO₂, and HCO₃, assess PaO₂ separately.

PaO₂Oxygenation Interpretation
80–100 mmHgNormal oxygenation
60–79 mmHgMild hypoxemia
40–59 mmHgModerate hypoxemia
Less than 40 mmHgSevere hypoxemia
Example: PaO₂ 55 mmHg is moderate hypoxemia.

Clinical context: These are simplified adult sea-level reference bands. Interpret PaO₂ alongside age, altitude, inspired oxygen concentration (FiO₂), and the patient's clinical status; a PaO₂ on supplemental oxygen is not equivalent to the same value on room air.

Common Student Mistakes

What to Avoid When Reading ABGs

Starting with PaCO₂: Always classify pH first so you know which direction the problem is moving.
Ignoring compensation: Compensation patterns help you identify the body’s response. Do not assume they establish whether a disorder is acute or chronic; that requires additional assessment.
Forgetting oxygenation: A patient can have a clear acid-base disorder and still need a separate oxygenation assessment.
Guessing from one value: Use the same five-step method every time.
