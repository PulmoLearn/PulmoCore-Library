Metabolic Acidosis Explained

Metabolic acidosis is an acid-base disorder caused by a primary decrease in bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻). The classic pattern is low pH with low HCO₃⁻. The lungs respond by increasing ventilation, lowering PaCO₂ to limit the fall in pH. Learn the pattern here, then apply it in PulmoLearn’s five guided ABG Steps.

Core Metabolic Acidosis Pattern

Metabolic acidosis occurs when bicarbonate is low enough to push the pH below normal. Since HCO₃ is the metabolic component, the disorder is metabolic when pH and HCO₃ move in the same direction.

pH:
Low, below 7.35
HCO₃:
Low, below 22 mEq/L
PaCO₂:
May be low if respiratory compensation is present
Memory cue:
pH and HCO₃ move in the same direction

Recognize Compensation

In metabolic acidosis, the lungs respond by increasing alveolar ventilation. This lowers PaCO₂ and helps bring pH toward normal. Respiratory compensation does not eliminate the underlying metabolic problem.

Low HCO₃⁻ → low pH → increased ventilation → lower PaCO₂

Start with the pH +1 / pH +2 shortcut

pH +1 pattern:
pH and HCO₃⁻ are abnormal while PaCO₂ remains within its reference range. In our introductory framework, this is an uncompensated pattern.
pH +2 pattern:
pH, HCO₃⁻, and PaCO₂ are abnormal. A lower PaCO₂ may indicate respiratory compensation, but first verify that it is appropriate.
Partial compensation:
pH remains below 7.35 while PaCO₂ has decreased in the expected direction.
Normal-range pH:
A pH in the reference range does not automatically mean all values are normal. Evaluate the full ABG and clinical context.

Check expected compensation with Winter’s formula

When evaluating a real patient, calculate the expected PaCO₂: 1.5 × HCO₃⁻ + 8 (± 2 mmHg). A measured PaCO₂ above the expected range suggests an additional respiratory acidosis; below the expected range suggests an additional respiratory alkalosis.

Clinical nuance: The pH +1 / pH +2 method is a beginner screening aid—not a definitive way to rule out mixed disorders. Compensation can begin even while PaCO₂ is within the population reference range, and uncomplicated metabolic acidosis usually does not fully normalize pH.

Common Causes of Metabolic Acidosis

Metabolic acidosis often occurs when the body gains acid, loses bicarbonate, or cannot clear acid effectively.

CauseWhy It Causes AcidosisRT Clinical Connection
Diabetic ketoacidosisKetone production increases acid load.May cause deep, rapid Kussmaul respirations.
Lactic acidosisPoor perfusion or hypoxia increases lactate.Look for shock, sepsis, hypoxemia, or severe work of breathing.
Renal failureKidneys cannot excrete acid effectively.May coexist with fluid overload or pulmonary edema.
DiarrheaBicarbonate is lost through the GI tract.Assess dehydration, perfusion, and respiratory compensation.
Toxin ingestionSome toxins create acid metabolites.Requires urgent team escalation and monitoring.

Example ABG: Work Through the Method

pH 7.29
PaCO₂ 30 mmHg
HCO₃⁻ 14 mEq/L
PaO₂ 92 mmHg (room air)

1. Start with pH: 7.29 indicates acidemia.

2. Find the primary disorder: HCO₃⁻ is low and explains the acidotic direction → metabolic acidosis.

3. Assess compensation: PaCO₂ is low, opposing the primary metabolic acidosis. This is a pH +2 pattern with pH still outside the normal range.

4. Verify the compensation: Winter’s formula predicts PaCO₂ = 1.5 × 14 + 8 = 29 ± 2 mmHg. Measured PaCO₂ is 30, within the expected range.

5. Assess oxygenation: PaO₂ 92 mmHg is within the typical adult room-air reference range; interpretation also depends on age, altitude, and FiO₂.

Complete introductory interpretation:
Metabolic acidosis with appropriate respiratory compensation (partially compensated pattern) and normal oxygenation on room air.

Common Student Mistakes

Calling it respiratory because PaCO₂ is abnormal
PaCO₂ may be abnormal because it is compensating.
Ignoring the bicarbonate
The low HCO₃ is the primary metabolic clue.
Forgetting oxygenation
Always assess PaO₂ separately after acid-base status.
Missing mixed disorders
If both PaCO₂ and HCO₃ are pushing pH in the same direction, think mixed disorder.
