Metabolic Alkalosis Explained

Metabolic alkalosis is an acid-base disorder caused by a primary increase in bicarbonate. For respiratory therapy students, the key pattern is a high pH with a high HCO₃. The lungs may compensate by decreasing ventilation, but oxygenation and ventilation must always be assessed carefully.

High HCO₃

Core Metabolic Alkalosis Pattern

Metabolic alkalosis occurs when bicarbonate is high enough to push the pH above normal. Since HCO₃ is the metabolic component, the disorder is metabolic when pH and HCO₃ move in the same direction.

pH:
High, above 7.45
HCO₃:
High, above 26 mEq/L
PaCO₂:
May be high if respiratory compensation is present
Memory cue:
pH and HCO₃ move in the same direction

Normal Values to Know

ABG ValueNormal RangeRole
pH7.35–7.45Determines acidotic, alkalotic, or normal status.
PaCO₂35–45 mmHgRespiratory component.
HCO₃22–26 mEq/LMetabolic component.
PaO₂80–100 mmHgOxygenation status.

How Compensation Works

In metabolic alkalosis, the respiratory system reduces ventilation and retains CO₂, helping move pH toward normal. This response is limited because hypoventilation can reduce oxygenation.

Primary metabolic alkalosis → ↑ HCO₃⁻ → ↑ pH → compensatory ↑ PaCO₂

Connect to the Guided Learning Steps: pH +1 / pH +2

pH +1 (introductory pattern)
pH is alkalemic, HCO₃⁻ is high, and PaCO₂ remains in range. This is an uncompensated pattern in the introductory method.
pH +2, pH still high
Both HCO₃⁻ and PaCO₂ are elevated in the expected directions. This suggests partial respiratory compensation.
pH +2, pH in range
Both values remain abnormal, but pH is 7.35–7.45. A value above 7.40 can point toward an underlying alkalosis in a straightforward compensated example.
Important limitation
Two abnormal values do not prove simple compensation. Mixed acid-base disorders can produce similar patterns.

Check Whether PaCO₂ Fits Expected Compensation

A common estimate in metabolic alkalosis is expected PaCO₂ ≈ 40 + 0.7 × (HCO₃⁻ − 24) mmHg, with an approximate ±5 mmHg range. Compare the measured value with this estimate and assess the clinical context; a large discrepancy suggests a possible additional respiratory disorder.

If HCO₃⁻ = 36: expected PaCO₂ ≈ 40 + 0.7 × 12 = 48.4 mmHg (approximately 43–53)

These shortcuts are teaching aids. Evaluate the patient, oxygenation, and other available laboratory findings before concluding that compensation is appropriate.

Common Causes of Metabolic Alkalosis

Metabolic alkalosis often occurs when the body loses acid, gains bicarbonate, or develops electrolyte and volume changes that maintain alkalosis.

CauseWhy It Causes AlkalosisRT Clinical Connection
Vomiting or gastric suctionLoss of gastric acid raises bicarbonate effect.Assess volume status and clinical context.
Diuretic therapyCan cause volume contraction and electrolyte shifts.Common in heart failure or fluid overload patients.
Excess bicarbonate administrationRaises metabolic base load.Review medication and treatment history.

Example ABG: Put the Method Together

pH 7.49
PaCO₂ 48 mmHg
HCO₃⁻ 36 mEq/L
PaO₂ 84 mmHg (room air)

1. pH: 7.49 is alkalemic.

2. Primary disorder: HCO₃⁻ is elevated, matching the alkalemic direction. Primary metabolic alkalosis.

3. Compensation: PaCO₂ is elevated in the appropriate compensatory direction. Both values are abnormal (pH +2), while pH remains high: a partially compensated pattern.

4. Expected response: Predicted PaCO₂ ≈ 48 mmHg (roughly 43–53); measured PaCO₂ is 48, consistent with expected respiratory compensation.

5. Oxygenation: PaO₂ 84 mmHg is within the introductory normal range of 80–100 mmHg on room air; clinical interpretation also depends on age, altitude, and inspired oxygen.

Complete interpretation:
Partially compensated metabolic alkalosis with oxygenation in the reference range.

Clinical connection: Consider vomiting or gastric suction, diuretic use, hypokalemia, volume depletion, and post-hypercapnic alkalosis when appropriate.

Common Student Mistakes

Calling it respiratory because PaCO₂ is high
High PaCO₂ may be compensating for high bicarbonate.
Ignoring vomiting or diuretic history
Clinical history often makes the ABG pattern easier to recognize.
Forgetting oxygenation
Compensatory hypoventilation may affect oxygenation, so always check PaO₂.
Missing post-hypercapnic alkalosis
Patients with chronic CO₂ retention may keep elevated HCO₃ after PaCO₂ is lowered quickly.
