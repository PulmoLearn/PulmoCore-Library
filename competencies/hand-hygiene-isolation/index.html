What You Need to Know Before You Practice

The goal is not to memorize isolated rules. It is to understand what you are trying to prevent: carrying organisms to the patient, to yourself, or to the next patient.

🛡️

STANDARD PRECAUTIONS

Every Patient — But PPE Depends on the Task

Standard Precautions apply to every patient. Hand hygiene is always part of the plan, but gloves are task-based. Wear gloves when the planned task may involve blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, non-intact skin, or contaminated equipment.

Compare: checking a pulse on intact skin does not automatically require gloves. Suctioning respiratory secretions does.
🧴

HAND HYGIENE

Choose the Method That Fits the Situation

When hands are not visibly soiled, alcohol-based hand rub is generally preferred for routine clinical hand hygiene. Use soap and water when hands are visibly soiled or when a specific clinical or facility indication calls for washing.

Not visibly soiled Alcohol-based hand rub Visible soil / special indication Soap & water
Why? The point is to remove or kill organisms at the moment hand hygiene is indicated. Routinely doing both washing and alcohol at every opportunity is not necessary.
🧤

GLOVES

Gloves Are a Barrier — Not a Substitute for Clean Hands

Clean hands→ Gloves if indicated→ Patient care→ Remove gloves→ Clean hands
Why clean your hands after removing gloves? Hands may become contaminated during glove removal, and microscopic glove defects can occur. Treat glove removal as a new hand-hygiene opportunity.
🚪

CONTACT PRECAUTIONS

Think Beyond What You Wear

Contact Precautions use gown + gloves to reduce transfer from the patient and environment. Equipment can also carry organisms from room to room.

🩺 Single-patient-use stethoscope
Why single-patient-use equipment? Keeping frequently used equipment with one patient reduces the chance of carrying organisms to another patient environment.
😷

TRANSMISSION-BASED PRECAUTIONS

Choose the Sign by Thinking Through the Patient Case

Do not choose the sign by memorizing a poster alone. Look at the symptoms, the suspected or confirmed disease, how the organism is likely transmitted, and whether there is a formal isolation order.

1. SymptomsCough? Sneezing? Diarrhea? Draining wound?
2. Disease cluesTB, influenza, C. difficile, RSV, MRSA, etc.
3. Route of spreadAirborne, droplet, contact, or no added isolation

DOFFING

The Order Matters Because the Outside of PPE Is Contaminated

When PPE has done its job, the outside surfaces may be contaminated. Doffing is designed to keep those surfaces away from your skin, clothing, eyes, nose, and mouth.

1GlovesHighest hand-contact contamination risk
→
2Eye protectionHandle from the back or straps
→
3GownTurn contaminated surface inward
→
4Mask / respiratorDo not touch the front
→
5Hand hygieneFinish with clean hands
Important: Perform hand hygiene between removal steps if your hands become contaminated. For airborne isolation, remove the respirator after leaving the room and closing the door.

This lesson uses one CDC example doffing sequence. Facilities may use an approved alternative sequence designed to prevent self-contamination.
