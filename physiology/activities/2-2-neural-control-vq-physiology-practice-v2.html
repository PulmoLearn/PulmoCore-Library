Neural Control of Ventilation & V/Q Matching

Breathing works only when the entire chain succeeds: the brain generates a drive → receptors modify it → nerves carry it → muscles create pressure → the lungs move gas. Follow a Rise in CO₂ from the Blood to a Bigger Breath

Big idea: when PaCO₂ rises, the respiratory control system responds through a negative-feedback loop that increases alveolar ventilation so more CO₂ is exhaled and PaCO₂ moves back toward normal.

1. The chemical signal begins with CO₂

When PaCO₂ rises in arterial blood, more CO₂ diffuses across the blood-brain barrier into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).

CO₂ ↑ in blood → CO₂ ↑ in CSF

2. CO₂ changes CSF chemistry

Inside the CSF, CO₂ combines with water and produces carbonic acid, which dissociates into hydrogen ions.

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

More H⁺ means lower pH.

Important precision: central chemoreceptors do not primarily “measure PaCO₂ directly.” CO₂ crosses the BBB easily, then the resulting increase in CSF H⁺ / decrease in CSF pH increases central chemoreceptor stimulation.

3. The medullary respiratory centers increase output

Greater central chemoreceptor stimulation increases activity in the medullary inspiratory network. The pons helps shape the pattern and transition between inspiration and expiration.

4. The neural signal reaches the ventilatory pump

Motor output travels through spinal pathways to the phrenic and intercostal nerves, increasing stimulation of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles.

Then the respiratory muscles do the mechanical work:
Neural output ↑→ Phrenic/intercostal firing ↑→ Muscle contractions ↑→ Rate/depth of alveolar ventilation ↑→ CO₂ exhalation ↑
This is negative feedback. As alveolar ventilation rises, more CO₂ is removed. PaCO₂ then falls toward normal, less CO₂ enters the CSF, H⁺ falls, CSF pH rises toward normal, and central chemoreceptor stimulation decreases. The response eases once the disturbance is corrected.

Chemoreceptors: What Does Each Sensor Actually Sense?

Central chemoreceptorsLocated near the medulla. CO₂ crosses the blood-brain barrier, changes CSF H⁺, and the receptors respond primarily to that CO₂-derived CSF hydrogen-ion change. They do not directly sense arterial oxygen.
Peripheral chemoreceptorsLocated in the carotid and aortic bodies. They respond rapidly to low PaO₂, arterial acidemia, and PaCO₂ changes. Carotid bodies are especially important for hypoxemic stimulation.
PaCO₂ ↑ → CO₂ crosses BBB ↑ → CSF H⁺ ↑ → central chemoreceptor stimulation ↑ → ventilation ↑
PaO₂ response is graded, not an on/off switch. Peripheral stimulation becomes much stronger below approximately 60 mm Hg; it does not suddenly become the only control of breathing.

Respiratory Drive Is an Integrated Control System

Replace the old slogan: breathing is not controlled by a single “CO₂ drive” or “hypoxic drive.” Multiple inputs modify a central pattern continuously.
ExerciseCentral command and proprioceptive feed-forward increase ventilation at exercise onset; chemical feedback then fine-tunes the response to metabolism, H⁺, temperature, and workload.
SleepBehavioral wakefulness input falls and ventilatory responses change. Sleep therefore modifies control even in healthy people.
MedicationsOpioids and sedatives can depress medullary output and reduce responsiveness to CO₂.
Chronic hypercapniaRenal bicarbonate retention and CSF buffering modify central chemoreceptor sensitivity over time; responsiveness is altered, not eliminated.
Neurologic diseaseBrainstem injury can impair drive; spinal or peripheral injury can leave drive intact but interrupt transmission.
Voluntary behaviorSpeech, swallowing, emotion, and breath holding can temporarily modify breathing, but rising CO₂ eventually overwhelms voluntary suppression.
Important correction to older “hypoxic drive” teaching: chronically hypercapnic patients do not simply “lose their CO₂ drive,” and oxygen does not universally switch off breathing. Ventilatory control remains integrated. The physiologic question is what inputs, drive, mechanics, and gas exchange are doing together.

Why Is PaCO₂ Rising? Locate the Failed Link

Use the drive → transmission → muscle capacity → mechanical load → ventilation framework. The same hypercapnia can result from very different failures.
Drive failureBrainstem output is inadequate. Example: opioid depression.
Transmission failureDrive exists but cannot reach the pump. Example: high cervical spinal injury or phrenic neuropathy.
Muscle-force failureNerves signal appropriately, but weak/fatigued muscles cannot generate adequate pressure.
Excess mechanical loadDrive and muscle effort may be high, but airway resistance or low compliance makes effective ventilation too costly.
Visible effort does not prove adequate ventilation. A patient can be awake, anxious, tachypneic, and still develop hypercapnia if tidal volume falls or dead-space ventilation dominates.

V/Q: First Ask What Is Present

V = ventilation

Fresh gas reaching a lung region.

Q = perfusion

Blood flow reaching the pulmonary capillaries around that region.

V/Q = regional alveolar ventilation ÷ regional perfusion
MatchedVentilation and perfusion are reasonably balanced.
Low V/QToo little ventilation for the amount of perfusion.
High V/QToo much ventilation for the amount of perfusion.
EndpointsV=0 with Q present → V/Q=0, shunt-like. Q→0 with V present → V/Q→∞, dead-space-like.

Why the Apex and Base Have Different V/Q Ratios

In an upright lung, both ventilation and perfusion increase toward the base—but perfusion increases proportionally more. That is why basal V/Q is lower and apical V/Q is higher.

Hypoxic Pulmonary Vasoconstriction & Integrated Challenge

Localized hypoxia: potentially helpful

Low alveolar PO₂ causes nearby pulmonary arterioles to constrict. Perfusion is redirected away from the poorly ventilated region toward better-ventilated alveoli, which can improve regional matching.

Diffuse hypoxia: potentially harmful

If much of the lung is hypoxic, widespread vasoconstriction raises pulmonary vascular resistance and right-ventricular afterload. Chronic diffuse hypoxia can contribute to pulmonary hypertension.

Pulmonary vessels behave differently from systemic vessels: alveolar hypoxia promotes pulmonary vasoconstriction. This changes perfusion; it does not open the airway or restore ventilation.
