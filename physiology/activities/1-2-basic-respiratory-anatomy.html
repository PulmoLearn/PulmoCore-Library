Basic Respiratory Anatomy: Where Does Gas Exchange Actually Occur?

Follow one breath from the upper airway to the alveolar-capillary interface. Your goal is not just to name structures, but to decide whether gas is being transported, wasted, or exchanged—and explain why.

Section 1

Follow the Breath: Conducting → Respiratory

Gas must travel through many structures before it reaches a true gas-exchanging surface. The terminal bronchiole is the final conducting airway. The respiratory bronchiole begins the respiratory zone and is the transition point where alveoli first appear; gas exchange becomes increasingly important through the alveolar ducts and alveoli.

Course boundary to use: terminal bronchiole = final conducting-zone airway; respiratory bronchiole = beginning of the respiratory zone and the transition point where alveoli first appear.
Core rule: The conducting zone moves gas but does not directly exchange gases. The respiratory zone contains alveoli and is where gas exchange becomes possible.

Transition point: The respiratory bronchiole is the first airway in the respiratory zone. It still conducts gas, but because alveoli begin appearing along its walls, gas exchange also becomes possible here.
Section 2

Anatomic Dead Space: Gas Moved, but No Exchange Yet

Every tidal breath first fills the conducting airways. That portion of the breath is anatomic dead space: gas is present and moving, but there are no alveoli available for direct gas exchange.

Use ideal body weight (IBW), not actual body weight. Conducting-airway size is related to predicted body size and height; excess body mass does not proportionally enlarge the conducting airways.

Section 3

Three Alveoli: Air + Blood Must Meet

This is where dead-space reasoning becomes physiologic. An alveolus may receive air and still accomplish little exchange if perfusion is absent. Likewise, a perfused alveolus cannot exchange normally if little or no fresh gas reaches it.

Section 4

Even Air + Blood Are Not Enough: The Diffusion Surface Must Work

Normal exchange requires a large alveolar surface area, a very thin barrier, and a partial-pressure gradient that drives gas across the membrane. Type I pneumocytes create a thin exchange surface; type II pneumocytes produce surfactant to support alveolar stability.

Change the diffusion conditions

Loss of alveolar walls reduces available exchange area.

Interstitial/alveolar fluid increases diffusion distance.

A smaller driving gradient reduces net transfer.

Relative diffusion potential
100%
Large area + thin membrane + adequate gradient support efficient diffusion.
