Before you sequence it: what do these terms mean?

During a spontaneous breath, the muscles act first. Their contraction changes the size of the thorax. That changes the pressure around the lungs, which expands the alveoli and briefly changes alveolar pressure. Airflow is the final result of the pressure difference.

Boyle's law connects volume to pressure: when the amount of gas and temperature are unchanged, pressure and volume move in opposite directions.
Volume ↑ → Pressure ↓    |    Volume ↓ → Pressure ↑

During inspiration, the expanding thorax pulls the lungs and alveoli larger. As alveolar volume increases, alveolar pressure falls slightly below atmospheric pressure. Air then flows from the higher pressure outside the body toward the lower pressure inside the alveoli. As air enters, alveolar pressure rises back toward atmospheric pressure. When the pressures equalize, the gradient disappears and airflow stops.

Thoracic & alveolar volume ↑→ Alveolar pressure ↓ below atmosphere→ High pressure → low pressure→ Air flows inward→ Pressures equalize→ Flow stops
Thoracic volume The amount of space inside the chest. When the diaphragm contracts and moves downward, the thorax gets larger.
Pleural pressure The pressure in the pleural space surrounding the lungs. During inspiration it becomes more negative as the chest expands.
Transpulmonary pressure The distending pressure across the lung: alveolar pressure minus pleural pressure. As pleural pressure becomes more negative, transpulmonary pressure increases and helps pull the lung open.
Alveolar pressure The pressure inside the alveoli. Boyle's law explains why expanding alveoli briefly lower this pressure. That creates the gradient that moves air inward.
Atmospheric pressure The pressure outside the body. We use it as the reference value of 0 cm H₂O. It becomes the higher-pressure side of the gradient during inspiration when alveolar pressure falls below it.
Airflow Gas moves from higher pressure to lower pressure. Once alveolar pressure returns to atmospheric pressure, airflow stops even though the lungs are still expanded.
Now combine muscle action, Boyle's law, and the pressure gradient. Use this chain instead of memorizing the cards:
Muscles contract→ Thorax enlarges→ Pleural pressure becomes more negative→ Transpulmonary pressure rises→ Alveoli expand & alveolar pressure falls→ Air flows inward

One subtle point: alveolar pressure does not stay negative for the whole inspiration. As air enters and the alveoli fill, alveolar pressure returns toward atmospheric pressure. When the two pressures are equal, the pressure gradient disappears and inspiratory flow stops.

Name the mechanical burden

Elastic work: pressure needed to overcome recoil/stiffness.
Resistive work: pressure needed to move flow through resistance.

Stiff lung: large ΔP, small ΔV → elastic burden.

Narrowed airway: large ΔP needed for same flow → resistive burden.

Time Constants & Assessment Readiness

Predict which units fill, empty, lag, or trap gas when time is limited.

Competency focus: τ = R × C, 63% at one time constant, long versus short time constants, maldistribution, and air trapping.

Compare two lung units

τ = Resistance × Compliance

Unit X: R 2 × C 0.05 = 0.10 s

Unit Y: R 8 × C 0.10 = 0.80 s

What the slider demonstrates: this changes how much time both lung units are allowed to fill. Unit X has a short time constant, so it approaches equilibrium quickly. Unit Y has a long time constant, so it needs more time. Move the slider shorter and longer and watch how the gap between the two units changes.

Interpret the timing

One time constant achieves about 63% of the available volume change. A long-τ unit needs more time to approach equilibrium and also more time to empty.

Rate is not part of the τ equation. Respiratory rate matters because it determines how much inspiratory and expiratory time is available.

Required calculation practice • Time constant
