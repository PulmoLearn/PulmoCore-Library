Why does RV matter?

Residual volume remains after maximal exhalation. Because it cannot be voluntarily exhaled, simple spirometry cannot directly measure RV or capacities that contain it (FRC and TLC).

Relationship strategy

Ask whether the value describes one breath or one minute. Per breath, VT is divided into alveolar and dead-space portions. Per minute, VE is divided into VA and dead-space ventilation. Respiratory rate is the bridge between the two.

Dead space: locate the wasted ventilation

Differentiate anatomic, alveolar, and physiologic dead space, then calculate VD/VT.

✓ Complete
Competency focus: Build on the 1.2 anatomy: distinguish conducting-airway (anatomic) dead space from ventilated-but-poorly-perfused alveolar dead space, then calculate total physiologic dead space using VD/VT and the Bohr relationship.
Anatomic

Conducting airways

Gas occupies airways that do not contain a gas-exchanging surface.

Alveolar

Ventilated, poorly perfused

Gas reaches an alveolus, but little or no pulmonary blood flow is available for exchange.

Physiologic

Total wasted volume

Anatomic dead space + alveolar dead space.

How to calculate physiologic dead space

Use the Bohr relationship in two steps. First find the fraction of the tidal breath that is wasted. Then convert that fraction into a volume in milliliters.

  1. Calculate VD/VT: subtract mixed-expired PCO2 from PaCO2, then divide by PaCO2.
  2. Convert the fraction to mL: multiply VD/VT by tidal volume.
Step 1: VD/VT = (40 − 28) ÷ 40 = 12 ÷ 40 = 0.30
Step 2: Physiologic VD = 0.30 × 500 mL = 150 mL

Remember: VD/VT is a ratio (or percent). Physiologic dead space is the wasted volume from that breath in mL.

Now try the Bohr calculation

PaCO2 = 40 mm Hg
PECO2 = 28 mm Hg
VT = 500 mL

VD/VT = (PaCO2 − PECO2) / PaCO2

Interpret the result
Reason from the equation

Dead-space gas contains little carbon dioxide, so it dilutes mixed-expired PCO2. A larger PaCO2-to-PECO2 difference therefore indicates a larger fraction of wasted ventilation.
