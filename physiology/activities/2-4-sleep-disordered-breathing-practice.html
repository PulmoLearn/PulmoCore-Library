Sleep-Disordered Breathing: Read the Signals, Find the Failure

A sleep study is not just an AHI number. Follow the same physiologic sequence every time: Is airflow present? Is respiratory effort present? What happens to oxygen and CO₂? When does it happen? Do the signals agree?

Know What the Sleep-Study Numbers Mean

ApneaCessation or near-cessation of airflow for at least about 10 seconds in adult scoring.
HypopneaPartial airflow reduction associated with qualifying desaturation and/or arousal under the scoring rule used.
AHIApneas + hypopneas divided by hours of sleep. It measures event frequency.
ODIQualifying oxygen desaturations per hour. It measures oxygen-drop frequency, not necessarily the same events as AHI.
ArousalA brief shift toward wakefulness that can restore airway tone/ventilation but fragments sleep.
Time below thresholdThe percentage or minutes spent below an SpO₂ cutoff; this describes burden that AHI alone can miss.

Required AHI calculation

A patient has 42 apneas + hypopneas during 6 hours of sleep.

AHI = total apneas + hypopneas ÷ hours of sleep
AHI = events/hour
Do not stop at the category. Two patients can have the same AHI but very different event duration, oxygen nadir, time below 88%, CO₂ retention, arousal burden, REM clustering, or positional dependence.

Why Sleep Makes Breathing More Vulnerable

Sleep removes physiologic supports that help keep ventilation and the upper airway stable while awake.
Drive ↓Behavioral wakefulness input falls; chemical control becomes more important.
Upper-airway tone ↓Pharyngeal dilator activity decreases, making a susceptible airway easier to collapse.
Accessory-muscle help ↓Especially during REM, patients with limited respiratory-muscle reserve can lose compensatory support.
FRC / oxygen reserve ↓Supine sleep and obesity can reduce end-expiratory lung volume, so saturation falls faster during an event.
REM is a stress test for ventilatory reserve. A patient who appears adequate while awake may develop sustained hypoventilation or deeper desaturation during REM when drive and accessory-muscle contribution are reduced.

What Recurrent Obstruction Does to the Body

OSA is not just “snoring plus pauses.” Each obstructive event creates a repeating physiologic stress cycle.
Upper-airway collapse → continued inspiratory effort → alveolar ventilation ↓ → O₂ ↓ / CO₂ ↑ → increasingly negative intrathoracic pressure → arousal → airway reopens → sympathetic surge → cycle repeats

Gas exchange

Intermittent hypoxemia and CO₂ fluctuation occur as ventilation stops and restarts.

Pressure & cardiovascular stress

Forceful effort against a closed airway produces large negative pressure swings and contributes to autonomic/cardiovascular stress.

Arousal

Arousal can restore airway tone and airflow, but repeated arousals fragment normal sleep architecture.

Sympathetic activation

Recovery from an event may include transient heart-rate and blood-pressure surges.

Same AHI, Different Physiologic Burden

Patient A

AHI 15Mostly brief hypopneasNadir 90%Minimal time <88%

Frequent events, but relatively modest gas-exchange burden.

Patient B

AHI 15Long apneasNadir 76%Prolonged time <88%

Same event frequency category, but much greater physiologic burden.

AHI and ODI can disagree. A mismatch may reflect unscored events, sustained hypoventilation, baseline lung disease, or artifact. The correct response is to examine the signals—not force the two indices to match.
A “normal” AHI does not exclude sleep-related respiratory failure. Sustained desaturation with rising transcutaneous/end-tidal CO₂ and few discrete events points toward hypoventilation rather than frequent apnea/hypopnea.

What PAP Changes Physiologically

CPAP / EPAPRaises pharyngeal intraluminal pressure and acts as a pneumatic splint to keep a collapsible upper airway open. It does not directly create central respiratory drive.
Pressure supportThe inspiratory pressure above expiratory pressure assists inspiration, can increase tidal volume, and can improve alveolar ventilation.
Backup rateTimed breaths can support ventilation when spontaneous central effort is absent or unreliable. This addresses breath initiation, not simply airway collapse.
Important distinction: a patent airway does not guarantee adequate ventilation. CPAP can correct obstruction while tidal volume remains inadequate and CO₂ continues to rise.
Isolated OSADiscrete obstructive events; daytime ventilation may be normal.
Obesity hypoventilationIncludes awake daytime hypercapnia; increased mechanical load and reduced ventilatory drive may coexist with OSA.
Neuromuscular weaknessDrive may be intact but muscle force is inadequate, often worse in REM when accessory-muscle contribution falls.
CNS depressionCentral respiratory output may be reduced, producing hypoventilation or central pauses.
Unstable central controlPaCO₂ may fall below the sleeping apneic threshold, allowing central pauses and waxing/waning periodic breathing.
