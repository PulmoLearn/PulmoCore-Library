Cardiac Pathology & Hemodynamics

Hemodynamics becomes easier when every number answers one of four questions: How much blood is flowing? How full is the ventricle? What resistance must it eject against? How strongly can it contract? Then use pressure, flow, and bedside findings to locate the failed link.

Start With Flow: Cardiac Output & Cardiac Index

Cardiac output (CO) = Heart rate × Stroke volume
Cardiac index (CI) = Cardiac output ÷ Body surface area
Units matter. If stroke volume is given in mL/beat, HR × SV gives mL/min. Divide by 1,000 to report L/min. Cardiac index then expresses that flow relative to patient size.

Required CO calculation

HR = 84/min; SV = 65 mL.

CO = L/min

Required CI calculation

CO = 4.8 L/min; BSA = 1.8 m².

CI = L/min/m²
Same CO ≠ same physiologic state. A patient reaching 4.2 L/min with HR 120 and SV 35 mL is much more dependent on tachycardia than a patient with a normal rate and larger stroke volume.

What Determines Stroke Volume?

PreloadEnd-diastolic myocardial fiber stretch. Influenced by venous return, blood volume, compliance, atrial contraction, and intrathoracic pressure.
AfterloadThe force/resistance the ventricle must overcome to eject blood. SVR contributes to LV afterload; PVR contributes to RV afterload.
ContractilityIntrinsic myocardial force generation at a given preload and afterload. It is not the same thing as stroke volume.

Frank-Starling lab

Try it: drag preload higher. In the useful range, greater end-diastolic stretch raises stroke volume. Beyond the useful range, filling pressure/congestion can keep rising while stroke volume gains flatten.

Relative stroke-volume response: 70%

Increase afterload

Resistance to ejection ↑→Ventricular work ↑→SV tends ↓

Increase contractility

Force generation ↑→End-systolic volume ↓→SV tends ↑

Build a Hemodynamic Map

Do not memorize numbers in isolation. First identify what part of the circulation each variable represents.
CVPApproximate right-atrial/right-sided filling pressure.
PAPPressure generated by the RV against the pulmonary circulation.
PAOP / PCWPApproximate left-atrial/left-sided filling pressure when assumptions are valid.
SVRSystemic resistance and a major contributor to LV afterload.
PVRPulmonary vascular load and a major contributor to RV afterload.

Required SVR calculation

SVR = 80 × (MAP − CVP) ÷ CO

MAP 82 mm Hg • CVP 7 mm Hg • CO 5.0 L/min

SVR = dyn·s/cm⁵

Required PVR calculation

PVR = 80 × (mean PAP − PAOP) ÷ CO

Mean PAP 28 mm Hg • PAOP 12 mm Hg • CO 4.0 L/min

PVR = dyn·s/cm⁵
Pattern logic: high PAP + high PAOP can reflect backward transmission from the left side. High PAP + normal PAOP points you toward pulmonary vascular/RV afterload rather than primary left-atrial hypertension.

Pathology as Pressure Load, Volume Load, or Pump Failure

Stenosis = pressure loadA narrowed valve requires a larger upstream pressure gradient to move blood forward. Chronic pressure load promotes hypertrophy and can reduce forward flow.
Regurgitation = volume loadAn incompetent valve permits backward flow. Chambers receive extra volume, dilate, and effective forward stroke volume can fall even if total LV stroke volume looks large.
Ischemia/infarction = pump problemReduced coronary O₂ supply can impair relaxation and contraction; necrotic myocardium no longer contributes normally to force generation.

Example: mitral regurgitation

Total LV stroke volume = 88 mL; 30 mL returns to the left atrium during systole.

Forward SV = total SV − regurgitant SV = 58 mL

The circulation only receives the forward portion as effective systemic stroke volume.

Example: LV infarction

Contractility ↓→SV/CO ↓→Residual LV volume ↑→Left filling pressure / PAOP ↑→Pulmonary venous congestion ↑

Translate Bedside Findings Into Pressure, Flow, Resistance, or Volume

JVD / dependent edemaEvidence supporting elevated right-sided venous/filling pressure.
Bibasilar cracklesCan reflect elevated left-sided filling pressure causing pulmonary hydrostatic fluid accumulation.
Cool, mottled skinSupports poor forward flow with sympathetic vasoconstriction.
Narrow pulse pressureCan support reduced stroke volume/forward ejection in the proper context.
S3Rapid filling into a dilated or volume-overloaded ventricle; a filling sound, not a murmur.
MAPA pressure result. It must be interpreted with flow and resistance—not used alone as proof of adequate perfusion.
Pressure is not flow. Low CO can trigger intense vasoconstriction and high SVR. That response may partly preserve MAP while tissue perfusion remains inadequate.

C4 + C8

Positive Pressure Can Improve Oxygenation While Reducing Flow

Respiratory and cardiovascular effects can move in opposite directions.
Intrathoracic pressure ↑→Systemic venous-return gradient ↓→RV preload ↓→CO may ↓

Most vulnerable

A patient with limited circulating-volume/preload reserve can become hypotensive when positive intrathoracic pressure reduces venous return.

Possible LV benefit

Positive intrathoracic pressure can reduce LV transmural afterload in some failing left ventricles. Net effect depends on volume status, ventricular function, PVR, and airway pressure.

Oxygen delivery comparison

DO₂ = CO × CaO₂ × 10

Before: CO 5.0 L/min, CaO₂ 16 mL/dL → DO₂ = 800 mL/min

After positive pressure: saturation improves and CaO₂ rises to 18 mL/dL, but CO falls to 3.2 L/min.

New DO₂ = mL/min
Better SpO₂ does not guarantee better oxygen delivery. Delivery depends on arterial content and cardiac output.
