Build the mucociliary escalator

See how mucus hydration and ciliary motion work together to move trapped material toward the pharynx.

✓ Complete
Competency focus: Explain how mucus composition, hydration, and coordinated ciliary motion produce mucociliary transport—and why either dehydrated mucus or injured cilia can stop the system.

Read the mechanism

Mucus traps. Cilia transport. The cilia must beat through a properly hydrated periciliary layer so the mucus above them can move toward the pharynx.

Functional cilia + appropriate mucus hydration → upward secretion transport
Dehydration → ↑ viscosity → slower transport → retention
Ciliary injury → reduced coordinated transport → retention

Build an effective cough

Sequence the mechanical steps and identify which failure prevents central-airway clearance.

✓ Complete
Competency focus: Explain why cough requires adequate inspiration, glottic closure, expiratory-muscle force, and sudden high expiratory flow—and why normal cilia cannot replace a weak cough.
1
Adequate inspiration

Builds lung volume for the expulsive maneuver.

2
Glottic closure

Temporarily traps gas while pressure is generated.

3
Expiratory-muscle contraction

Raises intrathoracic pressure.

4
Sudden glottic opening

Creates high expiratory flow and mucus shearing.

Failure A

Deep inspiration and glottic closure are intact, but expiratory muscles are very weak.

Failure B

Mucus is hydrated and cilia are intact, but the patient cannot close the glottis effectively.

Failure C

Cough force is normal, but cilia are damaged and peripheral mucus transport is slow.

Why cough and cilia are complementary

Cilia move peripheral conducting-airway secretions toward larger airways. Cough is most effective once secretions reach those larger central airways. One mechanism cannot fully substitute for failure of the other.

Assessment Readiness: find the failed mechanism

Use new scenarios to trace defense failure into secretion retention, airway resistance, and distal risk.

✓ Complete
Competency focus: Analyze a clinical pattern by answering two questions in order: Where is the problem? Then, what happens next physiologically?

Reasoning chain to use

failed defense→impaired clearance→secretion retention→↓ airway radius→↑ resistance / work
Challenge 1 • Dry gas

A patient on an artificial airway has progressively thicker secretions despite unchanged mucus production. Which sequence best explains the change?

Challenge 2 • Same symptom, different failure

Patient A has dehydrated secretions and intact muscle strength. Patient B has normal mucus hydration but profound expiratory weakness. What differs?

Challenge 3 • Downstream mechanics

Ciliary injury causes secretion retention while respiratory effort and airway anatomy are otherwise unchanged. Which trend best supports the expected mechanical consequence?

Challenge 4 • Distal protection

Fine particles reach alveoli in a patient whose proximal defenses are intact. Which finding would most directly represent failure of the defense that now matters most?

Challenge 5 • Integrated failure

Humidification is adequate, but a patient has smoke-related ciliary injury and weak expiratory muscles. Which interpretation best integrates the pattern?

Challenge 6 • Early proximal failure

A patient has audible large-airway secretions from weak cough but normal oxygenation and no distal infiltrate. What is the best physiologic conclusion?

Assessment strategy

Do not jump from “secretions” to one diagnosis. First identify the failed defense: conditioning, mucus hydration, ciliary transport, cough mechanics, or distal macrophage activity. Then predict the next physiologic effect.
