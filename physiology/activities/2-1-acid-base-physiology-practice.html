CO₂ Transport & the Bicarbonate Buffer

Start with what happens to CO₂ made by your tissues.

Competency focus: CO₂ transport, bicarbonate-carbonic acid buffering, carbonic anhydrase, hemoglobin buffering, and the chloride shift.

Beginner foundation: acid, base, buffer

Hydrogen ion (H⁺)More free H⁺ means a more acidic solution and a lower pH.
Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻)A major extracellular base that can accept H⁺ and help limit changes in pH.
Carbon dioxide (CO₂)A volatile acid source because CO₂ combines with water and can ultimately generate H⁺.
BufferA chemical system that reduces sudden pH change by accepting or releasing H⁺.
CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

Think of this reaction as reversible. In tissues it helps convert CO₂ into bicarbonate for transport. In the lungs it reverses so bicarbonate can become CO₂ again and be exhaled.

Tissues

Cells produce CO₂ during metabolism.

CO₂ → blood
Inside the red blood cell
CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

Carbonic anhydrase speeds the reversible CO₂ + H₂O reaction.
Hemoglobin helps buffer H⁺.
HCO₃⁻ exits while Cl⁻ enters: the chloride shift.

Lungs

The chloride shift reverses. HCO₃⁻ returns to the RBC, is converted back toward CO₂, and CO₂ diffuses into alveoli for exhalation.

blood → CO₂
Important distinction: most total CO₂ is carried as bicarbonate, but the small dissolved fraction is what determines the measured PaCO₂.

The Respiratory Side: Ventilation Controls PaCO₂

Connect alveolar ventilation to CO₂, carbonic acid, H⁺, and pH.

Competency focus: Predict how alveolar ventilation changes PaCO₂ and pH when CO₂ production is stable.

What is PaCO₂ actually telling you?

PaCO₂ is a ventilation variable. If the body is producing CO₂ at the same rate, PaCO₂ varies inversely with effective alveolar ventilation:

Alveolar ventilation ↑ → PaCO₂ ↓    |    Alveolar ventilation ↓ → PaCO₂ ↑

That matters because changing CO₂ shifts the buffer reaction. More retained CO₂ pushes the reaction toward more carbonic acid and H⁺; removing CO₂ pulls it the other direction.

A patient’s PaCO₂ rises from 36 to 54 mm Hg while CO₂ production remains unchanged. Before the change, alveolar ventilation was 6.0 L/min. Estimate the patient’s new alveolar ventilation.

VA₂ / VA₁ = PaCO₂₁ / PaCO₂₂

  2. Use that ratio to determine what fraction of the original ventilation remains.
  3. Multiply that fraction by the original alveolar ventilation of 6.0 L/min.

The Metabolic Side: What the Kidneys Actually Do

Separate fast respiratory compensation from slower renal adaptation.

Competency focus: Reclaim filtered bicarbonate, excrete H⁺, generate new bicarbonate, and increase ammonium production during sustained acid loads.

Three renal jobs that students often blend together

1. Reclaim filtered HCO₃⁻Prevent existing bicarbonate from being lost in urine.
2. Excrete H⁺Remove acid from the body, including as titratable acid and ammonium (NH₄⁺).
3. Generate new HCO₃⁻When acid is excreted as titratable acid or NH₄⁺, new bicarbonate is added back to blood.
Ammonium adaptationDuring sustained acid loads, renal ammonium production increases and becomes an important way to excrete acid.

Minutes: lungs

Ventilation can change quickly, altering PaCO₂ within minutes. This is why the lungs provide rapid compensation for a primary metabolic disturbance.

Hours → days: kidneys

Renal bicarbonate handling and acid excretion change more slowly. Chronic respiratory disorders therefore show more renal compensation than acute ones.

Compensation helps—it does not erase the primary disorder. The nonprimary system moves in a direction that reduces the pH disturbance.

Compensation: Expected or Mixed?

Predict the direction first, then test it mathematically.

Competency focus: Apply expected-compensation relationships to decide whether the response is appropriate or whether another primary process is present.

Before the formula: what should compensation do?

If the primary problem lowers pH, the other system should respond in a direction that raises pH toward normal. If the primary problem raises pH, the other system should respond in a direction that lowers pH toward normal.

Key rule: If the measured compensatory value is outside the expected physiologic range, suspect an additional primary acid-base process.

Winter’s formula for metabolic acidosis

Expected PaCO₂ = 1.5(HCO₃⁻) + 8 ± 2

How to interpret it:

  • Measured PaCO₂ inside expected range → appropriate respiratory compensation
  • Measured PaCO₂ above range → additional respiratory acidosis
  • Measured PaCO₂ below range → additional respiratory alkalosis

ABG Investigation Lab

Put the physiology together in a repeatable five-step process.

Competency focus: Integrate pH, PaCO₂, HCO₃⁻, anion gap, expected compensation, time course, and internal consistency.

The five-step framework

  1. Read pH: acidemia, alkalemia, or near-normal.
  2. Identify the primary process: which abnormal variable explains the pH direction?
  3. Test compensation: is the nonprimary response in the correct direction and expected range?
  4. If metabolic acidosis is present, calculate anion gap: AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻).
  5. Write one mechanism sentence: explain why the numbers fit—or why they do not.

Anion gap: AGMA vs NAGMA

AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻)

The anion gap helps explain why bicarbonate is low when metabolic acidosis is present. It estimates negatively charged particles that are not routinely measured.

AGMA — Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis

Think: added or accumulated acid.

Hydrogen ions from an added acid consume HCO₃⁻. The accompanying negatively charged particles remain in the blood as unmeasured anions, so the anion gap rises.

Pattern: ↓ HCO₃⁻ + ↑ anion gap → look for accumulation of an unmeasured acid.

NAGMA — Non-Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis

Think: bicarbonate/base loss or an acid-excretion problem without a large unmeasured-anion load.

As HCO₃⁻ falls, chloride commonly rises to maintain electrical neutrality. Because chloride is measured, it replaces the missing bicarbonate in the equation and the anion gap does not rise.

Pattern: ↓ HCO₃⁻ + relative ↑ Cl⁻ + no increased gap → NAGMA, often called hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis.
Do not use the anion gap first. First establish that the patient has a metabolic acidosis. Then use the gap to classify the mechanism as AGMA or NAGMA.
