Physiologic Oxygenation: Where Did Oxygen Fail?

Follow oxygen from the atmosphere to the tissues. At each step, ask a different question: Was enough oxygen inspired? Did it reach the alveoli? Did it cross into arterial blood? Is there enough hemoglobin to carry it? Is there enough blood flow to deliver it?

C1

Pressure, Saturation, Content & Delivery Are Not the Same Thing

Beginner rule: before interpreting an oxygen value, name what it actually measures.
PAO₂Oxygen pressure in the alveoli.
PaO₂Dissolved oxygen pressure in arterial plasma.
SaO₂ / SpO₂Percent of available hemoglobin binding sites occupied by oxygen.
CaO₂Total arterial oxygen content, dominated by hemoglobin-bound oxygen.
DO₂Oxygen delivered to tissues each minute: content × blood flow.
Hypoxemia means arterial oxygen pressure/saturation is low. Tissue hypoxia means tissues are not receiving or using enough oxygen. You can have tissue hypoxia with a normal PaO₂—for example, severe anemia or very low cardiac output.

Most oxygen rides on hemoglobin

Only a small amount of oxygen is dissolved in plasma. Under ordinary conditions, approximately 98% of arterial oxygen content is carried on hemoglobin.

SpO₂ is not oxygen content

A saturation of 98% tells you that most available hemoglobin sites are occupied. It does not tell you how much hemoglobin exists or how much blood is flowing.

Hemoglobin Affinity, P50, Bohr & Haldane

Affinity asks: how tightly is hemoglobin holding oxygen? Capacity asks: how much hemoglobin is available? These are different problems.

Oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve

Try it: click Left shift, Normal, and Right shift one at a time. Watch the curve move, the P50 marker change, and the explanation below. The dark button shows the curve you are currently viewing.

How to read a shift

Right shift → affinity ↓ → P50 ↑ → tissue unloading easier
Left shift → affinity ↑ → P50 ↓ → tissue unloading harder

P50 is the PO₂ at which hemoglobin is 50% saturated. It is an affinity marker—not the PaO₂ at which plasma oxygen is 50% “full.”

Right-shift clues: ↑ temperature, ↑ CO₂, ↑ H⁺ (lower pH), ↑ 2,3-DPG. Think active tissue that needs oxygen unloaded.
Mnemonic: CADET face Right

Conditions that decrease hemoglobin’s affinity for oxygen and shift the curve to the right can be remembered as:

CO₂ ↑+ Acid / H⁺ ↑+ DPG (2,3-DPG) ↑+ Exercise+ Temperature ↑

Why exercise appears in the mnemonic: active tissues commonly generate more CO₂, H⁺, and heat, all of which favor oxygen unloading. The mnemonic helps recall the direction; the physiology explains why.

Bohr effect = CO₂/H⁺ change O₂ affinity

Active tissue→CO₂/H⁺ ↑→Hb affinity ↓→O₂ unloading ↑

Haldane effect = O₂ changes CO₂/H⁺ carriage

Lung O₂ loading→Hb carries less CO₂/H⁺→CO₂/H⁺ released→CO₂ unloading ↑

P50: use the number to describe affinity

P50 is the PO₂ at which hemoglobin is 50% saturated. It is useful because the direction of the number tells you what happened to hemoglobin affinity.

Low P50Usually below ~26 mm Hg in this course → left shift → higher affinity → hemoglobin holds O₂ more tightly → tissue unloading is more difficult.
Normal P50About 26–27 mm Hg → expected adult hemoglobin affinity under standard conditions.
High P50Usually above ~27 mm Hg → right shift → lower affinity → hemoglobin releases O₂ more readily to tissues.
P50 ↓ = affinity ↑    |    P50 ↑ = affinity ↓
Do not confuse P50 with oxygen content. P50 tells you about binding affinity, not how much hemoglobin is present and not the patient’s total arterial oxygen content.

Step 1 of Oxygenation: Establish Alveolar Oxygen

Before oxygen can cross into blood, enough oxygen must first be present in the alveolus. Inspired oxygen, barometric pressure, and alveolar ventilation determine PAO₂.

Sea-level room-air shortcut

PAO₂ ≈ 150 − (PaCO₂ ÷ 0.8)

The “150” term approximates humidified inspired oxygen at sea level on room air. Rising PaCO₂ lowers PAO₂ because hypoventilation means less fresh oxygen is brought into alveoli while CO₂ accumulates.

Altitude lowers the starting oxygen pressure

Lower barometric pressure means a lower inspired oxygen partial pressure even though FiO₂ remains 21%.

Try it: drag altitude higher. This is a conceptual demonstration of why low inspired oxygen can lower PAO₂ before any alveolar-transfer problem exists.

Approximate barometric pressure: 760 mm Hg

Required calculation • PAO₂

At sea level on room air, PaCO₂ = 48 mm Hg. Use R = 0.8.

  2. 150 − 60 = ?

Did Oxygen Cross from Alveoli into Arterial Blood?

The A–a gradient compares oxygen in the alveolus with oxygen that actually reached arterial blood.
A–a gradient = PAO₂ − PaO₂

Normal / near-normal gradient

PAO₂ and PaO₂ fall together. Think low inspired oxygen or pure hypoventilation when lung transfer itself remains intact.

Elevated gradient

Alveolar oxygen is available but arterial oxygen is lower than expected. Think V/Q mismatch, diffusion limitation, or shunt.

Required calculation • A–a gradient

Your PAO₂ calculation above was 90 mm Hg. The measured PaO₂ is 58 mm Hg.

A–a = 90 − 58
mm Hg
The gradient narrows the category—it does not name one exact diagnosis. An elevated A–a gradient tells you transfer failed somewhere, but you still need the clinical pattern and oxygen response to separate V/Q mismatch, diffusion limitation, and shunt.

Five Mechanisms of Hypoxemia

Use two organizing questions: Is the A–a gradient normal or elevated? Then ask: does raising inspired oxygen improve the problem?
Low inspired O₂A–a usually normalO₂ responsive

The oxygen pressure entering alveoli starts low, such as reduced barometric pressure.

HypoventilationA–a usually normalO₂ responsive

PaCO₂ rises and PAO₂ falls because fresh alveolar ventilation is inadequate.

Low V/QA–a elevatedUsually improves

Some ventilation remains, so increasing alveolar oxygen can improve end-capillary oxygenation.

Diffusion limitationA–a elevatedOften improves

Thickened membrane, low surface area, or short transit time slows equilibration; extra gradient can help.

Large shuntA–a elevatedPoor O₂ response

Blood bypasses ventilated alveoli or perfuses nonventilated alveoli, so that blood cannot directly contact the added oxygen.

Why high-V/Q lung cannot fully “make up” for low-V/Q lung: hemoglobin in well-ventilated units is already near the flat upper plateau, so it cannot load enough extra oxygen to completely compensate for poorly oxygenated blood.

Content & Delivery: Normal PaO₂ Can Still Hide Tissue Hypoxia

Arterial oxygen content

CaO₂ = 1.34(Hb)(SaO₂) + 0.003(PaO₂)

The first term is hemoglobin-bound oxygen and dominates normal content. SaO₂ must be entered as a decimal.

Systemic oxygen delivery

DO₂ = Cardiac Output × CaO₂ × 10

The ×10 converts CaO₂ from mL O₂/dL to mL O₂/L so it can combine with cardiac output in L/min.

Required calculation • CaO₂

Hb = 10 g/dL, SaO₂ = 92%, PaO₂ = 65 mm Hg.

  1. Convert 92% → 0.92.
  2. Bound term: 1.34 × 10 × 0.92.
  3. Dissolved term: 0.003 × 65.
  4. Add both terms.
mL O₂/dL

How to interpret CaO₂ after you calculate it

Use these as approximate teaching ranges, not absolute diagnostic cutoffs. CaO₂ varies mainly with hemoglobin concentration and saturation.

Low: < ~16 mL O₂/dLReduced oxygen-carrying content. Think low Hb, low saturation, dyshemoglobinemia, or a combination—even if PaO₂ is acceptable.
Expected: ~16–22 mL O₂/dLTypical resting arterial oxygen content when hemoglobin concentration and saturation are adequate. A value near 20 mL/dL is a useful reference.
High: > ~22 mL O₂/dLUsually reflects increased hemoglobin concentration and/or very high oxygen loading. More content does not automatically mean tissues are receiving or using oxygen normally.

Required calculation • DO₂

Use CaO₂ = 12.5 mL O₂/dL and cardiac output = 4.5 L/min.

DO₂ = 4.5 × 12.5 × 10
mL O₂/min

How to interpret DO₂ after you calculate it

A resting adult DO₂ is commonly around 1,000 mL O₂/min; body size and metabolic state matter. The important physiology is identifying whether the failed component is content, flow, or both.

Low: < ~800 mL O₂/minSystemic oxygen delivery is reduced. Look for low CaO₂, low cardiac output, or both.
Expected: ~800–1,200 mL O₂/minTypical resting delivery range for many adults when content and cardiac output are adequate.
High: > ~1,200 mL O₂/minCan occur with increased cardiac output and/or higher CaO₂, such as during exercise or compensation. High delivery does not guarantee normal cellular utilization.

Quick oxygenation index • P/F ratio

PaO₂ = 80 mm Hg on FiO₂ = 0.40.

P/F = PaO₂ ÷ FiO₂

The P/F ratio summarizes oxygenation severity but does not identify the mechanism by itself.

How to interpret the P/F ratio

For this physiology course, use the P/F ratio as a quick oxygen-transfer index, not as a diagnosis by itself. FiO₂ and clinical conditions affect interpretation.

Low: < 200Marked impairment in arterial oxygenation relative to the inspired oxygen concentration.
Reduced: 200–300Oxygenation efficiency is impaired. The lower the ratio, the greater the gas-exchange problem.
Preserved: > 300More efficient transfer relative to FiO₂; values around 400–500 may occur with healthy gas exchange. A high P/F is generally favorable rather than a pathology.
Remember: P/F tells you how impaired oxygenation is; it does not tell you why. Use A–a gradient, V/Q, diffusion, and shunt reasoning to identify the mechanism.
