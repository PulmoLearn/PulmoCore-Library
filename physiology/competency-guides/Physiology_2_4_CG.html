Before you begin

This chapter prepares you for the PulmoLearn interactive lesson by building the physiology first. AHRC 155 is about understanding why cardiopulmonary variables change. As you read, repeatedly ask: What is normal? What changed? Which physiologic variable changed first? What mechanism explains it? What should change next?

Big idea for Week 2.4: Keep the course centered on physiology and signal interpretation rather than sleep-laboratory workflow or device setup. Require students to use the same sequence for every tracing: determine whether airflow is reduced or absent, determine whether respiratory effort persists, identify the oxygenation or carbon-dioxide consequence, and then explain the control or upper-airway mechanism. C1: Define apnea, hypopnea, arousal, oxygen-desaturation index, apnea-hypopnea index, and related sleep-study variables in physiologic terms.

Sleep Event Definitions, AHI & Physiologic Burden

Apnea duration. In adult scoring, apnea generally requires cessation or near-cessation of airflow for at least 10 seconds. Duration is necessary for classification but should be interpreted with effort, oxygenation, and arousal signals.

Hypopnea. A hypopnea is a partial reduction in airflow associated with a qualifying desaturation and/or arousal under the scoring rule being used. It represents reduced ventilation rather than complete airflow cessation.

AHI calculation. AHI equals the total number of apneas plus hypopneas divided by hours of sleep. It is a frequency measure, not a direct measurement of event severity or gas-exchange burden.

Sleep time versus recording time. AHI calculated from true sleep time may differ from a respiratory event index based on total recording time. The denominator affects the apparent event frequency.

Why this matters: AHI provides a useful standardized count, but the physiologic consequences of sleep-disordered breathing depend on more than frequency. Faculty should repeatedly require students to integrate oxygenation, ventilation, duration, position, sleep stage, and arousal information.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • AHI is a complete measure of disease burden. Clarify that it does not directly quantify hypoxemic burden, hypercapnia, or cardiovascular stress.
  • AHI and ODI must always be identical. Clarify that they measure different events and may diverge.
  • A normal AHI excludes sleep-related respiratory impairment. Clarify that sustained hypoventilation or isolated sleep-related hypoxemia may occur without frequent scored apneas or hypopneas.
  • Every hypopnea is physiologically less important than every apnea. Clarify that duration, desaturation, and arousal determine consequence.
Clinical reasoning example: Patient A has an AHI of 15 consisting mostly of brief hypopneas with mild desaturation. Patient B also has an AHI of 15 but has long apneas, a nadir of 76%, and prolonged time below 88%. Their frequency category is the same, but Patient B has a much greater gas-exchange burden.
Check your reasoning: A patient has AHI 4 events/hour, average SpO2 87%, and rising transcutaneous CO2 during REM. Why is “normal AHI” an incomplete conclusion?

Integrated Sleep-Study Interpretation

Quantify frequency and burden. Use AHI, event duration, apnea/hypopnea composition, ODI, nadir, time below threshold, CO2 trend, and arousal pattern together.

Why this matters: Integrated interpretation prevents overreliance on any single channel. The student should be able to explain how airflow, effort, oxygenation, carbon dioxide, sleep state, and intervention response support one coherent mechanism.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • The lowest SpO2 automatically identifies the apnea type. Clarify that oxygenation describes consequence, not whether effort is present.
  • Waveform interpretation begins with the AHI category. Clarify that event mechanism is established from signals before summary statistics are interpreted.
  • All signal disagreement indicates mixed apnea. Clarify that artifact and sensor failure must be considered.
  • Improved oxygenation always proves obstruction was corrected. Clarify that oxygen can change independently of airway patency or ventilation.
Clinical reasoning example: A study shows repeated airflow cessation, absent effort, and crescendo-decrescendo ventilation between pauses. The internally consistent pattern indicates unstable central ventilatory control rather than fixed upper-airway obstruction.
Check your reasoning: A report lists a low AHI but shows prolonged REM-related CO2 elevation and desaturation without discrete events. What signal pattern argues for sustained hypoventilation rather than obstructive apnea?

C2: Explain how sleep state changes respiratory drive, upper-airway dilator tone, accessory-muscle contribution, functional residual capacity, and ventilatory reserve.

Sleep State, Ventilatory Control & Upper-Airway Stability

Wakefulness versus sleep. Wakefulness adds a behavioral drive to breathing and supports upper-airway dilator activity. During sleep, behavioral drive falls, chemoreceptor control becomes more dominant, and the ventilatory response to CO2 and oxygen may be reduced.

Upper-airway dilator tone. Pharyngeal airway patency depends on the balance between collapsing pressure and neuromuscular activation of dilator muscles. Sleep-related loss of dilator tone increases collapsibility, especially when anatomy already narrows the airway.

REM vulnerability. REM sleep reduces accessory-muscle contribution and produces irregular breathing. Patients with limited diaphragmatic strength, obesity, COPD, or reduced ventilatory reserve may develop their greatest hypoventilation and desaturation during REM.

Functional residual capacity. Supine position, obesity, and sleep can reduce end-expiratory lung volume. A smaller oxygen reservoir allows saturation to fall faster during an event, even if event duration is similar.

Ventilatory-control stability. When PaCO2 falls below the sleeping apneic threshold, central output may cease temporarily. A delayed or excessive corrective response can produce periodic waxing and waning ventilation.

Why this matters: Sleep removes several physiologic supports that are present during wakefulness. Students who understand these changes can explain why a patient may ventilate adequately while awake yet develop obstruction, hypoventilation, or central instability after sleep begins.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Sleep is a uniform physiologic state. Clarify that NREM and REM have different effects on drive, muscle recruitment, and breathing regularity.
  • Obstruction is caused only by excess tissue. Clarify that anatomy interacts with neuromuscular tone, lung volume, and collapsing pressure.
  • Desaturation severity depends only on apnea duration. Clarify that baseline saturation, FRC, oxygen consumption, and cardiopulmonary reserve also matter.
  • An arousal completely solves the problem. Clarify that it terminates the immediate event while contributing to fragmentation and sympathetic stress.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient with obesity and weak respiratory muscles may maintain acceptable ventilation while awake. During REM sleep, reduced accessory-muscle contribution and a smaller oxygen reservoir increase the likelihood of sustained hypoventilation and deeper desaturation even without frequent discrete apneas.
Check your reasoning: Two patients each have a 20-second obstructive apnea. One begins at SpO2 96% with normal FRC; the other begins at 89% with obesity and reduced FRC. Why should the second patient desaturate more rapidly?

C3: Differentiate obstructive, central, and mixed respiratory events using airflow, thoracoabdominal effort, timing, and gas-exchange signals.

Obstructive, Central & Mixed Event Physiology

Obstructive apnea. Airflow is absent while respiratory effort continues because central output persists but the upper airway is occluded. Thoracoabdominal movement may become paradoxical or out of phase as inspiratory muscles generate pressure against the obstruction.

Central apnea. Both airflow and respiratory effort are absent because central ventilatory output temporarily ceases. The airway may remain patent, but no inspiratory pressure gradient is generated.

Mixed apnea. The event includes both absent effort and obstructive effort, commonly beginning centrally and ending with respiratory effort against a closed airway. Classification requires following the pattern across the entire event.

Paradoxical effort. Continued diaphragmatic contraction against obstruction can pull the chest inward while the abdomen moves outward. This is evidence of effort, not evidence of effective ventilation.

Signal integration. Airflow defines whether gas is moving; effort indicates whether the nervous system and respiratory muscles are attempting a breath; SpO2 and CO2 reveal the downstream consequence; EEG/arousal signals explain sleep disruption.

Why this matters: The defining distinction is not simply whether airflow stops. The key question is whether respiratory effort persists. This separates failure of airway patency from failure or instability of central ventilatory output.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Absent airflow always means obstructive apnea. Clarify that effort channels determine the mechanism.
  • Chest movement proves ventilation occurred. Clarify that movement against an occluded airway may produce no airflow.
  • Central apnea is caused by airway collapse. Clarify that the primary problem is absent neural drive and effort.
  • Mixed apnea is the same as having separate obstructive and central events. Clarify that both components occur within one event.
Clinical reasoning example: A tracing shows 20 seconds of absent airflow with continued out-of-phase chest and abdominal motion and falling SpO2. Persistent effort against an occluded airway identifies obstructive apnea. If both effort channels were flat, the same airflow loss would indicate a central event.
Check your reasoning: A tracing begins with absent airflow and absent effort, then effort returns while airflow remains absent. How should the event be classified, and what changed physiologically during the event?

Recurrent Obstruction, Gas Exchange & Cardiovascular Stress

Intermittent hypoxemia. Repeated obstruction lowers alveolar ventilation and oxygen stores until arousal reopens the airway. Reoxygenation follows, producing a cyclic rather than continuously stable oxygen pattern.

CO2 accumulation. During an event, alveolar ventilation falls and CO2 rises. The degree of hypercapnia depends on event duration, baseline ventilation, metabolic production, and recovery between events.

Negative intrathoracic pressure. Inspiratory effort against a closed airway produces large negative pressure swings, increasing venous return and left-ventricular afterload and stressing the cardiovascular system.

Sympathetic surges. Hypoxemia, hypercapnia, and arousal activate sympathetic pathways. Repetition across the night contributes to heart-rate and blood-pressure variability.

Sleep fragmentation. Frequent arousals reduce restorative sleep even when each gas-exchange disturbance is brief. Daytime symptoms can therefore reflect both respiratory events and disrupted architecture.

Bradycardia-tachycardia pattern. Vagal influence and hypoxemia during apnea may slow heart rate; arousal and sympathetic activation then accelerate it. Repetition can promote rhythm instability.

Burden beyond event count. Long events, deep desaturation, large pressure swings, prolonged recovery, or persistent hypercapnia can create substantial physiologic stress at a modest AHI.

Why this matters: Sleep-disordered breathing affects cardiovascular and neurologic physiology through repeated cycles of obstruction, blood-gas change, intrathoracic pressure, and arousal. Understanding the sequence prevents students from viewing OSA as only an airway-noise problem.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Obstructive events only affect oxygen saturation. Clarify that CO2, pressure, autonomic activity, cardiac loading, and sleep architecture also change.
  • Each event ends without residual effect. Clarify that recovery surges and cumulative fragmentation are part of the burden.
  • Negative pressure makes it easier for the heart to eject. Clarify that markedly negative intrathoracic pressure can increase transmural LV afterload.
  • Daytime sleepiness is explained only by hypoxemia. Clarify that repeated arousal and loss of restorative sleep are major contributors.
Clinical reasoning example: During repeated obstructive apneas, the patient continues to generate forceful inspiratory effort. SpO2 falls and CO2 rises; arousal restores patency and ventilation but produces a heart-rate and blood-pressure surge. This cycle may repeat hundreds of times.
Check your reasoning: Why can two patients with the same AHI experience different cardiovascular stress if one has longer events and much larger negative intrathoracic pressure swings?

Positive Airway Pressure & Sustained Hypoventilation Physiology

CPAP as a pneumatic splint. Continuous positive airway pressure increases pharyngeal intraluminal pressure and helps maintain a positive transmural pressure across a collapsible upper airway. Its primary effect is airway stabilization, not stimulation of central respiratory drive.

EPAP function. In bilevel support, expiratory positive pressure contributes to upper-airway patency and end-expiratory lung volume, similar to the splinting role of CPAP.

Pressure support. The difference between inspiratory and expiratory pressure assists inspiration, can increase tidal volume, and may improve alveolar ventilation.

Backup rate. A timed breath can support ventilation when spontaneous central effort is absent or unreliable. This addresses failure to initiate breaths rather than simple upper-airway collapse.

Oxygen without ventilation correction. Supplemental oxygen may improve saturation while leaving obstruction or hypoventilation unresolved and may mask a rising CO2 burden.

Why this matters: AHI provides a useful standardized count, but the physiologic consequences of sleep-disordered breathing depend on more than frequency. Faculty should repeatedly require students to integrate oxygenation, ventilation, duration, position, sleep stage, and arousal information.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • AHI is a complete measure of disease burden. Clarify that it does not directly quantify hypoxemic burden, hypercapnia, or cardiovascular stress.
  • AHI and ODI must always be identical. Clarify that they measure different events and may diverge.
  • A normal AHI excludes sleep-related respiratory impairment. Clarify that sustained hypoventilation or isolated sleep-related hypoxemia may occur without frequent scored apneas or hypopneas.
  • Every hypopnea is physiologically less important than every apnea. Clarify that duration, desaturation, and arousal determine consequence.
Clinical reasoning example: Patient A has an AHI of 15 consisting mostly of brief hypopneas with mild desaturation. Patient B also has an AHI of 15 but has long apneas, a nadir of 76%, and prolonged time below 88%. Their frequency category is the same, but Patient B has a much greater gas-exchange burden.
Check your reasoning: A patient has AHI 4 events/hour, average SpO2 87%, and rising transcutaneous CO2 during REM. Why is “normal AHI” an incomplete conclusion?

Obstructive, Central & Mixed Event Physiology

Periodic breathing. Crescendo-decrescendo ventilation with central pauses suggests unstable feedback control, prolonged circulation time, altered chemosensitivity, or another central mechanism rather than fixed pharyngeal obstruction.

Why this matters: The defining distinction is not simply whether airflow stops. The key question is whether respiratory effort persists. This separates failure of airway patency from failure or instability of central ventilatory output.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Absent airflow always means obstructive apnea. Clarify that effort channels determine the mechanism.
  • Chest movement proves ventilation occurred. Clarify that movement against an occluded airway may produce no airflow.
  • Central apnea is caused by airway collapse. Clarify that the primary problem is absent neural drive and effort.
  • Mixed apnea is the same as having separate obstructive and central events. Clarify that both components occur within one event.
Clinical reasoning example: A tracing shows 20 seconds of absent airflow with continued out-of-phase chest and abdominal motion and falling SpO2. Persistent effort against an occluded airway identifies obstructive apnea. If both effort channels were flat, the same airflow loss would indicate a central event.
Check your reasoning: A tracing begins with absent airflow and absent effort, then effort returns while airflow remains absent. How should the event be classified, and what changed physiologically during the event?

Positive Airway Pressure & Sustained Hypoventilation Physiology

Backup rate. A timed breath can support ventilation when spontaneous central effort is absent or unreliable. This addresses failure to initiate breaths rather than simple upper-airway collapse.

Obesity hypoventilation. Sustained awake hypercapnia distinguishes obesity hypoventilation from isolated OSA. Reduced ventilatory drive, increased mechanical load, and sleep-related worsening may coexist with obstructive events.

Neuromuscular weakness. Reduced force generation may produce shallow ventilation and CO2 retention, especially in REM when accessory-muscle contribution falls. Airflow and effort may persist but be insufficient.

CNS depression. Opioids or other depressants may reduce respiratory rate, drive, and response to CO2, producing central events or sustained hypoventilation.

Oxygen without ventilation correction. Supplemental oxygen may improve saturation while leaving obstruction or hypoventilation unresolved and may mask a rising CO2 burden.

Why this matters: Positive pressure has different physiologic roles depending on whether the dominant problem is collapsible airway, inadequate tidal volume, or absent central initiation. Students should explain the mechanism before attaching a device label.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • CPAP directly forces the patient to take breaths. Clarify that ordinary CPAP maintains airway pressure but does not provide inspiratory pressure support or a mandatory breath.
  • All bilevel pressure is used only for comfort. Clarify that pressure difference can augment tidal volume and ventilation.
  • Normal daytime ventilation is required for obesity hypoventilation. Clarify that awake hypercapnia is a defining feature.
  • Correcting SpO2 proves ventilation is corrected. Clarify that oxygenation may improve while CO2 continues to rise.
Clinical reasoning example: An obstructive tracing normalizes when continuous pressure prevents collapse, demonstrating an airway-patency mechanism. A different patient has persistent shallow breathing and rising transcutaneous CO2 despite airway patency; inspiratory assistance is needed to augment tidal volume.
Check your reasoning: A patient’s obstructive events disappear with CPAP, but transcutaneous CO2 continues to rise during REM. Which physiologic problem has improved, which remains, and what does the remaining pattern imply?

Integrated Sleep-Study Interpretation

Differentiate intermittent from sustained failure. Recurrent discrete events suggest apnea/hypopnea physiology, while prolonged desaturation and rising CO2 without discrete airflow events suggest sustained hypoventilation or underlying lung disease.

Assess intervention response physiologically. Improvement in airflow with persistent effort confirms relief of obstruction; improved tidal pattern or CO2 suggests improved ventilation; persistent central pauses indicate unresolved control instability.

Why this matters: Integrated interpretation prevents overreliance on any single channel. The student should be able to explain how airflow, effort, oxygenation, carbon dioxide, sleep state, and intervention response support one coherent mechanism.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • The lowest SpO2 automatically identifies the apnea type. Clarify that oxygenation describes consequence, not whether effort is present.
  • Waveform interpretation begins with the AHI category. Clarify that event mechanism is established from signals before summary statistics are interpreted.
  • All signal disagreement indicates mixed apnea. Clarify that artifact and sensor failure must be considered.
  • Improved oxygenation always proves obstruction was corrected. Clarify that oxygen can change independently of airway patency or ventilation.
Clinical reasoning example: A study shows repeated airflow cessation, absent effort, and crescendo-decrescendo ventilation between pauses. The internally consistent pattern indicates unstable central ventilatory control rather than fixed upper-airway obstruction.
Check your reasoning: A report lists a low AHI but shows prolonged REM-related CO2 elevation and desaturation without discrete events. What signal pattern argues for sustained hypoventilation rather than obstructive apnea?

Sleep State, Ventilatory Control & Upper-Airway Stability

Ventilatory-control stability. When PaCO2 falls below the sleeping apneic threshold, central output may cease temporarily. A delayed or excessive corrective response can produce periodic waxing and waning ventilation.

Why this matters: Sleep removes several physiologic supports that are present during wakefulness. Students who understand these changes can explain why a patient may ventilate adequately while awake yet develop obstruction, hypoventilation, or central instability after sleep begins.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Sleep is a uniform physiologic state. Clarify that NREM and REM have different effects on drive, muscle recruitment, and breathing regularity.
  • Obstruction is caused only by excess tissue. Clarify that anatomy interacts with neuromuscular tone, lung volume, and collapsing pressure.
  • Desaturation severity depends only on apnea duration. Clarify that baseline saturation, FRC, oxygen consumption, and cardiopulmonary reserve also matter.
  • An arousal completely solves the problem. Clarify that it terminates the immediate event while contributing to fragmentation and sympathetic stress.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient with obesity and weak respiratory muscles may maintain acceptable ventilation while awake. During REM sleep, reduced accessory-muscle contribution and a smaller oxygen reservoir increase the likelihood of sustained hypoventilation and deeper desaturation even without frequent discrete apneas.
Check your reasoning: Two patients each have a 20-second obstructive apnea. One begins at SpO2 96% with normal FRC; the other begins at 89% with obesity and reduced FRC. Why should the second patient desaturate more rapidly?

Integrated Sleep-Study Interpretation

Begin with signal quality. Apparent airflow loss can result from displaced sensors, while effort channels and oxygen trends may remain normal. Physiologic conclusions require internally consistent signals.

Classify the event. Determine whether airflow is absent or reduced and whether effort is present, absent, or changes during the event.

Locate the timing. Determine whether events cluster in REM, NREM, or supine position. Clustering reveals state-dependent or positional vulnerability.

Quantify frequency and burden. Use AHI, event duration, apnea/hypopnea composition, ODI, nadir, time below threshold, CO2 trend, and arousal pattern together.

Differentiate intermittent from sustained failure. Recurrent discrete events suggest apnea/hypopnea physiology, while prolonged desaturation and rising CO2 without discrete airflow events suggest sustained hypoventilation or underlying lung disease.

Assess intervention response physiologically. Improvement in airflow with persistent effort confirms relief of obstruction; improved tidal pattern or CO2 suggests improved ventilation; persistent central pauses indicate unresolved control instability.

Identify internal inconsistency. A severe reported desaturation without airflow, effort, heart-rate, or waveform change may represent artifact and should not be interpreted as physiologic deterioration without corroboration.

National Board for Respiratory Care connection

These competencies support the physiologic reasoning used on the Respiratory Therapy Examination: gathering and evaluating cardiopulmonary data, interpreting trends, performing calculations when appropriate, distinguishing normal from abnormal physiology, and connecting findings to the mechanism that best explains the patient’s condition. The goal is not to memorize an NBRC code; it is to recognize the physiology that makes a clinical finding coherent.
