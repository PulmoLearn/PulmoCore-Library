Basic Respiratory Anatomy

This week develops the structure-function foundation needed to explain how gas moves from the upper airway to the alveoli, how the conducting and respiratory zones differ, and how the alveolar-capillary interface supports diffusion. Students connect airway branching, pleural relationships, respiratory muscles, alveolar cell types, and membrane structure to normal ventilation and gas transfer....

Before you begin

This chapter prepares you for the PulmoLearn interactive lesson by building the physiology first. AHRC 155 is about understanding why cardiopulmonary variables change. As you read, repeatedly ask: What is normal? What changed? Which physiologic variable changed first? What mechanism explains it? What should change next?

Big idea for Week 1.2: Keep instruction centered on anatomic relationships that explain physiology. Students should repeatedly trace the path of gas, identify where a process occurs, and explain how a structural change alters airflow, lung expansion, airway protection, or diffusion. Require complete cause-and-effect statements such as “emphysema destroys alveolar walls, reducing surface area and therefore diffusion capacity,” rather than isolated vocabulary definitions. Treatment selection and comprehensive patient assessment remain secondary to explaining the underlying mechanism.

Gas Pathway, Airway Branching & Functional Zones

Proximal-to-distal gas pathway. Trace gas through the nose or mouth, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, terminal bronchioles, respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, and alveoli. Students should understand that each narrowing branch increases total cross-sectional area even though individual airways become smaller.

Conducting zone. Explain that the conducting zone ends at the terminal bronchioles. Its major functions are moving, warming, humidifying, and filtering gas. Because no alveoli are present, this region contributes to anatomic dead space rather than direct gas exchange.

Respiratory zone. Explain that the respiratory zone begins at the respiratory bronchioles and includes alveolar ducts and alveoli. Alveoli become increasingly abundant distally, allowing gas exchange with the pulmonary capillary network.

Why this matters: Respiratory problems cannot be interpreted accurately without locating them anatomically. Conducting-airway disease primarily affects gas movement and resistance, while respiratory-zone disease more directly affects diffusion and effective gas exchange. The same symptom may therefore arise from different mechanisms.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • The respiratory zone begins at the terminal bronchioles. Clarify that terminal bronchioles are the final conducting airways; respiratory bronchioles begin the respiratory zone.
  • All small airways participate equally in gas exchange. Clarify that alveoli must be present for meaningful gas exchange.
  • Smaller individual airways mean lower total cross-sectional area. Clarify that the enormous number of parallel distal airways produces a much larger total area.
  • Open airways guarantee adequate oxygen transfer. Clarify that airflow and diffusion are separate requirements.
Clinical reasoning example: A mucus plug obstructs a segmental bronchus. The alveoli beyond the obstruction may initially remain structurally intact, but gas cannot reach them effectively. Students should identify this as an anatomic airflow-distribution problem rather than a primary membrane-diffusion defect.
Check your reasoning: A patient has a completely patent upper airway and normal tracheobronchial flow, yet oxygen transfer remains impaired. Which anatomic region must be evaluated next, and what structural features of that region normally support gas exchange?

Pleural Anatomy, Diaphragm & Lung-Chest Wall Coupling

Upper-airway protection. The epiglottis and coordinated laryngeal closure help prevent food and liquid from entering the lower airway during swallowing. Anatomy supports airway protection as well as gas conduction.

Why this matters: The lungs do not actively pull themselves open. They expand because respiratory muscles enlarge the thorax and the pleural pressure relationship transmits that movement to the lung. Loss of pleural coupling therefore causes collapse even when the airway itself is open.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • The visceral pleura lines the chest wall. Clarify that visceral pleura covers the lung; parietal pleura lines the thorax.
  • The pleural space normally contains air. Clarify that it is a potential space containing only a thin fluid layer.
  • The diaphragm is innervated by the vagus nerve. Clarify that the phrenic nerve supplies its primary motor innervation.
  • A pneumothorax is simply an airway obstruction. Clarify that it is loss of the pressure coupling needed to expand the lung.
Clinical reasoning example: After chest trauma, air enters the pleural space. The affected lung recoils inward even though the main bronchus remains open. Students should identify loss of transpulmonary distending pressure, not loss of airway patency, as the primary mechanism.
Check your reasoning: Air enters the pleural space, but the trachea and bronchi remain patent. Why does the lung still collapse, and which pressure difference has been lost?

Alveolar Cells, Surfactant & the Alveolar-Capillary Interface

Type I pneumocytes. Explain that type I cells are extremely thin and cover most of the alveolar surface area. Their thin cytoplasm minimizes diffusion distance and supports rapid oxygen and carbon dioxide transfer.

Type II pneumocytes. Explain that type II cells produce surfactant and can proliferate to help repair damaged alveolar epithelium. They cover less surface area than type I cells but are essential for alveolar stability.

Components of the diffusion barrier. The barrier includes alveolar epithelium, fused or closely associated basement membranes and interstitium, and capillary endothelium. The normal barrier is thin enough to permit rapid equilibration.

Why this matters: The alveolar-capillary membrane is a functional unit. Damage to any component can impair gas transfer even when airflow reaches the alveoli. Type I cells provide thin exchange surfaces, type II cells preserve mechanical stability, and capillary proximity maintains access to blood.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Type II cells cover most of the gas-exchange surface. Clarify that type I cells cover most of the surface; type II cells produce surfactant.
  • Surfactant increases surface tension. Clarify that surfactant lowers surface tension.
  • The alveolar wall and capillary wall are widely separated. Clarify that the interface is normally extremely thin.
  • Alveolar macrophages produce surfactant. Clarify that macrophages perform phagocytosis; type II cells produce surfactant.
Clinical reasoning example: A premature infant has inadequate surfactant. The alveoli require greater distending pressure, tend to collapse, and become harder to reopen. Students should connect type II cell immaturity to increased surface tension, reduced compliance, and greater work of breathing.
Check your reasoning: If type I cells are damaged but the airways remain open, which physiologic process is most directly affected? How would the consequence differ from isolated type II cell dysfunction?

Diffusion: Surface Area, Thickness & Pressure Gradient

Fick relationship. Gas transfer increases with greater surface area and a larger partial-pressure gradient and decreases as membrane thickness increases. Students should use this relationship qualitatively even when no numeric calculation is required.

Surface-area loss. Emphysema destroys alveolar septa, merging small alveoli into larger spaces and reducing total surface area available for diffusion. Open airways do not compensate for lost exchange surface.

Increased membrane thickness. Interstitial or alveolar edema adds distance between alveolar gas and capillary blood. Oxygen transfer is especially vulnerable because it diffuses less readily than carbon dioxide.

Why this matters: Mechanism determines interpretation. Reduced surface area, increased thickness, and reduced pressure gradient all slow gas transfer, but they are not interchangeable. Recognizing the failed variable strengthens later reasoning about pulmonary function testing, exercise desaturation, and oxygenation abnormalities.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Emphysema impairs diffusion mainly by increasing membrane thickness. Clarify that it primarily reduces surface area.
  • Edema reduces diffusion by eliminating perfusion. Clarify that its primary diffusion effect is increased membrane thickness, though severe disease can also alter perfusion.
  • Carbon dioxide is always more affected than oxygen because it is produced in greater quantity. Clarify that carbon dioxide diffuses much more readily.
  • A normal airway proves diffusion is normal. Clarify that airway patency and membrane function are distinct.
Clinical reasoning example: Patient A has emphysema with destroyed alveolar walls. Patient B has interstitial edema with preserved alveolar number. Both become hypoxemic with exertion. Patient A has less surface area; Patient B has a thicker diffusion barrier. The common outcome is impaired oxygen transfer, but the structural mechanism differs.
Check your reasoning: Two patients have equal alveolar oxygen pressure. One has extensive alveolar destruction and the other has interstitial edema. Why can both have reduced oxygen transfer despite the same driving pressure?

Pleural Anatomy, Diaphragm & Lung-Chest Wall Coupling

Visceral and parietal pleura. The visceral pleura directly covers the lung surface, while the parietal pleura lines the thoracic cavity. A thin fluid layer permits low-friction movement while surface forces maintain mechanical coupling.

Transpulmonary pressure. Define transpulmonary pressure as alveolar pressure minus pleural pressure. It is the distending pressure across the lung and is essential for maintaining lung volume.

Diaphragm innervation. The phrenic nerves, arising primarily from C3-C5, provide motor innervation to the diaphragm. Diaphragmatic contraction enlarges the thoracic cavity and contributes most of the volume change during quiet inspiration.

Pneumothorax mechanism. Air entering the pleural space reduces or eliminates the normal negative pleural pressure. The lung recoils inward and the chest wall moves outward because the mechanical link between them has been disrupted.

Why this matters: The lungs do not actively pull themselves open. They expand because respiratory muscles enlarge the thorax and the pleural pressure relationship transmits that movement to the lung. Loss of pleural coupling therefore causes collapse even when the airway itself is open.

National Board for Respiratory Care connection

These competencies support the physiologic reasoning used on the Respiratory Therapy Examination: gathering and evaluating cardiopulmonary data, interpreting trends, performing calculations when appropriate, distinguishing normal from abnormal physiology, and connecting findings to the mechanism that best explains the patient’s condition. The goal is not to memorize an NBRC code; it is to recognize the physiology that makes a clinical finding coherent.
