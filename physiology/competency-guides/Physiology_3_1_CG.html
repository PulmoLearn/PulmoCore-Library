Cardiovascular System & Cardiac Electrophysiology

This week connects the electrical events recorded on the ECG with the pressure, valve, sound, volume, and flow changes that produce an effective heartbeat. Before you begin

This chapter prepares you for the PulmoLearn interactive lesson by building the physiology first. AHRC 155 is about understanding why cardiopulmonary variables change. As you read, repeatedly ask: What is normal? What changed? Which physiologic variable changed first? What mechanism explains it? What should change next?

Big idea for Week 3.1: Keep the course centered on cardiovascular physiology. Use ECG findings as evidence of electrical timing and then require students to predict the mechanical consequence. Avoid turning the week into a rhythm-identification course or a detailed hemodynamic-calculation lesson, because advanced rhythm analysis and hemodynamics are emphasized elsewhere. For every concept, ask students to move through the same chain: electrical event → chamber contraction or relaxation → pressure change → valve position → heart sound or flow consequence. Emphasize that valves open and close passively in response to pressure gradients and that electrical activity can be present without effective mechanical output.

Core vocabulary: right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, left ventricle, tricuspid valve, pulmonic valve, mitral valve, aortic valve, sinoatrial (SA) node, atrioventricular (AV) node, bundle branches, Purkinje fibers, depolarization, repolarization, P wave, PR interval, QRS complex, ST segment, T wave, cardiac cycle, stroke volume, coronary perfusion.

C1: Trace blood through the right heart, pulmonary circulation, left heart, and systemic circulation while identifying the function of each chamber and valve.

Cardiac Chambers, Valves & Blood-Flow Pathway

Right-sided circulation. Systemic venous blood enters the right atrium through the venae cavae, crosses the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle, and is ejected through the pulmonic valve into the pulmonary arteries. The right ventricle operates in a low-pressure circuit because normal pulmonary vascular resistance is much lower than systemic vascular resistance.

Pulmonary circulation. Pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood to the lungs, and pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood to the left atrium. Reinforce that artery and vein names describe direction of flow relative to the heart, not oxygen content.

Left-sided circulation. Oxygenated blood crosses the mitral valve into the left ventricle and is ejected through the aortic valve into the systemic circulation. The left ventricle has a thicker wall because it must generate pressure against the higher-resistance systemic circuit.

Atrioventricular valves. The tricuspid and mitral valves permit atrium-to-ventricle flow during filling and close when ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure. Chordae tendineae and papillary muscles prevent prolapse during systole; they do not actively pull the valves open.

Semilunar valves. The pulmonic and aortic valves open when ventricular pressure exceeds pulmonary-artery or aortic pressure and close when arterial pressure again exceeds ventricular pressure. Their motion is produced by pressure gradients.

Flow continuity. An obstruction or incompetent valve changes upstream pressure, downstream flow, and chamber workload. Even when detailed valve pathology is not assessed, students should be able to predict the direction of the physiologic effect.

Why this matters: Accurate blood-flow sequencing gives students the framework needed to interpret pressure changes, valve sounds, oxygenated versus deoxygenated blood, and later hemodynamic patterns. Without this pathway, students often memorize disconnected facts and cannot predict where pressure or volume will accumulate.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Pulmonary arteries carry oxygenated blood because they are arteries. Clarify that arteries carry blood away from the heart; pulmonary arteries normally carry deoxygenated blood.
  • Valves are opened by papillary-muscle contraction. Clarify that valves move passively because of pressure gradients; papillary muscles stabilize the AV valves during systole.
  • The right and left ventricles perform the same pressure work. Clarify that they eject the same average flow but against very different vascular resistances.
  • Blood pauses in the atria until the ventricles are ready. Clarify that most ventricular filling is passive and continuous during diastole.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient develops acute obstruction at the pulmonic valve. Right-ventricular pressure must rise to eject blood, while pulmonary blood flow and left-sided filling may fall. The primary consequence begins upstream of the obstruction but can reduce systemic output downstream.
Check your reasoning: If left-ventricular ejection suddenly decreases while venous return continues, which chambers and vessels would experience rising volume or pressure first, and why?

C2: Identify the normal cardiac conduction pathway and explain the role of the SA node, AV node, bundle branches, and Purkinje system in coordinated contraction.

Electrical Conduction Pathway & Coordinated Contraction

SA node. The sinoatrial node normally initiates the impulse and establishes sinus rhythm. Atrial depolarization spreads from the SA node through both atria and produces the P wave.

AV nodal delay. The AV node slows conduction before the impulse reaches the ventricles. This delay permits atrial contraction to complete its contribution to ventricular end-diastolic volume before ventricular systole begins.

His-Purkinje system. After the AV node, the impulse travels through the bundle of His, right and left bundle branches, and Purkinje fibers. Rapid distribution activates the ventricles in a coordinated sequence and creates a relatively narrow normal QRS complex.

Why this matters: Conduction timing determines whether atrial and ventricular contractions contribute effectively to filling and ejection. Students who connect electrical timing with mechanics can explain why heart block, loss of atrial contraction, or very rapid rates may reduce cardiac output even when some ECG activity remains organized.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • The SA node directly contracts the ventricles. Clarify that it initiates an electrical impulse that travels through the conduction system before ventricular activation.
  • The AV nodal delay is an abnormal pause. Clarify that a normal delay is essential for coordinated filling.
  • A normal-looking QRS proves the patient has an effective pulse. Clarify that ECG voltage does not measure mechanical output.
  • Atrial and ventricular depolarization occur at the same time. Clarify that atrial activation and AV nodal delay precede ventricular activation.
Clinical reasoning example: A rhythm strip shows regular P waves at 80/min, but only every second atrial impulse is followed by a QRS complex. Atrial activity continues, yet the effective ventricular rate falls to approximately 40/min. The immediate physiologic concern is reduced ventricular contractions and potentially reduced cardiac output.
Check your reasoning: Why can a patient display organized electrical activity on the monitor yet have no palpable pulse? Identify at least three mechanical or circulatory requirements that the ECG does not measure.

ECG Waves, Intervals & Electrical Meaning

P wave. The P wave represents atrial depolarization. Atrial contraction follows after a brief electromechanical delay and contributes the final portion of ventricular filling.

PR interval. The PR interval includes atrial depolarization and conduction through the AV node and proximal conduction system. Prolongation indicates delayed atria-to-ventricle conduction, not prolonged atrial contraction.

QRS complex. The QRS complex represents ventricular depolarization. Atrial repolarization occurs at approximately the same time but is usually hidden within the larger ventricular signal. Mechanical ventricular systole follows.

ST segment. The ST segment corresponds to the period when ventricular myocardium is uniformly depolarized and contracting. It is an electrical interval, while ejection depends on the pressure generated during the associated mechanical systole.

T wave. The T wave represents ventricular repolarization. Mechanical relaxation follows, ventricular pressure falls, and the heart progresses toward diastolic filling.

Intervals versus events. ECG intervals describe electrical timing. Valve opening, valve closure, sounds, and changes in volume must be inferred from the associated mechanical sequence rather than read directly from the ECG.

Why this matters: The ECG provides a time map for cardiac electrical activation. Correct interpretation allows students to anticipate when atrial contraction, ventricular contraction, relaxation, filling, and ejection should occur and to detect when electrical timing may disrupt mechanical efficiency.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • The P wave is atrial contraction. Clarify that it is atrial depolarization; contraction follows.
  • The QRS complex is ventricular systole. Clarify that it is ventricular depolarization; pressure development and systole follow.
  • The T wave means the ventricles are completely filled. Clarify that it represents repolarization and precedes mechanical relaxation.
  • Heart sounds are produced by ECG waves. Clarify that sounds result mainly from valve closure and associated vibration after pressure reversals.
Clinical reasoning example: A prolonged PR interval means atrial depolarization still begins normally, but conduction to the ventricles takes longer. If every P wave is followed by a QRS, ventricular rate may remain unchanged; if conduction begins to fail, ventricular activation and output can fall.
Check your reasoning: At the instant the QRS begins, are the semilunar valves already open? Explain the electrical and pressure events that must occur before ejection begins.

Four Phases of the Cardiac Cycle

Ventricular filling. During diastole, ventricular pressure is below atrial pressure, the AV valves are open, and the semilunar valves are closed. Most filling is passive; atrial contraction adds the final portion of end-diastolic volume.

Isovolumetric contraction. Ventricular depolarization is followed by contraction. Ventricular pressure rises, the mitral and tricuspid valves close, S1 occurs, and all four valves are briefly closed. Ventricular volume remains constant until semilunar valves open.

Ejection. When ventricular pressure exceeds aortic or pulmonary-artery pressure, the semilunar valves open. Ventricular volume falls as stroke volume is ejected. The AV valves remain closed because ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure.

Isovolumetric relaxation. Ventricular repolarization is followed by relaxation. When arterial pressure exceeds ventricular pressure, the semilunar valves close and S2 occurs. All valves are briefly closed while pressure falls at constant ventricular volume.

Return to filling. When ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, the AV valves open and rapid filling begins. Valve position changes only when the pressure gradient reverses.

Volume landmarks. End-diastolic volume is the volume before ejection; end-systolic volume is the volume remaining after ejection. Stroke volume equals EDV minus ESV, linking filling and emptying to effective output.

Why this matters: The cardiac cycle integrates every major concept in the week. Students must understand that pressure changes precede valve movement, valve closures produce S1 and S2, and ventricular volume changes only when an inflow or outflow valve is open.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Isovolumetric means pressure does not change. Clarify that volume is constant while pressure changes rapidly.
  • All valves are open during ejection. Clarify that semilunar valves are open and AV valves are closed.
  • S1 occurs when the semilunar valves open. Clarify that S1 is associated with AV valve closure.
  • Atrial contraction provides all ventricular filling. Clarify that most filling is passive under normal conditions.
Clinical reasoning example: At the beginning of ventricular systole, ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure, closing the AV valves and producing S1. Because ventricular pressure has not yet exceeded aortic or pulmonary pressure, semilunar valves remain closed. This creates isovolumetric contraction: rising pressure with unchanged volume.
Check your reasoning: During isovolumetric relaxation, why does ventricular volume remain unchanged even though ventricular pressure is falling rapidly?

Heart Sounds, Filling & Coronary Perfusion

S1. The first heart sound is associated mainly with closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves as ventricular systole begins. It marks the transition from filling to pressure generation.

S2. The second heart sound is associated mainly with closure of the aortic and pulmonic valves as ventricular ejection ends. It marks the transition toward ventricular diastole.

Why this matters: Heart rate and rhythm affect more than the number of contractions per minute. They determine how much time is available for filling and coronary perfusion and whether atrial contraction occurs at a useful time. These relationships explain why an apparently fast rhythm may still produce poor perfusion.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Faster heart rate always increases cardiac output. Clarify that excessive rate can reduce stroke volume through shortened filling.
  • Atrial contraction is unimportant because most filling is passive. Clarify that its importance increases in stiff ventricles or short diastole.
  • Coronary perfusion is greatest during ventricular systole because pressure is highest. Clarify that systolic muscle compression limits left coronary flow.
  • S1 and S2 are produced by valves striking one another. Clarify that closure and vibration of valves, blood, and surrounding structures generate the sounds.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient develops a heart rate of 150/min. Although the higher rate initially tends to increase cardiac output, shortened diastole reduces ventricular filling and left-coronary perfusion. Stroke volume may fall, and myocardial oxygen demand rises while oxygen supply time decreases.
Check your reasoning: Why might a patient with severe tachycardia become hypotensive even though the heart is contracting more times per minute?

Heart Sounds, Filling & Coronary Perfusion

Atrial kick. Atrial contraction contributes a minority of normal ventricular filling but becomes more important when ventricular compliance is reduced or filling time is shortened. Loss of atrial contraction can therefore reduce end-diastolic volume and stroke volume.

Diastolic filling time. As heart rate rises, diastole shortens disproportionately. Moderate rate increases may raise cardiac output, but marked tachycardia can reduce filling, stroke volume, and coronary perfusion.

Rate-output relationship. Cardiac output equals heart rate times stroke volume, but the relationship is not unlimited. When rate becomes excessive, falling stroke volume can offset or reverse the expected increase in output.

Integrated Electrical-Mechanical Reasoning

Loss of atrial contraction. Disorganized or absent effective atrial contraction reduces late ventricular filling. The impact is greatest when ventricular compliance is low or diastolic time is short.

Excessive rate. Rapid activation shortens filling and coronary-perfusion time. The student should predict lower end-diastolic volume, reduced stroke volume, and possible mismatch between myocardial oxygen demand and supply.

Why this matters: Board-style questions often present an ECG or rate finding and ask for the downstream consequence. A consistent causal framework helps students analyze unfamiliar scenarios rather than relying on memorized rhythm labels.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Every ECG abnormality has the same mechanical effect. Clarify that the consequence depends on rate, conduction ratio, filling, contractility, and vascular load.
  • Atrial electrical activity guarantees useful atrial contraction. Clarify that electrical activation and mechanical contraction can be ineffective or poorly timed.
  • Pulse rate and electrical rate are always identical. Clarify that not every electrical event necessarily produces a palpable pulse.
  • A single normal pressure proves the entire cycle is normal. Clarify that timing and trends across multiple variables matter.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient has regular P waves but intermittent QRS complexes, a ventricular pulse of 40/min, and hypotension. The atrial pacemaker is functioning, but conduction failure reduces effective ventricular contractions. The low pulse and blood pressure confirm the mechanical consequence predicted from the ECG.

  • The QRS complex is ventricular systole. Clarify that it is ventricular depolarization; pressure development and systole follow.
  • The T wave means the ventricles are completely filled. Clarify that it represents repolarization and precedes mechanical relaxation.
  • Heart sounds are produced by ECG waves. Clarify that sounds result mainly from valve closure and associated vibration after pressure reversals.
Clinical reasoning example: A prolonged PR interval means atrial depolarization still begins normally, but conduction to the ventricles takes longer. If every P wave is followed by a QRS, ventricular rate may remain unchanged; if conduction begins to fail, ventricular activation and output can fall.
Check your reasoning: At the instant the QRS begins, are the semilunar valves already open? Explain the electrical and pressure events that must occur before ejection begins.

Integrated Electrical-Mechanical Reasoning

Conduction failure. If some atrial impulses fail to activate the ventricles, atrial activity may remain normal while ventricular rate and cardiac output fall. The mechanical consequence depends on how many effective ventricular contractions occur.

Ineffective contraction. Electrical depolarization may occur without adequate pressure generation because of severe pump failure, low preload, obstruction, or other mechanical causes. ECG organization therefore cannot be used alone to infer perfusion.

Why this matters: Board-style questions often present an ECG or rate finding and ask for the downstream consequence. A consistent causal framework helps students analyze unfamiliar scenarios rather than relying on memorized rhythm labels.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Every ECG abnormality has the same mechanical effect. Clarify that the consequence depends on rate, conduction ratio, filling, contractility, and vascular load.
  • Atrial electrical activity guarantees useful atrial contraction. Clarify that electrical activation and mechanical contraction can be ineffective or poorly timed.
  • Pulse rate and electrical rate are always identical. Clarify that not every electrical event necessarily produces a palpable pulse.
  • A single normal pressure proves the entire cycle is normal. Clarify that timing and trends across multiple variables matter.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient has regular P waves but intermittent QRS complexes, a ventricular pulse of 40/min, and hypotension. The atrial pacemaker is functioning, but conduction failure reduces effective ventricular contractions. The low pulse and blood pressure confirm the mechanical consequence predicted from the ECG.
Check your reasoning: A patient has a normal P-QRS-T sequence but weak pulses and severe hypotension. Which parts of the electrical-mechanical chain appear intact, and which possible mechanical failures remain?

Cardiac Chambers, Valves & Blood-Flow Pathway

Flow continuity. An obstruction or incompetent valve changes upstream pressure, downstream flow, and chamber workload. Even when detailed valve pathology is not assessed, students should be able to predict the direction of the physiologic effect.

Why this matters: Accurate blood-flow sequencing gives students the framework needed to interpret pressure changes, valve sounds, oxygenated versus deoxygenated blood, and later hemodynamic patterns. Without this pathway, students often memorize disconnected facts and cannot predict where pressure or volume will accumulate.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Pulmonary arteries carry oxygenated blood because they are arteries. Clarify that arteries carry blood away from the heart; pulmonary arteries normally carry deoxygenated blood.
  • Valves are opened by papillary-muscle contraction. Clarify that valves move passively because of pressure gradients; papillary muscles stabilize the AV valves during systole.
  • The right and left ventricles perform the same pressure work. Clarify that they eject the same average flow but against very different vascular resistances.
  • Blood pauses in the atria until the ventricles are ready. Clarify that most ventricular filling is passive and continuous during diastole.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient develops acute obstruction at the pulmonic valve. Right-ventricular pressure must rise to eject blood, while pulmonary blood flow and left-sided filling may fall. The primary consequence begins upstream of the obstruction but can reduce systemic output downstream.
Check your reasoning: If left-ventricular ejection suddenly decreases while venous return continues, which chambers and vessels would experience rising volume or pressure first, and why?

Electrical Conduction Pathway & Coordinated Contraction

Conduction delay. A prolonged PR interval reflects slowed atria-to-ventricle conduction, commonly through the AV nodal region. A delay may preserve one-to-one conduction, whereas intermittent failure to conduct can reduce ventricular rate and output.

Electrical-mechanical dissociation. Organized electrical complexes do not guarantee an effective pulse. Mechanical output still depends on contractility, filling, valve function, and an intact circulation.

Why this matters: Conduction timing determines whether atrial and ventricular contractions contribute effectively to filling and ejection. Students who connect electrical timing with mechanics can explain why heart block, loss of atrial contraction, or very rapid rates may reduce cardiac output even when some ECG activity remains organized.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • The SA node directly contracts the ventricles. Clarify that it initiates an electrical impulse that travels through the conduction system before ventricular activation.
  • The AV nodal delay is an abnormal pause. Clarify that a normal delay is essential for coordinated filling.
  • A normal-looking QRS proves the patient has an effective pulse. Clarify that ECG voltage does not measure mechanical output.
  • Atrial and ventricular depolarization occur at the same time. Clarify that atrial activation and AV nodal delay precede ventricular activation.
Clinical reasoning example: A rhythm strip shows regular P waves at 80/min, but only every second atrial impulse is followed by a QRS complex. Atrial activity continues, yet the effective ventricular rate falls to approximately 40/min. The immediate physiologic concern is reduced ventricular contractions and potentially reduced cardiac output.
Check your reasoning: Why can a patient display organized electrical activity on the monitor yet have no palpable pulse? Identify at least three mechanical or circulatory requirements that the ECG does not measure.

Heart Sounds, Filling & Coronary Perfusion

Rate-output relationship. Cardiac output equals heart rate times stroke volume, but the relationship is not unlimited. When rate becomes excessive, falling stroke volume can offset or reverse the expected increase in output.

Why this matters: Heart rate and rhythm affect more than the number of contractions per minute. They determine how much time is available for filling and coronary perfusion and whether atrial contraction occurs at a useful time. These relationships explain why an apparently fast rhythm may still produce poor perfusion.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Faster heart rate always increases cardiac output. Clarify that excessive rate can reduce stroke volume through shortened filling.
  • Atrial contraction is unimportant because most filling is passive. Clarify that its importance increases in stiff ventricles or short diastole.
  • Coronary perfusion is greatest during ventricular systole because pressure is highest. Clarify that systolic muscle compression limits left coronary flow.
  • S1 and S2 are produced by valves striking one another. Clarify that closure and vibration of valves, blood, and surrounding structures generate the sounds.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient develops a heart rate of 150/min. Although the higher rate initially tends to increase cardiac output, shortened diastole reduces ventricular filling and left-coronary perfusion. Stroke volume may fall, and myocardial oxygen demand rises while oxygen supply time decreases.
Check your reasoning: Why might a patient with severe tachycardia become hypotensive even though the heart is contracting more times per minute?
