Mechanics of Ventilation

This week develops the pressure-volume-flow relationships that produce spontaneous ventilation. Students connect respiratory-muscle action to changes in pleural and alveolar pressure, distinguish compliance from elastance and resistance, explain laminar and turbulent flow, and analyze how surfactant, airway radius, and time constants alter work of breathing. Before you begin

This chapter prepares you for the PulmoLearn interactive lesson by building the physiology first. AHRC 155 is about understanding why cardiopulmonary variables change. As you read, repeatedly ask: What is normal? What changed? Which physiologic variable changed first? What mechanism explains it? What should change next?

Big idea for Week 1.3: Keep instruction centered on physiologic mechanisms. Ask students to move through a consistent chain: identify what changed, determine whether the change primarily affects pressure, volume, flow, resistance, compliance, or surface tension, and then predict the resulting effect on ventilation and work of breathing. Clinical examples should support this reasoning rather than shift the lesson into equipment selection or comprehensive patient assessment.

C1: Apply Boyle's law and pressure-gradient principles to explain spontaneous inspiration and passive expiration.

Pressure Gradients & the Spontaneous Breath Cycle

Boyle’s law. Explain that at constant temperature, pressure varies inversely with volume. When inspiratory muscles enlarge the thorax and lungs, alveolar volume increases and alveolar pressure falls below atmospheric pressure. Gas then moves into the lungs because a pressure gradient exists.

Quiet inspiration. Diaphragmatic contraction and external intercostal activity enlarge the thorax. Pleural pressure becomes more negative, transpulmonary pressure increases, alveoli expand, and alveolar pressure briefly falls below atmospheric pressure.

Quiet expiration. Inspiratory muscles relax and elastic recoil decreases thoracic and lung volume. Alveolar pressure rises slightly above atmospheric pressure, driving gas outward. Quiet expiration is normally passive.

Why this matters: Pressure-gradient reasoning explains why gas moves and why airflow stops. Students who understand the full sequence can predict the direction of flow from pressure data instead of memorizing isolated pressure values.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Air enters because the lungs “suck” air inward. Clarify that gas moves because alveolar pressure falls below atmospheric pressure.
  • Pleural pressure and alveolar pressure are the same. Clarify that they occupy different compartments and serve different mechanical roles.
  • Alveolar pressure remains negative throughout inspiration. Clarify that it returns to atmospheric pressure when inspiratory flow stops.
  • Expiration always requires muscle contraction. Clarify that quiet expiration is normally passive and driven by recoil.
Clinical reasoning example: A spontaneously breathing patient begins inspiration. Pleural pressure becomes more negative, lung volume rises, and alveolar pressure falls from 0 to -1 cm H2O relative to atmosphere. Gas enters until alveolar pressure returns to 0. The changing pressure gradient, not the absolute lung volume, determines whether flow is occurring.
Check your reasoning: A patient has an open airway and adequate inspiratory-muscle effort, but pleural coupling is lost on one side. Which step in the normal pressure-volume sequence fails, and what happens to regional ventilation?

C3: Calculate and interpret respiratory-system compliance and relate compliance inversely to elastance.

Compliance, Elastance & Pressure-Volume Relationships

Compliance. Define compliance as the change in volume divided by the change in pressure: C = ΔV/ΔP. A highly compliant system gains more volume for a given pressure change; a low-compliance system requires more pressure for the same volume.

Units and calculation. Express compliance in L/cm H2O or mL/cm H2O and require students to keep volume and pressure units visible. A 300 mL volume change produced by 5 cm H2O equals 60 mL/cm H2O.

Elastance. Define elastance as the tendency to resist deformation and recoil toward the resting state. Elastance is the inverse of compliance. A stiff fibrotic lung has low compliance and high elastance.

Unequal compliance. When two lung units receive the same pressure but have different compliance, the more compliant unit receives a larger volume change. Equal pressure does not guarantee equal ventilation.

Why this matters: Compliance determines how pressure is converted into volume. This relationship is central to understanding stiff lungs, unequal regional ventilation, increased elastic work, and the mechanical consequences of fibrosis, edema, atelectasis, and chest-wall restriction.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Compliance means the same thing as airway resistance. Clarify that compliance is a pressure-volume property, whereas resistance is a pressure-flow property.
  • A stiff lung has high compliance. Clarify that stiffness means high elastance and low compliance.
  • Equal inspiratory pressure produces equal tidal volume in all lung units. Clarify that volume distribution depends on regional compliance and resistance.
  • A large tidal volume always proves normal compliance. Clarify that the pressure required to produce that volume must also be known.
Clinical reasoning example: Two lung regions receive the same 10 cm H2O pressure change. Region A changes volume by 200 mL and Region B by 500 mL. Region B is more compliant. If disease lowers Region B compliance, the same pressure will produce less regional volume and increase elastic work.
Check your reasoning: A patient with fibrosis requires twice the pressure change to achieve the previous tidal volume. What happened to compliance, elastance, and elastic work?

C4: Explain how airway radius, gas viscosity, gas density, flow velocity, and airway geometry influence laminar and turbulent resistance.

Airway Resistance, Radius & Flow Patterns

Airway radius. Emphasize that radius has the largest effect on resistance during laminar flow. Small decreases in radius from bronchoconstriction, edema, secretions, or tube narrowing can markedly increase resistance.

Laminar flow. Describe orderly parallel streamlines, most likely at lower velocity in straight smaller airways. In laminar flow, pressure requirements are strongly influenced by viscosity.

Turbulent flow. Describe disorganized flow favored by high velocity, larger airways, irregular surfaces, sharp branching, and narrowing. Turbulent pressure requirements are strongly influenced by gas density.

Gas density. In severe narrowing with turbulent flow, a lower-density gas mixture can reduce the pressure needed to move gas even though the airway radius has not changed.

Why this matters: Resistance determines how much pressure must be devoted to moving gas through the airways. Recognizing the dominant role of airway radius and flow pattern helps students explain why pressure requirements rise dramatically during bronchospasm or high-flow breathing.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • The smallest individual airways create the greatest total airway resistance. Clarify that the large number of parallel peripheral airways produces a very large total cross-sectional area.
  • Viscosity and density affect all flow patterns equally. Clarify that viscosity dominates laminar flow and density becomes especially important in turbulence.
  • Resistance and flow are interchangeable terms. Clarify that resistance is opposition to flow and is calculated from pressure and flow.
  • Lower gas density directly bronchodilates the airway. Clarify that it reduces turbulent pressure loss without enlarging the airway.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient with severe bronchoconstriction needs a much larger inspiratory pressure to maintain the same flow. Narrowing increases resistance and may convert flow toward turbulence. Reducing flow velocity or using a lower-density gas reduces turbulence-related pressure loss, but neither removes the underlying airway narrowing.
Check your reasoning: Two patients have the same airway radius, but one has much higher flow velocity and turbulent flow. Why might the second patient require a greater pressure gradient?

C5: Explain how surface tension and surfactant affect alveolar stability, opening pressure, compliance, and elastic work.

Surface Tension, Surfactant & Alveolar Stability

Surfactant function. Surfactant lowers surface tension, reduces opening pressure, improves compliance, limits alveolar collapse, and decreases elastic work.

Surfactant deficiency. Connect deficient surfactant to increased surface tension, alveolar instability, atelectasis, low compliance, and increased elastic work.

Why this matters: Surfactant links microscopic alveolar forces to whole-lung mechanics. Students should be able to explain why deficiency produces unstable, stiff lungs rather than simply recall that surfactant “keeps alveoli open.”

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Surfactant increases surface tension to hold alveoli open. Clarify that it lowers surface tension.
  • Surfactant primarily changes airway resistance. Clarify that its dominant effect is on alveolar surface tension and elastic mechanics.
  • Only premature infants depend on surfactant. Clarify that surfactant is essential in all lungs, although deficiency is especially important in prematurity.
  • Collapse from surfactant deficiency is caused by weak respiratory muscles. Clarify that the primary problem is increased inward surface force and reduced compliance.
Clinical reasoning example: A premature infant with surfactant deficiency must generate a large pressure change for a small tidal volume. The chain is low surfactant → high surface tension → alveolar instability and collapse → low compliance → increased elastic work.
Check your reasoning: Two patients have increased work of breathing: one has surfactant loss and one has bronchoconstriction. Which mechanical burden predominates in each, and why?

C6: Differentiate elastic work from resistive work and identify the mechanical abnormality responsible for each.

Work of Breathing & Respiratory Time Constants

Elastic work. Define elastic work as the pressure-energy required to overcome lung and chest-wall recoil. It rises when compliance decreases, as in fibrosis, edema, atelectasis, or surfactant deficiency.

Resistive work. Define resistive work as the pressure-energy required to move gas through the airways and tissues. It rises when airway resistance increases, as in bronchoconstriction, mucus, edema, or high turbulent flow.

Why this matters: Work-of-breathing and time-constant reasoning integrates compliance, resistance, pressure, flow, and volume. It allows students to explain why mechanically different lung units respond differently even when exposed to the same breathing frequency or inspiratory pressure.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • All increased work of breathing is caused by airway resistance. Clarify the separate elastic and resistive components.
  • High compliance always means ventilation is easy. Clarify that high compliance may reduce elastic work but, when paired with high resistance, can create very long time constants and air trapping.
  • Resistance and compliance cancel each other in the time-constant equation. Clarify that they multiply.
  • Respiratory rate is required to calculate a time constant. Clarify that rate determines whether enough time is available, but τ itself is calculated from resistance and compliance.
Clinical reasoning example: Unit X has τ = 0.10 s and Unit Y has τ = 0.80 s. During a short inspiratory time, Unit X approaches its available volume quickly while Unit Y remains incompletely filled. During a short expiratory time, Unit Y also empties incompletely and may trap gas.
Check your reasoning: A lung unit with high resistance and high compliance receives progressively less effective ventilation as respiratory rate rises. Explain the mechanism using its time constant and available filling and emptying time.

C7: Calculate and interpret respiratory time constants and predict delayed filling, delayed emptying, maldistribution, and air trapping.

Work of Breathing & Respiratory Time Constants

Long time constant. High resistance, high compliance, or both prolong filling and emptying. Slow emptying promotes gas trapping when expiratory time is insufficient.

Unequal time constants. Regional differences cause asynchronous filling and emptying, maldistribution of ventilation, and frequency-dependent effects.

Calculation. For Unit X, R 2 × C 0.05 = 0.10 s. For Unit Y, R 8 × C 0.10 = 0.80 s. Unit Y fills and empties much more slowly.
