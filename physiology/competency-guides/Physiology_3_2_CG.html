Cardiac Output, Cardiac Index & Stroke-Volume Determinants

Cardiac output. CO = HR x SV. Convert stroke volume from mL to L when reporting L/min. A heart rate of 90/min and stroke volume of 70 mL produce 6.3 L/min.

Cardiac index. CI = CO / body surface area. Indexing output allows comparison across body size. A CO of 4.2 L/min and BSA of 2.0 m2 produce 2.1 L/min/m2.

Why this matters: These calculations and relationships are the bridge between cardiovascular mechanics and oxygen delivery. They allow students to distinguish whether low output is caused primarily by insufficient filling, excessive ejection load, weak contraction, or ineffective rate and rhythm.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Preload equals total body fluid volume. Clarify that preload is ventricular fiber stretch and can be altered by compliance and pressure, not only fluid amount.
  • Afterload is the pressure inside the ventricle at end-diastole. Clarify that afterload opposes ejection.
  • Contractility and stroke volume are interchangeable. Clarify that stroke volume is the outcome of preload, afterload, and contractility.
  • A higher heart rate always raises cardiac output. Clarify that shortened diastolic filling can reduce stroke volume.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient has HR 120/min and SV 35 mL. CO is 4.2 L/min, which may appear acceptable, but the patient is using marked tachycardia to compensate for a severely reduced stroke volume. The pattern is fragile because further loss of filling or contractility can rapidly decrease output.
Check your reasoning: Two patients have the same cardiac output. One has a normal heart rate and stroke volume; the other has severe tachycardia and a low stroke volume. Why are these not equivalent physiologic states?

Myocardial Ischemia, Injury & Infarction as Pump Dysfunction

Pressure-flow consequence. Loss of left-ventricular contractility lowers stroke volume and cardiac output. End-systolic and end-diastolic volumes may rise, increasing left-atrial and pulmonary venous pressure and eventually producing pulmonary congestion.

Why this matters: Myocardial pathology matters to respiratory physiology because impaired pump function can create pulmonary edema, worsen V/Q relationships, reduce mixed venous oxygen content, and limit oxygen delivery even when the lungs are capable of oxygenating blood.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • ST elevation means the tissue is already a mature scar. Clarify that it represents acute injury and time-sensitive risk of infarction.
  • Q waves show the exact current severity of ischemia. Clarify that they may persist as a record of prior necrosis.
  • Normal SpO2 means the myocardium is receiving enough oxygen. Clarify that coronary flow, hemoglobin, cardiac workload, and diastolic time also determine supply.
  • An infarction affects only electrical activity. Clarify that dead myocardium also reduces mechanical force and can alter valves, pressures, and output.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient with an acute anterior infarction develops falling blood pressure, cool skin, crackles, and a rising PAOP. The most coherent physiologic sequence is reduced left-ventricular contractility -> lower stroke volume and cardiac output -> higher residual ventricular volume and left-sided filling pressure -> pulmonary venous congestion and reduced systemic perfusion.
Check your reasoning: Why can a patient have a normal arterial oxygen saturation and still develop myocardial ischemia or inadequate systemic oxygen delivery?

Valve Dysfunction: Pressure Load, Volume Load & Forward Flow

Stenosis. A narrowed valve increases the pressure gradient required to move blood forward. The chamber upstream of the obstruction must generate more pressure, producing hypertrophy, higher filling pressure, and eventually reduced forward flow.

Regurgitation. An incompetent valve permits backward flow when it should be closed. The affected chambers experience volume overload, dilation, inefficient stroke volume, and eventually reduced effective cardiac output. Total stroke volume may appear high while forward stroke volume is inadequate.

Aortic stenosis. The left ventricle must generate high systolic pressure against a fixed outflow obstruction. This raises afterload, promotes concentric hypertrophy, and can narrow pulse pressure or reduce cardiac output.

Aortic regurgitation. Diastolic backflow from aorta to left ventricle increases ventricular preload and lowers aortic diastolic pressure, often widening pulse pressure. Chronic volume loading produces dilation.

Why this matters: Valve disease provides a direct demonstration of pressure gradients, resistance, preload, afterload, and effective versus total stroke volume. Students who reason from the location and timing of the abnormal flow can predict the chamber and circulation affected.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • All valve disease creates the same type of overload. Clarify that stenosis primarily creates pressure load, while regurgitation primarily creates volume load.
  • A louder murmur always means more severe disease. Clarify that murmur intensity also depends on flow and pressure gradient; severe low-flow disease may be quiet.
  • Regurgitant volume contributes fully to systemic cardiac output. Clarify that only forward stroke volume supports downstream perfusion.
  • Mitral stenosis is a left-ventricular afterload problem. Clarify that it primarily raises left-atrial and pulmonary venous pressure while limiting LV filling.
Clinical reasoning example: In severe mitral regurgitation, measured left-ventricular stroke volume may be 90 mL, but 35 mL returns to the left atrium. Effective forward stroke volume is only 55 mL. At 90 beats/min, forward cardiac output is 4.95 L/min rather than 8.1 L/min.
Check your reasoning: A patient with aortic stenosis develops tachycardia. How can shortened diastole and a fixed outflow obstruction combine to reduce coronary perfusion and systemic flow?

Auscultation & Bedside Findings as Hemodynamic Evidence

Aortic stenosis. A systolic crescendo-decrescendo murmur at the right upper sternal border reflects high-velocity ejection across a narrowed outflow valve and increased left-ventricular afterload.

Aortic regurgitation. A diastolic decrescendo murmur reflects aortic-to-left-ventricular backflow and increased diastolic ventricular volume.

S3 gallop. An S3 in an older adult indicates rapid filling into a dilated or volume-overloaded ventricle. It is evidence of increased filling volume and impaired ventricular handling, not a valve murmur.

Why this matters: Bedside findings become more meaningful when students treat them as outputs of a pressure-flow system. This prevents memorization of isolated signs and prepares students to test whether invasive values are physiologically coherent.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Crackles prove the patient has pneumonia. Clarify that hydrostatic pulmonary edema can produce fine bilateral crackles.
  • An S3 is the same as a diastolic murmur. Clarify that S3 is a low-frequency filling sound, not prolonged turbulent valve flow.
  • JVD directly measures left-ventricular preload. Clarify that it most closely reflects right-atrial/CVP elevation.
  • A normal MAP proves perfusion is adequate. Clarify that vasoconstriction may preserve pressure despite low flow.
Clinical reasoning example: An ICU patient has JVD, bilateral pitting edema, cool mottled skin, BP 88/70, tachycardia, and bibasilar crackles. The findings indicate elevated venous pressures and congestion combined with poor forward flow. The narrow pulse pressure supports reduced stroke volume.
Check your reasoning: How could a patient have high filling pressures, visible edema, and pulmonary crackles yet still have a low cardiac output?

Cardiac Output, Cardiac Index & Stroke-Volume Determinants

Preload. Preload reflects end-diastolic myocardial fiber stretch and is influenced by venous return, ventricular compliance, blood volume, atrial contraction, and intrathoracic pressure. CVP approximates right-sided filling pressure; PAOP estimates left-sided filling pressure when assumptions are valid.

Frank-Starling relationship. Within the useful range, increased end-diastolic fiber length increases contraction force and stroke volume. Beyond that range, additional filling raises pressure and congestion without meaningfully improving output.

Afterload. Afterload is the force opposing ventricular ejection. SVR contributes to left-ventricular afterload; PVR contributes to right-ventricular afterload. Increased afterload tends to reduce stroke volume and increase ventricular work.

Contractility. Contractility is intrinsic force generation at a given preload and afterload. Increased contractility lowers end-systolic volume and raises stroke volume; impaired contractility does the opposite.

Why this matters: These calculations and relationships are the bridge between cardiovascular mechanics and oxygen delivery. They allow students to distinguish whether low output is caused primarily by insufficient filling, excessive ejection load, weak contraction, or ineffective rate and rhythm.

Hemodynamic Pressures, SVR/PVR & Pattern Interpretation

CVP/right-atrial pressure. CVP most closely reflects right-atrial pressure and right-sided filling pressure. Elevated CVP can occur with fluid excess, right-ventricular failure, high PVR, tamponade, or increased intrathoracic pressure; a low value can support reduced circulating volume. Trends and waveform quality matter.

PAP. Pulmonary artery pressure reflects the pressure generated by the right ventricle against the pulmonary circulation. It rises with increased PVR, increased pulmonary blood flow, or backward transmission of elevated left-sided pressure.

PAOP/PCWP. A properly obtained wedge pressure approximates left-atrial pressure and, under appropriate conditions, left-ventricular filling pressure. It does not directly measure contractility or total body fluid.

SVR. SVR = 80 x (MAP - CVP) / CO. It quantifies resistance to systemic blood flow and contributes to LV afterload. Low CO often triggers compensatory vasoconstriction and high SVR.

PVR. PVR = 80 x (mean PAP - PAOP) / CO. It quantifies the pulmonary vascular load faced by the right ventricle. Rising PVR can elevate PAP and CVP while PAOP remains normal.

Why this matters: Interpreting pressure and flow together prevents the common error of treating each number independently. The same pressure can mean different things depending on cardiac output, intrathoracic pressure, valve function, and the rest of the hemodynamic pattern.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • PAOP is a direct measurement of left-ventricular end-diastolic volume. Clarify that it is a pressure estimate influenced by compliance and other assumptions.
  • High PAP always means primary pulmonary vascular disease. Clarify that left-sided pressure can be transmitted backward into the pulmonary circulation.
  • High SVR means blood pressure and perfusion are both adequate. Clarify that high resistance may preserve pressure while reducing tissue flow.
  • CVP and PAOP should always rise and fall together. Clarify that isolated right- or left-sided dysfunction can separate the pressures.
Clinical reasoning example: MAP is 75 mm Hg, CVP 10 mm Hg, and CO 5 L/min. SVR = 80(75 - 10)/5 = 1040 dyn*s/cm5. The result is within a plausible range, but interpretation still requires the trend, clinical perfusion, and the cause of the measured cardiac output.
Check your reasoning: Why does a high PAP with a normal PAOP point toward a different mechanism than a high PAP accompanied by a high PAOP?

Cardiopulmonary Interaction & Systemic Oxygen Delivery

Right-ventricular afterload. High lung volume, alveolar overdistention, hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, or pulmonary vascular obstruction can raise PVR and increase RV afterload. The effect depends on lung volume and vascular zone relationships.

Why this matters: Respiratory support and cardiovascular function are mechanically linked. Students must understand why an intervention that improves arterial oxygenation may still reduce total oxygen delivery if it substantially lowers cardiac output.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Positive pressure always improves cardiac output because it reduces work of breathing. Clarify that it can also reduce venous return.
  • Improved SpO2 guarantees improved oxygen delivery. Clarify that delivery depends on both arterial content and cardiac output.
  • All patients respond to PEEP in the same direction. Clarify that volume status, ventricular function, PVR, and recruitability influence the response.
  • PVR is determined only by pulmonary disease. Clarify that oxygenation, lung volume, pressure, and vascular obstruction all contribute.
Clinical reasoning example: After initiation of positive-pressure ventilation, a hypovolemic patient develops hypotension while oxygen saturation improves. The likely mechanism is reduced venous return and right-ventricular preload, causing lower cardiac output. The improvement in arterial oxygenation does not prove that systemic oxygen delivery improved.
Check your reasoning: A patient's saturation rises from 88% to 96% after positive-pressure ventilation, but cardiac output falls from 5.0 to 3.2 L/min. What additional information is needed to decide whether systemic oxygen delivery improved?

Cardiopulmonary Interaction & Systemic Oxygen Delivery

Positive intrathoracic pressure. Positive-pressure ventilation raises pressure around the right atrium and thoracic veins, reducing the pressure gradient for systemic venous return. Right-ventricular preload and cardiac output may fall, especially when circulating volume is limited.

Myocardial Ischemia, Injury & Infarction as Pump Dysfunction

Ischemia. Reduced coronary oxygen supply initially produces reversible cellular dysfunction. Repolarization changes, commonly T-wave inversion or ST depression, may appear before irreversible necrosis. Even reversible ischemia can reduce relaxation, contractility, and stroke volume.

Injury. Persistent severe oxygen deprivation causes membrane instability and acute cellular injury. ST elevation reflects an injury current and signals that myocardial function is threatened. A larger injured territory produces a larger risk of reduced contractility and cardiogenic shock.

Infarction. Necrotic myocardium no longer contributes normally to electrical or mechanical activity. Pathologic Q waves may remain as evidence of prior tissue loss. The mechanical consequence depends on infarct size and location: reduced ejection, altered compliance, papillary-muscle dysfunction, or conduction disturbance.

Oxygen supply-demand balance. Coronary flow, diastolic perfusion time, arterial oxygen content, and myocardial workload determine whether supply meets demand. Tachycardia can worsen ischemia by shortening diastole while increasing oxygen consumption.

Downstream complications. Chamber enlargement promotes atrial fibrillation and stasis; low output reduces organ perfusion; elevated upstream pressure produces pulmonary or systemic congestion. These are consequences of altered pressure and flow, not disconnected disease facts.

Why this matters: Valve disease provides a direct demonstration of pressure gradients, resistance, preload, afterload, and effective versus total stroke volume. Students who reason from the location and timing of the abnormal flow can predict the chamber and circulation affected.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • All valve disease creates the same type of overload. Clarify that stenosis primarily creates pressure load, while regurgitation primarily creates volume load.
  • A louder murmur always means more severe disease. Clarify that murmur intensity also depends on flow and pressure gradient; severe low-flow disease may be quiet.
  • Regurgitant volume contributes fully to systemic cardiac output. Clarify that only forward stroke volume supports downstream perfusion.
  • Mitral stenosis is a left-ventricular afterload problem. Clarify that it primarily raises left-atrial and pulmonary venous pressure while limiting LV filling.
Clinical reasoning example: In severe mitral regurgitation, measured left-ventricular stroke volume may be 90 mL, but 35 mL returns to the left atrium. Effective forward stroke volume is only 55 mL. At 90 beats/min, forward cardiac output is 4.95 L/min rather than 8.1 L/min.
Check your reasoning: A patient with aortic stenosis develops tachycardia. How can shortened diastole and a fixed outflow obstruction combine to reduce coronary perfusion and systemic flow?

Auscultation & Bedside Findings as Hemodynamic Evidence

Mitral stenosis. A low-pitched diastolic rumble at the apex reflects turbulent filling across a narrowed mitral valve. The physiology is elevated left-atrial pressure and impaired ventricular inflow.

S3 gallop. An S3 in an older adult indicates rapid filling into a dilated or volume-overloaded ventricle. It is evidence of increased filling volume and impaired ventricular handling, not a valve murmur.

Bibasilar crackles. Elevated left-sided filling pressure increases pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure. Interstitial and alveolar fluid lowers compliance and creates inspiratory crackles. PAOP above approximately 18 mm Hg is commonly associated with clinical pulmonary edema.

JVD, edema, skin, and pulse pressure. JVD and dependent edema support elevated right-sided pressure; cool mottled skin supports low forward flow and sympathetic vasoconstriction; pulse-pressure changes provide clues about stroke volume and arterial runoff.

Why this matters: Bedside findings become more meaningful when students treat them as outputs of a pressure-flow system. This prevents memorization of isolated signs and prepares students to test whether invasive values are physiologically coherent.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Crackles prove the patient has pneumonia. Clarify that hydrostatic pulmonary edema can produce fine bilateral crackles.
  • An S3 is the same as a diastolic murmur. Clarify that S3 is a low-frequency filling sound, not prolonged turbulent valve flow.
  • JVD directly measures left-ventricular preload. Clarify that it most closely reflects right-atrial/CVP elevation.
  • A normal MAP proves perfusion is adequate. Clarify that vasoconstriction may preserve pressure despite low flow.
Clinical reasoning example: An ICU patient has JVD, bilateral pitting edema, cool mottled skin, BP 88/70, tachycardia, and bibasilar crackles. The findings indicate elevated venous pressures and congestion combined with poor forward flow. The narrow pulse pressure supports reduced stroke volume.
Check your reasoning: How could a patient have high filling pressures, visible edema, and pulmonary crackles yet still have a low cardiac output?

Hemodynamic Pressures, SVR/PVR & Pattern Interpretation

PAP. Pulmonary artery pressure reflects the pressure generated by the right ventricle against the pulmonary circulation. It rises with increased PVR, increased pulmonary blood flow, or backward transmission of elevated left-sided pressure.

Left-pump-failure pattern. Low CO, high PAOP, and high SVR suggest impaired LV pumping with left-sided congestion and compensatory vasoconstriction.

Right-pressure-load pattern. Elevated CVP and PAP with normal PAOP and low CO suggest increased RV afterload or impaired right-sided output rather than primary left-atrial hypertension.

Internal consistency. Values should be checked against one another and against the patient. A single pressure can be distorted by transducer leveling, zeroing, respiratory pressure, catheter position, or waveform misidentification.

Why this matters: Interpreting pressure and flow together prevents the common error of treating each number independently. The same pressure can mean different things depending on cardiac output, intrathoracic pressure, valve function, and the rest of the hemodynamic pattern.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • PAOP is a direct measurement of left-ventricular end-diastolic volume. Clarify that it is a pressure estimate influenced by compliance and other assumptions.
  • High PAP always means primary pulmonary vascular disease. Clarify that left-sided pressure can be transmitted backward into the pulmonary circulation.
  • High SVR means blood pressure and perfusion are both adequate. Clarify that high resistance may preserve pressure while reducing tissue flow.
  • CVP and PAOP should always rise and fall together. Clarify that isolated right- or left-sided dysfunction can separate the pressures.
Clinical reasoning example: MAP is 75 mm Hg, CVP 10 mm Hg, and CO 5 L/min. SVR = 80(75 - 10)/5 = 1040 dyn*s/cm5. The result is within a plausible range, but interpretation still requires the trend, clinical perfusion, and the cause of the measured cardiac output.
Check your reasoning: Why does a high PAP with a normal PAOP point toward a different mechanism than a high PAP accompanied by a high PAOP?

Cardiac Output, Cardiac Index & Stroke-Volume Determinants

Flow versus pressure. MAP is influenced by CO and SVR. A patient may maintain pressure through intense vasoconstriction while tissue flow and oxygen delivery remain poor.

Why this matters: These calculations and relationships are the bridge between cardiovascular mechanics and oxygen delivery. They allow students to distinguish whether low output is caused primarily by insufficient filling, excessive ejection load, weak contraction, or ineffective rate and rhythm.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Preload equals total body fluid volume. Clarify that preload is ventricular fiber stretch and can be altered by compliance and pressure, not only fluid amount.
  • Afterload is the pressure inside the ventricle at end-diastole. Clarify that afterload opposes ejection.
  • Contractility and stroke volume are interchangeable. Clarify that stroke volume is the outcome of preload, afterload, and contractility.
  • A higher heart rate always raises cardiac output. Clarify that shortened diastolic filling can reduce stroke volume.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient has HR 120/min and SV 35 mL. CO is 4.2 L/min, which may appear acceptable, but the patient is using marked tachycardia to compensate for a severely reduced stroke volume. The pattern is fragile because further loss of filling or contractility can rapidly decrease output.
Check your reasoning: Two patients have the same cardiac output. One has a normal heart rate and stroke volume; the other has severe tachycardia and a low stroke volume. Why are these not equivalent physiologic states?

Cardiopulmonary Interaction & Systemic Oxygen Delivery

Oxygen delivery. DO2 = CO x CaO2 x 10. A normal PaO2 or saturation cannot compensate indefinitely for severely reduced cardiac output. Conversely, a normal CO cannot fully compensate for very low hemoglobin or saturation.

Why this matters: Respiratory support and cardiovascular function are mechanically linked. Students must understand why an intervention that improves arterial oxygenation may still reduce total oxygen delivery if it substantially lowers cardiac output.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Positive pressure always improves cardiac output because it reduces work of breathing. Clarify that it can also reduce venous return.
  • Improved SpO2 guarantees improved oxygen delivery. Clarify that delivery depends on both arterial content and cardiac output.
  • All patients respond to PEEP in the same direction. Clarify that volume status, ventricular function, PVR, and recruitability influence the response.
  • PVR is determined only by pulmonary disease. Clarify that oxygenation, lung volume, pressure, and vascular obstruction all contribute.
Clinical reasoning example: After initiation of positive-pressure ventilation, a hypovolemic patient develops hypotension while oxygen saturation improves. The likely mechanism is reduced venous return and right-ventricular preload, causing lower cardiac output. The improvement in arterial oxygenation does not prove that systemic oxygen delivery improved.
Check your reasoning: A patient's saturation rises from 88% to 96% after positive-pressure ventilation, but cardiac output falls from 5.0 to 3.2 L/min. What additional information is needed to decide whether systemic oxygen delivery improved?
