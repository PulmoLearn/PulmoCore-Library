Lung Volumes & Capacities

Tidal volume (VT). Define tidal volume as the amount of gas inspired or expired during a normal breath. Emphasize that VT includes both alveolar volume and dead-space volume; not all of each breath participates in gas exchange.

Residual volume (RV). RV is the gas remaining after maximal exhalation. It cannot be voluntarily exhaled and therefore cannot be measured directly by simple spirometry. RV prevents complete lung emptying and contributes to alveolar stability.

Functional residual capacity (FRC). FRC equals ERV + RV and is the volume remaining after a normal passive exhalation. It serves as an oxygen reservoir between breaths and reflects the balance between inward lung recoil and outward chest-wall recoil.

Vital capacity (VC). VC equals IRV + VT + ERV and is the greatest movable volume between maximal inspiration and maximal expiration. A reduced VC can result from restriction, respiratory-muscle weakness, poor effort, or severe air trapping; the mechanism must be clarified with additional data.

Why this matters: Lung-volume relationships prevent students from interpreting one number in isolation. A low vital capacity may reflect reduced lung expansion, weakness, effort limitation, or air trapping. Identifying which volumes are reduced or disproportionately increased reveals the underlying mechanical pattern.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Residual volume is the same as expiratory reserve volume. Clarify that ERV can be exhaled, whereas RV remains after maximal exhalation.
  • FRC is the amount exhaled during a forced maneuver. Clarify that FRC is the resting volume remaining after a normal passive exhalation.
  • A reduced VC automatically proves restriction. Clarify that TLC must be reduced to confirm a restrictive volume pattern.
  • Spirometry measures every lung volume. Clarify that RV and capacities containing RV cannot be directly measured by simple spirometry.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient has reduced VC and reduced TLC with a normal RV/TLC ratio and normal airway resistance. The reduced TLC confirms reduced total lung size. Because RV is not disproportionately elevated and resistance is normal, the pattern supports reduced lung expansion rather than obstructive air trapping.
Check your reasoning: Two patients have the same reduced vital capacity. One has a low TLC and normal RV/TLC; the other has a normal TLC and high RV/TLC. How do the physiologic mechanisms differ?

Minute Ventilation & Alveolar Ventilation

Minute ventilation. Define minute ventilation as the total volume moved each minute: VE = VT × frequency. Convert milliliters to liters before reporting L/min. A VT of 500 mL at 12/min produces 6.0 L/min.

Alveolar ventilation. Define alveolar ventilation as the volume reaching gas-exchanging alveoli each minute: VA = (VT − VD) × frequency. Dead-space volume must be subtracted from each breath before multiplying by frequency.

Breathing pattern. Slow, deeper breaths generally devote a smaller fraction of each breath to fixed anatomic dead space than rapid, shallow breaths. Equal VE can therefore produce very different VA.

Why this matters: Minute ventilation describes total movement, but alveolar ventilation describes effective movement. This distinction explains why tachypnea can look impressive while carbon dioxide clearance worsens and why a change in tidal volume may affect ventilation more than an equal proportional change in respiratory rate.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Equal minute ventilation means equal gas exchange. Clarify that dead-space fraction and breathing pattern determine effective ventilation.
  • Dead space is subtracted once per minute. Clarify that it must be subtracted from every tidal breath.
  • A higher respiratory rate always raises alveolar ventilation. Clarify that very shallow breaths may mostly ventilate conducting airways.
  • PaCO2 is directly related to alveolar ventilation. Clarify that the relationship is inverse when carbon dioxide production is stable.
Clinical reasoning example: Patient 1 breathes 500 mL at 12/min and Patient 2 breathes 250 mL at 24/min. Both have VE = 6 L/min and VD = 150 mL. Patient 1 has VA = 4.2 L/min, while Patient 2 has VA = 2.4 L/min. Rapid shallow breathing wastes a larger fraction of total ventilation in dead space.
Check your reasoning: A patient maintains the same minute ventilation as tidal volume falls and respiratory rate rises. At what point does the pattern become progressively less effective, and why?

Dead Space & Wasted Ventilation

Anatomic dead space. Define anatomic dead space as gas in the conducting airways from the nose or mouth through the terminal bronchioles. This gas is ventilated but does not contact a gas-exchanging surface.

Alveolar dead space. Define alveolar dead space as ventilation reaching alveoli that receive absent or inadequate perfusion. The gas reaches the respiratory zone, but little or no blood is available for exchange.

Dead-space fraction. Calculate VD/VT by dividing dead-space volume by tidal volume. A VD of 200 mL with VT of 500 mL gives VD/VT = 0.40. A rising ratio means a larger fraction of each breath is wasted.

Bohr relationship. Use VD/VT = (PaCO2 − PECO2) / PaCO2. Mixed-expired PCO2 is lower than arterial PCO2 because dead-space gas contains little carbon dioxide and dilutes alveolar gas.

Why this matters: Dead-space reasoning connects ventilation to perfusion. A patient may move a large amount of gas yet accomplish little exchange if perfusion is absent or if tidal volume is only slightly larger than anatomic dead space.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • All dead space is abnormal. Clarify that anatomic dead space is a normal feature of the conducting zone.
  • Alveolar dead space means an alveolus is not ventilated. Clarify that it is ventilated but inadequately perfused.
  • VD/VT is reported in milliliters. Clarify that it is a dimensionless fraction or percentage.
  • Dead space and shunt are interchangeable. Clarify that they represent opposite V/Q extremes.
Clinical reasoning example: A patient has PaCO2 40 mm Hg, mixed-expired PCO2 28 mm Hg, and VT 500 mL. VD/VT = (40 − 28)/40 = 0.30, and physiologic VD = 0.30 × 500 = 150 mL. The diluted mixed-expired carbon dioxide reflects gas that did not exchange with blood.
Check your reasoning: A patient’s tidal volume remains unchanged while physiologic dead space rises. What must happen to respiratory rate or tidal volume to preserve alveolar ventilation, and what is the cost?

Pulmonary Function Measures & Physiologic Interpretation

DLCO. Diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide estimates transfer across the alveolar-capillary interface. It is influenced by surface area, membrane thickness, pulmonary capillary blood volume, hemoglobin, and capillary transit time.

Diffusion limitation during exercise. Shorter capillary transit time normally remains sufficient for equilibration, but a thickened membrane or reduced surface area can expose diffusion limitation when blood moves more quickly through the pulmonary capillary.

MIP and MEP. Maximum inspiratory pressure reflects global inspiratory-muscle strength; maximum expiratory pressure reflects expiratory-muscle strength. Low values can help explain reduced volumes or ineffective cough when mechanics alone do not account for the pattern.

Why this matters: Pulmonary function tests divide respiratory performance into measurable physiologic components. A patient may have normal airflow but reduced lung size, normal volumes but impaired diffusion, or reduced volumes from muscle weakness rather than intrinsic lung stiffness. Correct interpretation requires matching the abnormal measurement to the property it actually assesses.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • DLCO measures airflow obstruction. Clarify that it measures gas transfer across the alveolar-capillary interface.
  • Low FVC confirms restriction. Clarify that low TLC is required to confirm reduced total lung size.
  • MIP measures diaphragm function only. Clarify that it reflects global inspiratory-muscle pressure generation.
  • MVV is a direct measurement of alveolar ventilation. Clarify that it is a maximal integrated performance test and includes dead-space ventilation.
Clinical reasoning example: During exercise, capillary transit time decreases in a patient with a thickened alveolar-capillary membrane. Ventilation rises, yet hypoxemia worsens because blood has less time to equilibrate across a diffusion barrier that already slows gas transfer. The mechanism is diffusion limitation, not inadequate total ventilation.
Check your reasoning: A patient has low VC, normal TLC, normal airway resistance, and markedly low MIP. Which physiologic system best explains the reduced movable volume?

National Board for Respiratory Care connection

These competencies support the physiologic reasoning used on the Respiratory Therapy Examination: gathering and evaluating cardiopulmonary data, interpreting trends, performing calculations when appropriate, distinguishing normal from abnormal physiology, and connecting findings to the mechanism that best explains the patient’s condition. The goal is not to memorize an NBRC code; it is to recognize the physiology that makes a clinical finding coherent.
