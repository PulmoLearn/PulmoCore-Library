Physiologic Oxygenation, Oxygen Transport & Hypoxemia Mechanisms

This week examines how oxygen moves from inspired gas to alveoli, across the alveolar-capillary interface, into arterial blood, and ultimately to tissues. Before you begin

This chapter prepares you for the PulmoLearn interactive lesson by building the physiology first. AHRC 155 is about understanding why cardiopulmonary variables change. As you read, repeatedly ask: What is normal? What changed? Which physiologic variable changed first? What mechanism explains it? What should change next?

Big idea for Week 2.3: Keep the course centered on oxygen physiology rather than device selection or routine treatment protocols. Require students to name the variable being discussed—PAO2, PaO2, SaO2/SpO2, CaO2, cardiac output, or DO2—before interpreting it. Use a consistent mechanism sequence: inspired oxygen and alveolar ventilation establish PAO2; V/Q matching, diffusion, and shunt determine transfer to arterial blood; hemoglobin and saturation determine content; and cardiac output determines delivery. Clinical scenarios should require students to calculate, compare, predict, or identify the failed physiologic link.

C1: Differentiate hypoxemia from tissue hypoxia and distinguish oxygen partial pressure, hemoglobin saturation, arterial oxygen content, and systemic oxygen delivery.

Oxygen Pressure, Saturation, Content & Delivery

Multiple oxygen variables. Separate PAO2 in alveolar gas, PaO2 as the partial pressure of dissolved oxygen in arterial plasma, SaO2/SpO2 as the percentage of available hemoglobin binding sites occupied, CaO2 as total arterial oxygen content, and DO2 as oxygen delivered to tissues per minute. These variables are related but not interchangeable.

Hemoglobin-bound oxygen. Approximately 98% of arterial oxygen content is carried on hemoglobin, while only a small amount is physically dissolved. PaO2 governs diffusion and strongly influences saturation, but hemoglobin concentration determines carrying capacity.

Arterial oxygen content. Use CaO2 = 1.34(Hb)(SaO2) + 0.003(PaO2). The hemoglobin-bound term dominates under ordinary conditions; the dissolved term is small even when PaO2 is normal.

Oxygen delivery. Use DO2 = cardiac output × CaO2 × 10 when CaO2 is expressed in mL O2/dL. Delivery therefore depends on both oxygen content and blood flow. A normal saturation cannot compensate for severe anemia or very low cardiac output.

Hypoxemia versus hypoxia. Hypoxemia describes low arterial oxygen pressure or saturation. Hypoxia describes inadequate tissue oxygen availability or utilization. Hypoxemia can cause hypoxia, but anemia, low cardiac output, or cellular utilization failure can produce tissue hypoxia without low PaO2.

Why this matters: Oxygenation decisions become more accurate when students identify which variable is abnormal. Increasing PaO2 may improve saturation, but it may have limited effect on total content when hemoglobin is profoundly reduced. Likewise, normal content cannot ensure delivery if cardiac output is inadequate.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • A normal PaO2 proves oxygen delivery is adequate. Clarify that hemoglobin concentration, saturation, and cardiac output also determine delivery.
  • SpO2 directly measures oxygen content. Clarify that it estimates hemoglobin saturation, not hemoglobin concentration or blood flow.
  • Dissolved oxygen is the major oxygen reservoir. Clarify that hemoglobin-bound oxygen provides nearly all normal arterial content.
  • Hypoxemia and hypoxia always occur together. Clarify that tissue hypoxia can occur with normal PaO2 in anemia or circulatory failure.
Clinical reasoning example: Two patients have PaO2 80 mm Hg and SaO2 95%. One has hemoglobin 15 g/dL and the other 8 g/dL. Their pressure and saturation are similar, but the anemic patient has far lower CaO2 and therefore lower potential oxygen delivery.
Check your reasoning: A patient has normal PaO2 and SpO2 but severe anemia and rising lactate. Which oxygen variables are normal, which are abnormal, and why can tissue hypoxia still develop?

Tissue Hypoxia, Compensation & Physiologic Consequences

Compensatory responses. Acute reduction in oxygen availability activates sympathetic and ventilatory responses, including tachycardia, increased ventilation, and redistribution of blood flow. These responses increase oxygen delivery or preserve flow to vital organs but also increase metabolic and cardiac demand.

Cyanosis limitations. Visible cyanosis depends on the absolute amount of deoxygenated hemoglobin, not simply saturation percentage. Severe anemia may prevent obvious cyanosis despite profound tissue oxygen deficit.

Cellular utilization. Tissue oxygen failure may also occur when cells cannot use delivered oxygen effectively. This mechanism should be distinguished from hypoxemia, anemia, and low-flow states.

Why this matters: Clinical signs make sense when linked to oxygen delivery and organ vulnerability. Students should understand why early sympathetic compensation may coexist with worsening tissue oxygen debt and why cyanosis is an unreliable measure of severity in anemia or dyshemoglobinemia.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Cyanosis is the first reliable sign of hypoxemia. Clarify that it is late and depends on deoxygenated hemoglobin concentration.
  • Confusion in a hypoxemic patient is unrelated to oxygen physiology. Clarify that cerebral hypoxia can directly impair cognition and behavior.
  • Tachycardia corrects low oxygen content. Clarify that it may raise delivery by increasing cardiac output but does not restore content.
  • Normal pulse oximetry excludes carbon monoxide exposure. Clarify that standard pulse oximetry may misclassify dyshemoglobins and PaO2 may remain normal.
Clinical reasoning example: A severely anemic patient may have PaO2 88 mm Hg and SpO2 97% yet develop confusion and elevated lactate. The lung has established an acceptable dissolved oxygen pressure, but low hemoglobin markedly reduces CaO2 and tissue delivery.
Check your reasoning: A patient with severe anemia and a patient with cardiogenic shock both have normal PaO2. How does the oxygen-delivery equation identify the different failed components?

C2: Explain hemoglobin-oxygen binding, P50, and rightward or leftward shifts of the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve.

Hemoglobin Affinity, the Dissociation Curve & Gas-Transport Effects

Sigmoid binding curve. Cooperative binding produces a plateau at higher PaO2 values and a steep portion at lower values. The plateau preserves saturation during modest PaO2 changes; the steep portion promotes oxygen unloading as tissue PO2 falls.

Rightward shift. Increased temperature, increased PaCO2, increased hydrogen-ion concentration, and increased 2,3-DPG reduce hemoglobin affinity and increase P50. A right shift promotes tissue unloading but requires a higher PO2 to achieve the same saturation.

Leftward shift. Decreased temperature, decreased PaCO2, alkalemia, decreased 2,3-DPG, fetal hemoglobin, and carbon monoxide increase apparent affinity and decrease P50. A left shift may preserve saturation while impairing tissue unloading.

Affinity versus capacity. A curve shift changes how tightly hemoglobin holds oxygen; anemia changes how much hemoglobin is available. These are different mechanisms and should not be described as the same content problem.

Why this matters: The dissociation curve explains why the same PaO2 can correspond to different saturation under altered temperature or pH and why apparently acceptable saturation may coexist with poor tissue unloading. The Bohr and Haldane effects integrate oxygen and carbon dioxide transport across tissues and lungs.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • A right shift means hemoglobin carries more total oxygen. Clarify that it lowers affinity and promotes unloading; total capacity still depends on hemoglobin.
  • A left shift is always beneficial because saturation is higher. Clarify that excessive affinity can impede tissue unloading.
  • Bohr and Haldane describe the same direction of gas movement. Clarify that Bohr describes CO2/H+ effects on oxygen affinity; Haldane describes oxygenation effects on CO2/H+ carriage.
  • P50 is the PaO2 at 50% dissolved oxygen. Clarify that it is the PO2 at which hemoglobin is 50% saturated.
Clinical reasoning example: During exercise, active muscle becomes warmer and more acidic and produces more CO2. These changes shift the curve right, allowing greater oxygen unloading at a given tissue PO2. In pulmonary capillaries, oxygen loading promotes CO2 and hydrogen-ion release through the Haldane effect.
Check your reasoning: A febrile acidemic patient has the same PaO2 as an afebrile alkalemic patient. How might their hemoglobin affinity and tissue unloading differ even before hemoglobin concentration is considered?

C3: Explain the Bohr and Haldane effects and relate them to oxygen unloading in tissues and carbon dioxide unloading in the lungs.

Hemoglobin Affinity, the Dissociation Curve & Gas-Transport Effects

Bohr effect. Increased CO2 and hydrogen ions reduce hemoglobin oxygen affinity in metabolically active tissues, supporting oxygen unloading where demand is greatest. The reverse occurs in the lungs as CO2 is removed.

Haldane effect. Oxygenation of hemoglobin reduces its capacity to carry CO2 and hydrogen ions, promoting pulmonary CO2 unloading. Deoxygenated hemoglobin carries more CO2 and buffers more hydrogen ions in systemic tissues.

Why this matters: The dissociation curve explains why the same PaO2 can correspond to different saturation under altered temperature or pH and why apparently acceptable saturation may coexist with poor tissue unloading. Alveolar Oxygen, A-a Gradient & Hypoxemia Mechanisms

Alveolar gas equation. At sea level on room air, use the simplified relationship PAO2 ≈ 150 − PaCO2/R, with R commonly approximated as 0.8. Rising PaCO2 lowers PAO2 because alveolar ventilation is inadequate to remove CO2 and replenish oxygen.

A-a oxygen gradient. Calculate A-a = PAO2 − PaO2. A normal or near-normal gradient suggests that low arterial oxygen mainly reflects low alveolar oxygen, as in hypoventilation or low inspired oxygen. An elevated gradient indicates impaired transfer caused by V/Q mismatch, diffusion limitation, or shunt.

Why this matters: The A-a gradient organizes hypoxemia by asking whether oxygen was low before transfer or became low because transfer failed. Oxygen response then helps distinguish mechanisms further, especially shunt from V/Q mismatch or diffusion limitation.

Common beginner errors to avoid

  • Any low PaO2 means the A-a gradient is elevated. Clarify that hypoventilation and low inspired oxygen can lower PaO2 with a normal gradient.
  • The A-a gradient identifies one exact diagnosis. Clarify that it separates normal-gradient from transfer-related mechanisms but does not by itself distinguish V/Q mismatch, diffusion limitation, and shunt.
  • High-V/Q regions can completely correct low-V/Q blood. Clarify that the plateau limits additional oxygen loading in already well-oxygenated blood.
  • Increasing FiO2 corrects every form of hypoxemia equally. Clarify that a large shunt responds poorly because oxygen cannot reach the shunted blood.
Clinical reasoning example: At sea level on room air, a patient has PaCO2 40 mm Hg, so PAO2 is approximately 100 mm Hg. If PaO2 is 55 mm Hg, the A-a gradient is 45 mm Hg, indicating impaired transfer rather than pure hypoventilation. The clinical context and response to oxygen are then used to distinguish V/Q mismatch, diffusion limitation, and shunt. (C4-C6, Exam Q9-Q10)
Check your reasoning: Two patients have PaO2 55 mm Hg. One has PaCO2 68 mm Hg and a normal A-a gradient; the other has PaCO2 40 mm Hg and an A-a gradient of 45 mm Hg. Why are their mechanisms different?

C5: Compare low inspired oxygen, alveolar hypoventilation, V/Q mismatch, diffusion limitation, and shunt as distinct mechanisms of hypoxemia.

Alveolar Oxygen, A-a Gradient & Hypoxemia Mechanisms

A-a oxygen gradient. Calculate A-a = PAO2 − PaO2. A normal or near-normal gradient suggests that low arterial oxygen mainly reflects low alveolar oxygen, as in hypoventilation or low inspired oxygen. An elevated gradient indicates impaired transfer caused by V/Q mismatch, diffusion limitation, or shunt.

Low inspired oxygen. Reduced barometric pressure or reduced FiO2 lowers inspired and alveolar oxygen. The A-a gradient remains normal when the lung transfers oxygen normally.

Alveolar hypoventilation. Reduced alveolar ventilation raises PaCO2 and lowers PAO2. When isolated, arterial oxygen falls in parallel and the A-a gradient remains normal.

V/Q mismatch. Regions with low V/Q contribute blood with reduced oxygenation; high-V/Q regions cannot fully compensate because hemoglobin in well-ventilated regions is already near the plateau. V/Q mismatch usually responds to supplemental oxygen.

Diffusion limitation. Reduced surface area, increased membrane thickness, or shortened transit time impairs equilibration. The effect often appears first during exercise and generally improves with supplemental oxygen by increasing the driving gradient.

Shunt. Blood reaches arterial circulation without contacting ventilated alveoli. A large shunt produces an elevated A-a gradient and a limited response to increased FiO2 because the shunted blood never sees the added oxygen.
