Ischemia → Injury → Infarction — The Cascade

When coronary artery blood flow is reduced or stopped, myocardial tissue progresses through three stages of increasing damage. Each stage has a distinct ECG finding, a distinct reversibility status, and a distinct clinical urgency. Identifying the correct stage on ECG is one of the most critical skills in cardiac care.

Ischemia
ECG: INVERTED T WAVE
T wave flips below baseline
Appears within minutes of flow reduction

What's happening: Cells are hypoxic but still alive. Anaerobic metabolism begins. Metabolic byproducts accumulate.

REVERSIBLE — restore flow quickly and cells recover. Causes: atherosclerosis, spasm, anemia, demand exceeds supply.

Injury (STEMI)
ECG: ELEVATED ST SEGMENT
ST rises >1–2 boxes above baseline
Indicates acute transmural (full-thickness) damage

What's happening: Irreversible cellular injury beginning. Calcium influx, inflammatory cascade. Troponin/CK-MB elevated. Acute complete occlusion.

LIMITED REVERSIBILITY — Emergency: PCI or thrombolytics within minutes-hours. STEMI requires immediate intervention.

Infarction (MI)
ECG: DEEP Q WAVES
Width ≥0.04 sec, depth ≥1/3 of R wave
Permanent — stays on ECG forever

What's happening: Myocardial cells are dead (necrotic). Inflammatory response. Scar tissue will eventually replace dead myocardium.

NOT REVERSIBLE — dead tissue cannot recover. Goal: limit expansion. Q waves remain as permanent ECG record of past MI.

ECG Timeline of Evolving MI: Acute (0–12 hr): ST elevation prominent + T inversion beginning. Recent (1–7 days): Q waves deepen, ST returns toward baseline. Old (weeks–years): Q waves persist permanently, ST and T normalize. Q waves are the permanent scar on the ECG.

Inverted T waves in leads I and aVL. Patient is on supplemental oxygen; ST segment remains flat (no elevation). Troponin drawn — still pending. Chest pain began 20 minutes ago.

Heart Valve Disease — Stenosis, Regurgitation, and Atresia

Normal valves keep blood flowing one direction, open fully, and close completely. When they fail in any of these three modes — narrowing (stenosis), leaking (regurgitation), or absent (atresia) — the hemodynamic consequences directly affect cardiac output, preload, and tissue perfusion.

Stenosis — Narrowed Opening

Leaflets thicken, calcify, and fuse. Valve area is reduced. Blood must be forced through a narrower opening under increased pressure.

Hemodynamic effect: ↑ Pressure gradient across valve. Upstream chamber works harder → hypertrophy → dilation → failure.

Examples: Mitral stenosis (most common) — LA backs up into lungs. Aortic stenosis — LV must generate very high pressure to eject blood.

Regurgitation — Leaky Valve

Leaflets fail to coapt (meet) properly. Valve cannot close completely. Blood leaks backward through the closed valve with every beat. Also called "insufficiency" or "prolapse."

Hemodynamic effect: Volume overload of upstream chamber. Chamber dilates to accommodate extra volume. Eventually fails from chronic overload.

Examples: Mitral regurgitation — blood leaks back to LA during LV systole. Aortic regurgitation — blood leaks back into LV during diastole.

Atresia — No Opening (Congenital)

Valve never formed — solid sheet of tissue blocks flow completely. A congenital defect present from birth. There is no valve opening at all ("imperforate").

Hemodynamic effect: Complete blockade of flow in that direction. Incompatible with life unless alternative pathways (PDA, PFO) allow blood mixing.

Examples: Tricuspid atresia — requires PFO + PDA to survive. Mitral atresia. Requires immediate surgical intervention (Fontan procedure).

Identify the Valve Problem Type

Left atrium is significantly hypertrophied. On echocardiography, the mitral valve area is reduced to 0.8 cm² (normal 4–6 cm²). Left atrial pressure is markedly elevated with blood backing up into pulmonary circulation.
Echo shows the mitral valve leaflets bulging back into the left atrium during ventricular contraction. Left atrium is dilated from the volume of blood returning to it with every beat. Patient has a systolic murmur at the apex.
Newborn presents immediately after birth with severe cyanosis. Echo confirms the tricuspid valve was never formed — a solid membrane occupies its position. The infant survives temporarily via a patent foramen ovale and patent ductus arteriosus.

Complications of Heart Valve Disease — Seven Consequences

Untreated or advanced valve disease creates a cascade of systemic complications. Every organ system is vulnerable when the pump fails. The mechanism behind each complication flows directly from the hemodynamic disruption the valve problem creates.

1. Congestive Heart Failure (CHF)

Valve dysfunction → ↑ chamber workload → dilation and dysfunction → pump failure. Manifests as pulmonary edema, peripheral edema, hepatomegaly, ascites, dyspnea, and exercise intolerance. Most common complication of chronic valve disease.

2. Stroke (CVA)

Valve disease → atrial fibrillation → blood stasis in enlarged atrium → thrombus forms → embolus travels to brain → stroke. Mitral stenosis has highest stroke risk. Treated with anticoagulation (warfarin, NOACs).

3. Thromboembolism

Atrial fibrillation, dilated chambers, endothelial damage → clot formation → emboli to any organ (brain, extremities, mesenteric vessels). Prevention requires anticoagulation therapy in patients with AF from valve disease.

4. Renal Failure

Chronic low CO → reduced renal perfusion → prerenal AKI (cardiorenal syndrome). Pulmonary edema from mitral stenosis → increased intrathoracic pressure → further reduces renal perfusion. Manage by optimizing CO and fluid balance.

5. Arrhythmias

Chamber enlargement + conduction pathway stretching + ischemia → electrical instability. Most common: atrial fibrillation (especially with mitral stenosis). Consequences: loss of atrial kick (↓ SV), rapid ventricular rate, hemodynamic instability, and ↑ stroke risk.

6. Infectious Endocarditis

Damaged valve surfaces are susceptible to bacterial colonization during bacteremia (dental procedures, invasive lines). Manifestations: fever, new murmur, septic emboli, positive blood cultures. Prophylactic antibiotics before procedures in high-risk patients.

7. Death

Acute: sudden hemodynamic decompensation, lethal arrhythmia, massive pulmonary edema. Chronic: progressive heart failure with end-organ dysfunction. Advanced valve disease without repair/replacement has poor long-term prognosis.

Clinical Memory: The complications cluster around two mechanisms — pumping failure (CHF, renal failure, low perfusion) and clot formation (stroke, thromboembolism, endocarditis). Atrial fibrillation bridges both: it reduces CO AND creates the substrate for clots.

Auscultation of Valve Disease — Murmur Timing and Location

Every murmur tells a story: when it occurs (systolic or diastolic) identifies the type of valve problem, and where it is loudest pinpoints which valve. The two most high-yield auscultation rules: systolic murmurs occur between S1 and S2; diastolic murmurs occur between S2 and the next S1.

The Timing Rule: Systolic murmur (S1→S2) = blood being forced through a stenotic semilunar valve OR leaking backward through a regurgitant AV valve during contraction. Diastolic murmur (S2→S1) = blood flowing backward through a regurgitant semilunar valve OR struggling through a stenotic AV valve during filling.
DIASTOLIC
Mitral Stenosis

Location: Apex of heart

Quality: Low-pitched, rumbling

Tool: Use BELL (not diaphragm) — detects low-frequency sounds

Position: Patient in left lateral decubitus position

Why diastolic: During filling (diastole), blood struggles through the narrowed mitral opening. Also look for: opening snap after S2, irregular pulse (atrial fibrillation).

SYSTOLIC
Mitral Regurgitation

Location: Apex — may radiate to the axilla

Quality: High-pitched, blowing

Tool: Diaphragm

Why systolic: During ventricular contraction (systole), blood leaks backward into the left atrium through the incompetent mitral valve.

SYSTOLIC
Aortic Stenosis

Location: Right upper sternal border (2nd ICS) — may radiate to neck

Quality: Harsh, grinding, crescendo-decrescendo

Tool: Diaphragm

Why systolic: During ventricular ejection (systole), blood is forced through the narrowed aortic valve under high pressure. May have a soft or absent S2 (aortic component lost).

DIASTOLIC
Aortic Regurgitation

Location: Left sternal border (3rd–4th ICS) — patient leaning forward, full expiration

Quality: High-pitched, blowing, decrescendo

Tool: Diaphragm

Why diastolic: During ventricular relaxation (diastole), blood leaks backward from the aorta through the incompetent aortic valve into the LV. Associated with wide pulse pressure.

Harsh, grinding systolic murmur loudest at the right upper sternal border (2nd intercostal space); radiates to the carotid arteries in the neck; soft or absent aortic component of S2
High-pitched, blowing diastolic murmur best heard at the left sternal border (3rd–4th ICS) with the patient leaning forward in full expiration; associated with wide pulse pressure
High-pitched, blowing systolic murmur at the cardiac apex; may radiate to the left axilla; caused by blood leaking backward into the left atrium during ventricular contraction

CHF Auscultation — S3 Gallop and Pulmonary Crackles

Congestive heart failure has two hallmark auscultation findings that distinguish it from valve disease murmurs. Both arise from the pathophysiology of a failing, volume-overloaded left ventricle backing blood into the pulmonary circulation.

S3 Gallop — "Kentucky" Rhythm

Timing: Early diastole — just after S2, before the next S1. Sequence: S1 – S2 – S3 (sounds like "lub-DUB-duh" or "Ken-tuck-y").

Cause: Rapid, forceful blood rushing into a dilated, noncompliant, or volume-overloaded left ventricle during early diastolic filling creates a low-pitched vibration (turbulent filling).

Detection: Use the BELL at the apex. Low-pitched — barely audible. Patient in left lateral position improves detection.

Significance: Normal in children and young adults (<40). In adults over 40 → abnormal. Indicates left ventricular dysfunction, volume overload, or heart failure. Often the first auscultation sign of decompensating CHF.

Bibasilar Crackles — Pulmonary Congestion

Timing: Heard during inspiration (and sometimes throughout the respiratory cycle in severe cases).

Cause: Left ventricular failure or elevated PCWP causes hydrostatic pressure to push fluid from pulmonary capillaries into the alveoli and small airways. On inspiration, fluid-filled airways pop open → fine, wet crackles (rales).

Location: Bibasilar (both lung bases) — because fluid accumulates gravity-dependently in the lower lobes when the patient is upright or supine.

Significance: Indicates pulmonary edema from elevated left-sided filling pressures. PCWP >18 mmHg typically produces clinical pulmonary edema. The more crackles extend upward from the bases, the more severe the congestion.

Identify the CHF Auscultation Pattern

A 68-year-old patient with a history of mitral stenosis presents with 3-day worsening dyspnea and orthopnea. Auscultation reveals: fine crackles at both lung bases that do not clear with coughing, and a low-pitched sound occurring just after S2 at the apex when the patient is placed in left lateral position using the bell of the stethoscope. Which diagnosis do these findings support and what is the mechanism?

Stroke Volume Determinants — Preload, Afterload, Contractility

CO = SV × HR. To manipulate cardiac output clinically, you must understand the three factors that determine stroke volume. Each has distinct causes, clinical signs, and interventions.

Preload

"How much blood is coming in?"

Degree of ventricular stretch at end-diastole. Greater stretch → stronger contraction (Frank-Starling). Measured by CVP (RV preload, 2–6 mmHg) and PCWP (LV preload, 5–12 mmHg).

↑ Preload: fluid overload, venoconstriction
↓ Preload: hemorrhage, dehydration, vasodilation

Signs of ↑ preload: JVD, edema, S3, crackles. Signs of ↓ preload: flat neck veins, tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension.

Afterload

"How much resistance must the heart overcome?"

Resistance the ventricle overcomes to eject blood. LV afterload = SVR (systemic arterial resistance). RV afterload = PVR (pulmonary vascular resistance). Measured via MAP (normal 70–100 mmHg).

↑ Afterload: hypertension, vasoconstriction, aortic stenosis
↓ Afterload: vasodilation, sepsis (early), nitrates

↑ afterload signs: ↑ BP, cool extremities. ↓ afterload signs: warm flushed skin, hypotension.

Contractility

"How forcefully does the heart squeeze?"

Intrinsic force of cardiac muscle contraction — independent of preload or afterload. Measured by ejection fraction (EF). Normal EF ≥55%. Heart failure EF <40%.

↑ Contractility: sympathetic stimulation, dobutamine, milrinone
↓ Contractility: MI, acidosis, hypoxemia, beta-blockers, hypothermia

↓ contractility signs: weak pulse, low BP, pulmonary congestion, tachycardia compensating for low SV.

Invasive Hemodynamic Monitoring Devices

Arterial Line (A-Line)

Placement: Radial, femoral, or brachial artery

Measures: Continuous systolic, diastolic, and MAP

Advantage: Beat-to-beat BP monitoring; frequent ABG draws without repeated arterial sticks

Central Venous Line (CVP)

Placement: Superior vena cava via internal jugular, subclavian, or femoral vein

Measures: CVP = right atrial pressure = RV preload (2–6 mmHg)

↑ CVP = fluid overload or RV dysfunction. ↓ CVP = hypovolemia

Swan-Ganz Catheter (PA Catheter)

Placement: Central vein → RA → RV → PA (balloon-tipped, flow-directed)

Balloon deflated: Measures PAP (25/8 mmHg)

Balloon inflated (wedged): Measures PCWP (5–12 mmHg) = LV preload. Also measures CO by thermodilution (cold saline injection).

Elevated PAP and PCWP — Normal Ranges and What Causes Elevation

The two pressures most critical for the respiratory therapist to interpret: PAP (pulmonary artery pressure) tells you about the pulmonary circuit and right heart, and PCWP (pulmonary capillary wedge pressure) tells you specifically about left-sided filling pressure and LV preload.

Elevated PAP (Pulmonary Hypertension)

Normal: 25/8 mmHg | mPAP: 10–20 mmHg
Elevated: mPAP > 25 mmHg
  • Left heart dysfunction — LV failure, mitral stenosis/regurgitation, aortic stenosis → blood backs up from LA → pulmonary veins → pulmonary vasculature
  • Pulmonary pathology — chronic hypoxemia (COPD, altitude), pulmonary embolism, ARDS, pneumonia, atelectasis → hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction or obstruction
  • Pulmonary hypertension — idiopathic, collagen vascular disease, cor pulmonale
  • Right heart dysfunction — RV infarction, RV dysfunction from any cause
  • Fluid overload — excessive IV fluids, renal failure, CHF

Consequences: RV strain → RV hypertrophy → cor pulmonale (chronic RV failure)

Elevated PCWP (Left-Sided Backup)

Normal: 5–12 mmHg
Pulmonary edema threshold: PCWP > 18 mmHg
  • Left ventricular dysfunction — systolic heart failure (weak pump), MI, cardiomyopathy → LV backs blood up into LA → elevated LA pressure reflected by PCWP
  • Mitral valve disease — mitral stenosis raises LA pressure (blood can't flow through tight valve) or mitral regurgitation (LA volume overload)
  • Fluid overload — excessive IV fluids, sodium retention, renal failure → overloads both ventricles

Consequences: PCWP >18 mmHg → pulmonary edema → dyspnea, crackles, hypoxemia, frothy sputum

Physical Assessment for Hemodynamics

Before any hemodynamic catheter is placed, the bedside physical examination delivers powerful data about perfusion status, preload, and cardiac output. These physical findings correlate directly with the invasive hemodynamic values.

Jugular Venous Pressure (JVP)

Technique: Patient supine with head on pillow. Observe external jugular vein distension from clavicle toward jaw.

Elevated JVP (JVD): Veins do NOT collapse on inspiration; visible above clavicle. Indicates ↑ CVP → right heart failure, fluid overload, tamponade, tension pneumothorax, constrictive pericarditis.

Low JVP: Veins collapse easily; flat in supine position. Indicates ↓ CVP → hypovolemia, dehydration, hemorrhage.

Peripheral Edema and Pitting Test

Cause: Fluid accumulates in interstitium when venous/lymphatic pressure exceeds oncotic pressure or albumin is low. With right heart failure: ↑ CVP → back-pressure in venous system → edema (gravity-dependent: ankles, feet, sacrum).

Pitting test: Press fingertip firmly over the ankle bone for 5 seconds. If indentation remains >15 seconds: pitting edema — significant fluid retention.

JVD + edema together: Right heart failure or total fluid overload. JVD absent + edema: local venous/lymphatic cause.

Pulse Pressure

Pulse Pressure = Systolic BP − Diastolic BP
Normal: ~40 mmHg

Narrow pulse pressure (<30 mmHg): Hypovolemia, cardiogenic shock, cardiac tamponade, aortic stenosis. The diastolic does not fall because forward flow is compromised.

Wide pulse pressure (>50 mmHg): Aortic regurgitation (diastolic pressure falls as blood leaks back into LV), hyperthyroidism, severe anemia, sepsis (early vasodilation).

Skin Perfusion Signs

Warm, pink skin: Good CO, adequate vasodilation — or sepsis (distributive state).

Cool, clammy, pale or mottled: Low CO → sympathetic vasoconstriction → ↓ skin perfusion. Classic cardiogenic shock appearance.

Cyanosis: Central (lips, mucous membranes) = inadequate oxygenation. Peripheral (fingers, toes) = poor local perfusion.

Digital clubbing: Enlarged terminal phalanges from chronic hypoxemia (cyanotic heart disease, chronic lung disease).

Interpret the Physical Examination Findings

ICU patient. Findings: JVD present (veins distended to jaw level). Bilateral pitting edema 2+ at ankles and sacrum. BP 88/70 mmHg. Skin cool and mottled. HR 118 bpm. SpO₂ 89% on 4L NC. Bibasilar crackles. What hemodynamic profile does this represent?

Cardiac Output Calculations — Three Worked Examples

CO = SV × HR. Stroke volume may be given in mL (convert to L by ÷ 1000) or already in L. The answer is always in L/min. Normal CO is 4–8 L/min. Below 4 = low output (cardiogenic shock risk). Above 8 = high output (sepsis, fever, exercise).

CO (L/min) = Stroke Volume (L) × Heart Rate (bpm)
If SV given in mL: divide by 1000 first (e.g. 70 mL = 0.070 L)

FAQ

Common Student Questions

Why do Q waves stay on the ECG forever after an MI?

Pathological Q waves represent electrically silent dead myocardium. In a normal heart, every region of myocardium generates its own electrical vector during depolarization. The QRS complex is the sum of all these vectors. When a region of myocardium dies (infarcts), it no longer generates an electrical vector — it becomes electrically silent scar tissue. The absence of that region's vector creates a window effect: the ECG now records the electrical forces pointing away from the infarcted zone, producing a negative deflection (Q wave) in leads facing the scar. Because the scar tissue never regenerates into functioning myocardium, the Q wave is permanent. It can be subtle or become less prominent years later as compensatory hypertrophy of adjacent myocardium creates competing vectors, but it does not disappear entirely. This is why seeing Q waves on a routine ECG in an asymptomatic patient is clinically significant — it reveals a history of transmural MI even if the patient never reported chest pain.

Why is the BELL used for mitral stenosis and S3 but not for other murmurs?

The stethoscope has two sides with distinct acoustic properties. The diaphragm is a flat membrane that filters out low-frequency sounds and amplifies mid-to-high frequencies — ideal for lung sounds, normal heart sounds (S1/S2), and high-pitched murmurs (aortic stenosis at ~500 Hz, mitral regurgitation). The bell is an open cup without a membrane — it transmits all sound frequencies including very low ones (roughly 20–100 Hz). Mitral stenosis produces a low-pitched, rumbling sound because blood is barely squeezing through a tight valve opening at low velocity during diastolic filling — the turbulence frequency is very low. The S3 gallop is similarly low-pitched (the large ventricle vibrating at low frequency during rapid early diastolic filling). If you use the diaphragm on either of these, the membrane filters out the very frequencies that define these sounds — you may completely miss them. The left lateral decubitus position is used for both mitral stenosis and S3 because it rotates the heart so the apex presses closer to the chest wall, amplifying sounds from the apex region. Clinical pearl: if you're looking for either mitral stenosis or S3, roll the patient, use the bell, and press lightly — pressing too hard converts the bell into a diaphragm by stretching the skin beneath it.

What's the clinical difference between PAP elevated with high PCWP versus PAP elevated with normal PCWP?

This distinction is one of the most clinically important applications of Swan-Ganz catheter data. When both PAP and PCWP are elevated together: the problem is left-sided. The LV is failing (or the mitral valve is obstructed), causing blood to back up from LV → LA → pulmonary veins → pulmonary capillaries → pulmonary arteries. The PCWP elevation precedes the PAP elevation. This pattern = cardiogenic pulmonary edema. Treatment targets the left heart (diuretics, afterload reduction, inotropes). When PAP is elevated but PCWP is normal: the problem is in the pulmonary vasculature itself — the left heart is receiving adequate filling (normal LA pressure reflected by normal PCWP), but the pulmonary arteries are abnormally high-pressure. This = pulmonary arterial hypertension (primary or secondary to lung disease, chronic hypoxemia, PE, etc.). The right ventricle must work against elevated pulmonary pressure but the left side is fine. Treatment targets the pulmonary vasculature (pulmonary vasodilators like sildenafil, prostacyclins, endothelin antagonists) and the underlying lung disease. Separating these two patterns is exactly why PCWP measurement exists — PAP alone cannot distinguish left-heart failure from primary pulmonary hypertension.

Can you have a normal cardiac output but still have cardiogenic shock?

Yes — and this is a critical nuance for the ICU clinician. CO = SV × HR. If stroke volume is severely reduced (say, to 35 mL) but heart rate is very high (say, 140 bpm), the calculated CO might be: 0.035 × 140 = 4.9 L/min — technically "normal." But several problems make this compensated state unsustainable: (1) At extremely high heart rates, diastolic filling time is shortened — the ventricle doesn't have time to fill adequately before the next contraction, so SV may fall further as HR increases beyond a critical point. (2) The tachycardia itself increases myocardial oxygen demand (more beats per minute = more ATP consumed), potentially worsening ischemia in a patient whose myocardium is already compromised. (3) If the high HR is a sympathetic compensation for low SV, it will eventually fail as the myocardium exhausts its reserves. (4) The CO may be "normal" but cardiac INDEX (CO ÷ body surface area) may be low if the patient is large-bodied. For these reasons, clinicians also look at stroke volume, mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO₂), lactate levels, and organ perfusion markers — not just cardiac output — to assess whether hemodynamic compensation is adequate.

Why does mitral stenosis specifically lead to atrial fibrillation more than other valve diseases?

Atrial fibrillation develops when atrial myocardium is enlarged, stretched, or electrically unstable — and mitral stenosis creates exactly these conditions more severely than most other valve diseases. Here is the specific mechanism: (1) Mitral stenosis creates a fixed obstruction to blood flowing from the left atrium (LA) into the left ventricle during diastole. (2) Blood backs up behind the tight valve → LA pressure rises chronically (reflected as elevated PCWP). (3) Elevated LA pressure → LA dilates and hypertrophies as it tries to overcome the obstruction. (4) The enlarged, pressure-overloaded atrial myocardium develops fibrosis and electrical heterogeneity — some areas conduct rapidly, others slowly, creating the conditions for reentry circuits. (5) Atrial fibrillation develops when reentrant wavefronts circulate chaotically through the abnormal atrial tissue. Mitral regurgitation also causes LA volume overload (via backflow), but the LA dilates more gradually and at lower pressure, making AF less immediate. Aortic valve disease affects the LV, not directly the LA. Tricuspid disease affects the right atrium. Mitral stenosis is unique in directly and chronically pressurizing the left atrium — which is why it has the strongest association with AF and cardioembolic stroke among all the valve diseases.

Summary — Five High-Yield Takeaways

1. Cascade ECG findings: Ischemia = inverted T (reversible, hypoxic but viable). Injury = elevated ST — STEMI emergency (limited reversibility). Infarction = deep Q waves (permanent scar forever on ECG). Each stage is more severe and less reversible.
2. Valve complications (7): CHF (most common), Stroke (AF→clot→brain), Thromboembolism, Renal failure (low CO), Arrhythmias (AF especially with mitral stenosis), Endocarditis (damaged leaflets colonized by bacteria), Death.
3. CHF auscultation: S3 gallop = early diastole, apex, use BELL, left lateral position — LV volume overload/dysfunction. Bibasilar crackles = pulmonary edema from PCWP >18 mmHg. Mitral stenosis murmur = low-pitched, rumbling DIASTOLIC at apex, BELL, left lateral.
4 & 5. CO + Pressures: CO = SV × HR (normal 4–8 L/min). Convert mL to L first. CVP 2–6 mmHg (RV preload). PAP 25/8 mmHg, mPAP 10–20. PCWP 5–12 mmHg (LV preload). Elevated PCWP → LV failure or mitral disease. Elevated PAP with normal PCWP → pulmonary cause.
