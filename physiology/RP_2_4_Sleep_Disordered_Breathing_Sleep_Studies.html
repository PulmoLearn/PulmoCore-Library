Sleep-Disordered Breathing, Sleep Studies & Clinical Interpretation

Sleep apnea affects up to 30% of the population and remains underdiagnosed. This lesson builds the skills to identify sleep event types from waveform patterns, calculate and classify the AHI, interpret oxygen desaturation data, distinguish obstructive from central events, choose between CPAP/BiPAP/positional therapy, and explain why AHI alone is never the full clinical picture.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners should be able to describe NREM and REM sleep characteristics, define apnea and hypopnea and distinguish obstructive from central events by waveform pattern, calculate and classify AHI, explain why AHI alone is insufficient for treatment decisions, interpret SpO₂ and ODI data from a sleep study, select the appropriate device (CPAP, BiPAP, positional, supplemental O₂) based on event type and oxygen data, and compare home versus full polysomnography studies.

1. Sleep event definitions
Define apnea (≥10 sec complete cessation), hypopnea (≥10 sec partial reduction), and distinguish obstructive from central by the presence or absence of respiratory effort.
2. AHI calculation and classification
Calculate AHI = (Total A + H) ÷ hours of sleep; classify as normal (0–5), mild (5–15), moderate (15–30), or severe (>30). Interpret with O₂ data.
3. Obstructive vs. Central differentiation
Read waveform tracings and identify whether respiratory effort is present or absent to determine event type — which determines CPAP vs. BiPAP treatment.
4. Treatment selection
Match treatment to event type, AHI severity, SpO₂ data, and position analysis. Recognize when AHI + SpO₂ data together — not AHI alone — determine the correct device.

Sleep Architecture — NREM and REM

Normal sleep cycles through distinct stages approximately every 90 minutes. Understanding when and why breathing becomes more vulnerable during each stage explains why sleep apnea events cluster in specific phases and why full polysomnography (which can track sleep stages) reveals patterns that home studies miss.

NREM Sleep — Stages 1, 2, 3

  • Stage 1: Light sleep; easy to wake; transition from wakefulness
  • Stage 2: Deeper sleep; muscle tone decreases; more difficult to arouse
  • Stage 3 (Slow-wave/Deep sleep): Most restorative; HR, RR, BP all decrease; hardest to wake
  • Respiratory pattern: Regular, stable, predictable
  • SpO₂: Remains stable above 95% in healthy adults
  • Dominates: First half of the night

REM Sleep — Most Vulnerable Stage

  • Dreams occur; most vivid and memorable
  • Muscle paralysis: Voluntary muscles atonic except diaphragm and eyes
  • HR and BP increase; more variable than NREM
  • Respiratory pattern: Irregular and variable — less predictable
  • Most vulnerable to apnea: Atonic upper airway muscles + irregular breathing = collapse risk
  • Dominates: Second half of the night (longer REM periods toward morning)

Sleep Event Types — Apnea, Hypopnea, and Snoring

Three types of events are quantified during a sleep study. Each differs in severity, mechanism, and clinical significance. The waveform pattern — particularly the relationship between airflow and respiratory effort — is the key to classifying events correctly.

Apnea — Complete Cessation

Complete airflow stop ≥ 10 seconds

Obstructive apnea: Airway blocked — chest and abdomen effort IS present (muscles trying to breathe), but no air flows past obstruction.

Central apnea: Brain fails to signal — NO effort (chest and abdomen flat) AND no airflow. Both absent simultaneously.

Hypopnea — Partial Reduction

Airflow reduced 30–50% for ≥ 10 sec + SpO₂ drop ≥ 3–4%

Not complete cessation: Air still moves, but inadequately. Partial airway narrowing (obstructive) or reduced effort (central) depending on subtype.

Less severe than apnea — smaller SpO₂ drop, may not cause full arousal, but still disrupts sleep quality and causes hypoxemia.

Snoring — Vibration Without Obstruction

Measured in decibels (dB); loud snoring = 60–80+ dB

Pharyngeal tissue vibration from partial airway narrowing and turbulent airflow. Not always abnormal — but loud snoring + daytime sleepiness + witnessed apneas = OSA until proven otherwise.

Positional: Often worse supine (gravity pulls soft tissues posteriorly). Worse with alcohol, sedatives, and supine position.

The Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) — Formula, Calculation, and Severity

The AHI is the primary metric for quantifying sleep-disordered breathing severity. It tells you the average number of complete and partial breathing events per hour of sleep — but it is a starting point, not a complete picture.

The Limits of AHI Alone — Why Context Always Matters

An AHI of 12 is classified as "mild" OSA. But a patient with an AHI of 12 who spends 66.6% of their sleep time with SpO₂ below 88% is not having a mild problem. AHI counts events — it does not measure oxygen consequences, event composition, symptoms, or position dependence.

What AHI DOES NOT Tell You:
  • How low SpO₂ drops during events
  • How long SpO₂ stays low each time
  • What percentage of sleep time is spent hypoxic
  • Whether events are mostly apneas (worse) or hypopneas (less severe)
  • Whether events are position-dependent
  • What the patient's symptoms are
Always Correlate AHI With:
  • SpO₂ data: Nadir, % time below 90%, % time below 88%
  • ODI: Desaturation frequency per hour
  • Position: Supine vs. non-supine AHI breakdown
  • Composition: Apnea-to-hypopnea ratio
  • Event type: Obstructive vs. central
  • Symptoms: Daytime sleepiness, witnessed apneas
Clinical Rule: If SpO₂ drops below 88% for any meaningful period of sleep — CPAP is indicated regardless of AHI classification. Significant hypoxemia is the clinical emergency; AHI is a counting tool, not a severity verdict.

Oxygen Desaturation Data — Reading SpO₂ and ODI

Sleep studies measure oxygen saturation continuously throughout the night. The oxygen data often tells a different story than the AHI — and in cases where they diverge, the oxygen data takes clinical priority.

SpO₂ During SleepClinical StatusAction
>95%NormalNo hypoxemia; good oxygenation throughout sleep
92–95%BorderlineAcceptable; monitor closely; reassess if AHI elevated
88–92%Concerning — gray zoneTreatment strongly indicated if AHI ≥5 or symptoms present
80–88%Significant desaturationTreatment indicated; CPAP or BiPAP depending on event type
<80%Severe — urgentImmediate treatment; risk of cardiac arrhythmia and hypoxic injury

ODI — Oxygen Desaturation Index

ODI = Number of SpO₂ drops ≥3–4% ÷ Hours of sleep (events/hour)

ODI <5: Minimal. ODI 5–15: Mild-moderate. ODI >15: Significant — concerning even if AHI seems borderline.

ODI can exceed AHI — desaturations can occur with hypopneas the patient sleeps through (no arousal, so not counted the same way but still causing hypoxemia).

Key Sleep SpO₂ Report Variables

  • Baseline SpO₂: Level when awake/transitioning to sleep
  • Nadir SpO₂: Lowest point during any event — most concerning value
  • Mean SpO₂: Average across the night
  • % time <90%: Normal <1% of sleep time
  • % time <88%: Above zero = treatment threshold crossed

Obstructive vs. Central Sleep Apnea — Mechanism and Treatment Diverge

Obstructive and central sleep apnea look identical in terms of SpO₂ drops and arousal patterns. The distinction is entirely in the waveform tracings — specifically whether respiratory effort is present during the airflow absence. This distinction determines treatment: CPAP for obstructive; BiPAP with backup rate for central.

Cheyne-Stokes Respiration — Central Apnea in Heart Failure

Cheyne-Stokes respiration is a form of central sleep apnea producing a crescendo-decrescendo breathing pattern — gradually increasing, then decreasing, then pausing. It is the hallmark of heart failure-related central apnea: pulmonary congestion causes hyperventilation → CO₂ drops below the central chemoreceptor threshold → breathing stops (central apnea) → CO₂ builds again → hyperventilation resumes. The cycle repeats every 60–90 seconds. Treatment addresses the underlying heart failure PLUS BiPAP with backup rate to prevent the apnea episodes.

Sleep Position and Positional Therapy — Gravity Is Part of the Equation

Body position during sleep significantly influences airway patency. In most patients, supine sleeping (on the back) worsens airway obstruction. However, as the case in Section 4 illustrates, position analysis can produce surprises — and the data, not assumptions, must guide treatment decisions.

Positional Therapy — When to Use It

SlumberBump: Physical device worn on the back preventing supine sleep. Inflates/creates discomfort when patient rolls onto back.

Mandibular Advancement Device (MAD): Dental device advancing the lower jaw forward, pulling the tongue away from the posterior airway.

Positional therapy is appropriate ONLY when: Supine AHI is substantially higher than non-supine AHI AND non-supine AHI is normal or near-normal (<5–10) AND SpO₂ remains adequate (>90%) in non-supine position.

When Positional Therapy Is NOT Appropriate

  • Non-supine AHI remains elevated (>10) — positional change won't normalize breathing
  • Significant SpO₂ drops in any position (<88%)
  • Moderate to severe AHI overall — needs CPAP regardless of position
  • Events worse in non-supine position (as in the case from Section 4)
  • Patient compliance with positional device is poor

Home Sleep Study vs. Full Polysomnography — Choosing the Right Test

Sleep studies range from portable home devices to comprehensive laboratory setups. The choice affects what can be measured, what can be diagnosed, and whether CPAP can be titrated during the study itself.

Home Sleep Study (Portable/Type 3)

Measures: Airflow, SpO₂, HR, chest/abdomen effort, position, snoring

Does NOT measure: EEG (no sleep staging), EMG (no REM detection), leg movements

Best for: Initial screening for obvious OSA · Cost-conscious follow-up · Patients unable to access sleep labs · Severe disease (result clearly abnormal)

Limitation: Because there is no EEG, the home study cannot confirm actual sleep time — it measures recording time. AHI may be underestimated if the patient was awake for significant portions.

Full Polysomnography (PSG/Type 1)

Measures: EEG (sleep stages/arousals), EOG (REM), EMG (muscle tone, legs), full respiratory + cardiac + oxygen

Gold standard: Only study that can confirm sleep staging, detect all event types including central, and titrate CPAP pressure in real time

Best for: Diagnostic confirmation · Central apnea evaluation · Complex sleep disorders · CPAP/BiPAP titration · REM-only OSA · Narcolepsy, PLMD evaluation

Treatment Selection — Matching Device to Clinical Data

Treatment for sleep-disordered breathing is determined by four variables acting together: AHI severity, SpO₂ data, event type (obstructive vs. central), and position analysis. No single variable alone determines the device.

CPAP — Gold Standard for Obstructive OSA

Mechanism: Continuous positive airway pressure splints the upper airway open throughout the respiratory cycle — preventing pharyngeal collapse.

Indicated when: Obstructive events dominate · Moderate-severe AHI · Any AHI with SpO₂ <88% · Symptomatic mild OSA

BiPAP with Backup Rate — Central Apnea

Mechanism: Two pressures — higher IPAP for inspiration, lower EPAP for expiration. A set backup respiratory rate triggers mechanical breaths when the patient fails to breathe spontaneously.

Indicated when: Central apneas present · Cheyne-Stokes respiration · CPAP intolerance at required pressure · High AHI with severe desaturation

Positional Therapy / MAD — Mild Positional OSA

SlumberBump: Prevents supine sleep. Appropriate ONLY when supine AHI substantially exceeds non-supine AND non-supine AHI is <5 AND SpO₂ is adequate throughout.

MAD (Mandibular Advancement Device): Advances jaw forward; effective for mild OSA related to tongue position; alternative if CPAP intolerant.

Supplemental Oxygen — Limited Indication

Mechanism: Raises baseline SpO₂ and FiO₂. Does NOT prevent apneas or arousals. Does NOT restore airway patency.

Appropriate for: Sleep-related hypoventilation (low SpO₂ without significant AHI) · Palliative patients refusing CPAP · As adjunct to CPAP in severe chronic lung disease

AHI 12.6 alone: Mild classification → conservative management or positional therapy would seem appropriate. Patient would remain severely hypoxic 66.6% of each night.
Position data: Non-supine AHI (13) > Supine AHI (6.2) — unusual pattern that contraindicated positional therapy before oxygen data was even reviewed.

The fundamental lesson: AHI counts events. Oxygen data reveals their consequence. Both are required to make an appropriate treatment decision. When they conflict, the oxygen data carries greater clinical weight.

FAQ

Common Student Questions

Why can't CPAP treat central apneas if it can treat obstructive ones?

CPAP provides continuous positive airway pressure to mechanically splint the upper airway open — preventing it from collapsing. In obstructive apnea, the problem is a physical blockage: the soft tissue collapses and blocks airflow despite the patient trying to breathe. CPAP directly addresses this by creating a pneumatic splint of pressurized air that keeps the pharynx open. In central apnea, the airway itself is NOT blocked — the brain simply doesn't send a signal to the respiratory muscles. There is nothing to "splint open." CPAP cannot trigger breathing; it can only maintain airway patency when the patient is already breathing. BiPAP with a backup rate CAN trigger breaths by providing mandatory IPAP cycles at a set rate, overriding the CNS failure. The backup rate ensures the machine delivers a breath if the patient fails to trigger one spontaneously within the set time interval.

What does "paradoxical" chest-abdomen movement mean in obstructive apnea?

Normally during inspiration, the diaphragm contracts downward, creating negative intrathoracic pressure — the chest expands outward and the abdomen moves outward simultaneously. Both move in the same direction (outward). In an obstructive apnea, the airway is blocked. The diaphragm contracts (because the brain IS still sending signals), creating negative intrathoracic pressure — but no air can enter. The chest wall is pulled inward by the negative pressure (retraction), while the abdomen moves outward from the diaphragmatic descent. Chest and abdomen now move in opposite directions — this paradoxical movement is the hallmark of respiratory effort against a closed obstruction. On the sleep study tracing, you see the chest and abdomen channels oscillating out of phase with each other. This paradox — effort without airflow — is the defining signature of obstructive apnea.

If a patient has BOTH obstructive and central events (mixed apnea), what gets treated first?

Mixed apnea (also called "complex sleep apnea") begins with an obstructive component (effort present) and transitions to a central component (effort absent) within the same event. CPAP is typically the first treatment — if the obstructive component drives most of the AHI, CPAP treats the majority of events. Interestingly, CPAP sometimes worsens central apneas initially (a phenomenon called "treatment-emergent central apnea" or complex sleep apnea syndrome), where removing the obstructive events unmasks an underlying central pattern. If CPAP worsens central events, the patient may be transitioned to ASV (Adaptive Servo-Ventilation) — a sophisticated device that provides variable pressure support to normalize breathing pattern. For heart failure patients with Cheyne-Stokes, the approach depends on ejection fraction — ASV may be contraindicated in reduced EF heart failure.

Can sleep apnea cause cardiac arrhythmias?

Yes — and this is one of the most serious complications of untreated severe sleep apnea. During apnea, several cardiovascular stressors combine: (1) Hypoxemia directly suppresses the SA node → bradycardia. (2) The associated hypercapnia and acidosis further stress the myocardium. (3) Sympathetic activation on arousal causes a sudden HR spike (tachycardia). This repetitive saw-tooth pattern of bradycardia-then-tachycardia stresses the conduction system and myocardium with every event, hundreds of times each night. The arrhythmias that can result include: atrial fibrillation (the most common), premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), ventricular tachycardia, second-degree AV block, and in severe cases, asystole during the apnea itself. Treating OSA with CPAP reduces AF incidence and recurrence, reduces PVC burden, and decreases overall cardiovascular mortality in observational studies.

What is the RPSGT and how does it relate to respiratory therapy?

The RPSGT (Registered Polysomnographic Technician) is a national board credential offered by the NBRC for sleep study technologists. Respiratory therapists frequently earn this credential as a specialty certification. The RPSGT role involves: setting up and calibrating sleep study equipment, applying sensors (EEG, EMG, EOG, airflow, SpO₂, position), monitoring patients overnight in real time, troubleshooting technical artifacts, scoring sleep stages and respiratory events from waveform data, and preparing preliminary reports for physician review. Sleep medicine is a growing field — sleep lab positions offer RT graduates a specialty track with night-shift work, excellent job stability, and strong clinical skill development in pattern recognition and report interpretation. The clinical skills in this lesson (event classification, AHI interpretation, waveform reading) are the core competencies of the RPSGT role.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Sleep-disordered breathing ranges from benign snoring to life-threatening severe OSA. The AHI quantifies events — the oxygen data reveals their consequences. Both are required for every clinical decision.

Event classification: Apnea = complete cessation ≥10 sec. Obstructive: effort present, no airflow. Central: no effort AND no airflow (both flat). Hypopnea: 30-50% partial reduction + ≥3-4% SpO₂ drop. Type determines device.
AHI classification: 0-5 Normal · 5-15 Mild · 15-30 Moderate · >30 Severe. AHI alone is NEVER the complete picture — always review SpO₂ nadir, % time <88%, ODI, position, and event composition.
Treatment matching: Obstructive → CPAP. Central/Cheyne-Stokes → BiPAP + backup rate. Mild positional (non-supine AHI <5, SpO₂ ok) → SlumberBump. Hypoventilation without apnea → O₂. SpO₂ <88% = treat regardless of AHI.
Study type: Home study = good for obstructive OSA screening and follow-up. Full PSG (lab) = required for central events, CPAP titration, REM-only OSA, complex sleep disorders, or equivocal home results.
