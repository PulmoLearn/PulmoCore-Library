Why Breathing Happens

Breathing is the process of moving air into and out of the lungs. The body does this by changing the size of the chest cavity. When chest volume changes, pressure changes. When pressure changes, air moves.

Plain-Language Translation

The lungs do not pull air in by themselves. The diaphragm and chest muscles change chest size, and that creates the pressure difference that moves air.

Pressure & Airflow

At constant temperature, Boyle’s law states that pressure varies inversely with volume. During spontaneous inspiration, thoracic expansion makes pleural pressure more negative, increases transpulmonary pressure, expands alveoli, and briefly lowers alveolar pressure below atmospheric pressure. Gas enters until alveolar pressure again equals atmospheric pressure.

Airflow gradient
Flow depends on the difference between alveolar and atmospheric pressure.
Lung-distending gradient
Expansion depends on transpulmonary pressure: alveolar pressure minus pleural pressure.
No gradient, no flow
At end inspiration, lung volume is elevated but airflow stops because alveolar pressure has returned to atmospheric pressure.

Atmospheric pressurePressure of the air around the body.Air can enter only if pressure inside the lungs becomes lower.
Intrathoracic pressurePressure inside the chest cavity.Changes with diaphragm and chest wall movement.
Pressure gradientA difference between two pressures.The gradient determines which direction air flows.
AirflowMovement of air.Reduced airflow can cause distress, wheezing, or poor ventilation.

The Inhalation Sequence

Quiet inspiration begins when respiratory centers activate the phrenic nerves and external intercostals. The diaphragm descends and the rib cage expands. Pleural pressure becomes more negative, transpulmonary pressure rises, alveolar volume increases, and alveolar pressure falls below atmospheric pressure. Air then flows inward until the alveolar-to-atmospheric gradient disappears.

Exhalation: Recoil Reverses the Gradient

Quiet expiration is normally passive. Inspiratory muscles relax, thoracic volume decreases, and elastic recoil raises alveolar pressure slightly above atmospheric pressure. Gas flows outward until pressures equalize. During forced expiration, abdominal muscles and internal intercostals actively decrease thoracic volume and increase expiratory pressure.

Quiet exhalation
Usually passive. The diaphragm relaxes and elastic recoil helps air leave.
Forced exhalation
Active. Abdominal and internal intercostal muscles help push air out.
Clinical connection
Patients with obstructive disease may need more time to exhale.
Red flag
Air trapping can occur when the next breath starts before full exhalation.

Compliance, Elastance & Pressure-Volume Relationships

Compliance describes how much volume changes for a given pressure change: C = ΔV/ΔP. Elastance describes the tendency to resist deformation and recoil; it is the inverse of compliance.

Mechanical StatePressure-Volume MeaningCommon ExampleWork Consequence
Low compliance / high elastanceLarge pressure change produces a small volume change.Fibrosis, edema, atelectasis, surfactant deficiency.Elastic work increases.
High compliance / low elastanceSmall pressure change produces a large volume change, but recoil may be poor.Emphysema.Inflation may be easy, but emptying can be slow and inefficient.
Normal complianceExpected volume change occurs for the applied distending pressure.Healthy respiratory system.Elastic work remains relatively low.
Calculation
300 mL ÷ 5 cm H₂O = 60 mL/cm H₂O.
Curve slope
A steeper pressure-volume slope indicates greater compliance; a flatter slope indicates lower compliance.
Regional distribution
If two lung units receive the same pressure, the more compliant unit receives the larger volume change.

Airway Resistance & Flow Patterns

Resistance is the pressure difference required to generate flow: R = ΔP/Flow. Airway radius is a dominant determinant, but flow pattern changes which gas properties matter most.

ConceptMechanismDominant VariablesPhysiology Example
Laminar flowOrderly parallel streamlines, usually at lower velocity in straight airways.Radius and gas viscosity.Quiet flow in smaller straight airways.
Turbulent flowDisorganized flow promoted by high velocity, narrowing, irregularity, and branching.Radius, velocity, airway geometry, and gas density.Severe upper-airway or central-airway narrowing.
Transitional flowMixed pattern between laminar and turbulent behavior.Changing caliber and branch points.Much of normal respiratory flow.
Radial tractionHigher lung volume pulls airways open; low volume reduces support.Lung volume and surrounding tissue recoil.Small-airway narrowing during low-volume exhalation.

Density vs Viscosity

Viscosity is especially important during laminar flow. Density becomes more important during turbulent flow. A lower-density gas can reduce turbulence-related pressure loss without actually enlarging the airway.

Surface Tension, Surfactant & Alveolar Stability

Water molecules at the alveolar air-liquid interface create an inward collapsing force. Surfactant lowers this surface tension, reduces opening pressure, improves compliance, and decreases elastic work.

Without enough surfactant
Surface tension rises, alveoli become unstable, and collapse becomes more likely.
At low lung volume
Surfactant becomes especially important because smaller alveoli otherwise face a stronger tendency to collapse.
Opening pressure
Higher surface tension means more pressure is required to reopen collapsed or unstable alveoli.
Mechanical consequence
Low compliance and increased elastic work—not primarily increased airway resistance.

Mechanism Chain

Surfactant deficiency → increased surface tension → alveolar instability and collapse → reduced compliance → greater pressure requirement → increased elastic work.

Work of Breathing & Respiratory Time Constants

Mechanical work can be separated into the energy needed to overcome elastic recoil and the energy needed to move gas through resistance. Time constants then determine how quickly individual lung units fill and empty.

ConceptEquation or ClueMechanical Meaning
Elastic workLarge pressure change with small volume changeIncreases when compliance decreases or surface tension rises.
Resistive workLarge pressure difference needed to preserve flowIncreases with airway narrowing, secretions, edema, or turbulence.
Time constantτ = R × CDescribes the speed of filling and emptying.
One time constant≈63% of possible volume changeSeveral time constants are needed for near-complete filling or emptying.
Long time constantHigh R, high C, or bothSlow filling and slow emptying; insufficient expiratory time promotes air trapping.
Short time constantLow R, low C, or bothRapid filling and emptying, but total volume may remain small when compliance is low.
Unit X
R 2 × C 0.05 = τ 0.10 s.
Unit Y
R 8 × C 0.10 = τ 0.80 s.
Interpretation
Unit Y fills and empties much more slowly and is more vulnerable to incomplete emptying when rate rises.

Frequency Dependence

Respiratory rate does not calculate the time constant, but it determines whether enough inspiratory and expiratory time is available. When frequency increases, slow units receive proportionally less effective ventilation and may trap gas.

FAQ

Common Questions

Does the lung pull air in by itself?

No. Respiratory muscles change chest volume, which changes pressure. Air moves because of the pressure difference.

Why is the diaphragm so important?

The diaphragm is the main muscle of normal inhalation. When it contracts, it moves downward and increases chest volume.

What is the difference between resistance and compliance?

Resistance is how hard it is for air to move through airways. Compliance is how easily the lungs stretch.

Why does asthma make exhalation hard?

Bronchoconstriction raises airway resistance, and positive pressure surrounding small airways during exhalation can narrow them further. A long time constant may prevent complete emptying before the next breath.

How are compliance and resistance different?

Compliance is a pressure-volume relationship. Resistance is a pressure-flow relationship. A patient can have one abnormality, the other, or both.

Why can high compliance still cause problems?

Highly compliant lungs inflate easily but may have poor recoil. When high compliance is combined with high resistance, time constants become long and exhalation may be incomplete.

Does respiratory rate determine the time constant?

No. The time constant is resistance multiplied by compliance. Respiratory rate determines whether enough time is available for a unit to fill and empty.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Ventilation is a pressure-volume-flow process. Muscle activity changes pleural and alveolar pressures, compliance converts pressure into volume, resistance determines pressure required for flow, surfactant reduces elastic burden, and time constants determine how quickly lung regions fill and empty.

Pressure pearl
Airflow stops whenever alveolar pressure equals atmospheric pressure—even if lung volume is elevated.
Compliance pearl
Low compliance means more pressure is needed for the same volume and elastic work rises.
Resistance pearl
Radius dominates resistance; turbulence adds sensitivity to velocity, geometry, and gas density.
Time-constant pearl
High resistance × high compliance creates slow filling, slow emptying, and air-trapping risk.
