Why Breathing Happens

Breathing is the process of moving air into and out of the lungs. The body does this by changing the size of the chest cavity. When chest volume changes, pressure changes. When pressure changes, air moves.

Plain-Language Translation

The lungs do not pull air in by themselves. The diaphragm and chest muscles change chest size, and that creates the pressure difference that moves air.

Pressure & Airflow

Airflow follows a simple rule: air moves from an area of higher pressure to an area of lower pressure. During inhalation, the chest cavity expands. This lowers pressure inside the lungs compared with room air, so air flows inward.

TermMeaning for BeginnersWhy RTs Care
Atmospheric pressurePressure of the air around the body.Air can enter only if pressure inside the lungs becomes lower.
Intrathoracic pressurePressure inside the chest cavity.Changes with diaphragm and chest wall movement.
Pressure gradientA difference between two pressures.The gradient determines which direction air flows.
AirflowMovement of air.Reduced airflow can cause distress, wheezing, or poor ventilation.

The Inhalation Sequence

Inhalation begins when the brain sends a signal to the respiratory muscles. The phrenic nerve stimulates the diaphragm. The diaphragm contracts and moves downward, increasing chest volume and lowering pressure so air can enter.

Exhalation: Letting Air Out

During quiet breathing, exhalation is usually passive. The diaphragm relaxes, the chest cavity becomes smaller, pressure inside the lungs rises, and air flows outward. During exercise or distress, accessory muscles may help force air out.

Quiet exhalation
Usually passive. The diaphragm relaxes and elastic recoil helps air leave.
Forced exhalation
Active. Abdominal and internal intercostal muscles help push air out.
Clinical connection
Patients with obstructive disease may need more time to exhale.
Red flag
Air trapping can occur when the next breath starts before full exhalation.

Airway Resistance and Lung Compliance

Two beginner concepts help explain why breathing can become difficult: resistance and compliance. Resistance is how hard it is for air to move through airways. Compliance is how easily the lungs stretch.

ConceptPlain MeaningExampleRT Connection
High resistanceAirway is narrowed or blocked, so air is harder to move.Asthma, mucus plugging, bronchospasm.May cause wheezing, prolonged exhalation, and increased WOB.
Low complianceLungs are stiff and hard to stretch.Fibrosis, ARDS, pulmonary edema.Patient may take rapid, shallow breaths.
High complianceLungs stretch easily but may recoil poorly.Emphysema.Can contribute to air trapping and poor exhalation.

FAQ

Common Questions

Does the lung pull air in by itself?

No. Respiratory muscles change chest volume, which changes pressure. Air moves because of the pressure difference.

Why is the diaphragm so important?

The diaphragm is the main muscle of normal inhalation. When it contracts, it moves downward and increases chest volume.

What is the difference between resistance and compliance?

Resistance is how hard it is for air to move through airways. Compliance is how easily the lungs stretch.

Why does asthma make exhalation hard?

Asthma narrows airways, which increases resistance. Narrow airways make it harder for air to leave, especially during exhalation.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Ventilation depends on coordinated muscle movement, changing chest volume, pressure gradients, airway resistance, and lung compliance. Understanding these mechanics helps RT students explain why patients may breathe fast, use accessory muscles, wheeze, or fatigue.

Must know:
Air moves from high pressure to low pressure.
Sequence pearl:
Diaphragm contraction increases volume and lowers pressure.
RT pearl:
Resistance problems make airflow harder; compliance problems make lung expansion harder.
Red flag:
Accessory muscle use and fatigue suggest the normal mechanics of breathing are not enough.
