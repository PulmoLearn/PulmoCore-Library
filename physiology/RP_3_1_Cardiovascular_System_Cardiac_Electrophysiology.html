Cardiovascular System & Cardiac Electrophysiology

The heart is both a mechanical pump and an electrical organ — and understanding both dimensions is essential for every respiratory therapist who monitors patients in the ICU, interprets ECGs, or manages hemodynamically unstable patients. This lesson covers the four chambers and their blood flow pathway, the four valves and their function, the four phases of the cardiac cycle, action potential physiology, and the complete electrical conduction system from SA node to Purkinje fibers.

Heart Chambers & Complete Blood Flow Pathway

The heart is a four-chambered pump with two circuits running simultaneously: a right-sided circuit sending deoxygenated blood to the lungs (pulmonary circulation), and a left-sided circuit delivering oxygenated blood to the body (systemic circulation). The left ventricle generates much greater pressure because it must overcome systemic vascular resistance throughout the entire body.

The Four Heart Valves — Names, Locations, and Functions

Every heart valve serves the same fundamental purpose: allow blood to flow in one direction and prevent backflow in the other. The four valves divide into two anatomical classes — atrioventricular (AV) valves separating atria from ventricles, and semilunar valves separating ventricles from great arteries.

Tricuspid Valve

Class: Atrioventricular (AV) valve | Cusps: 3 leaflets

Location: Between right atrium (RA) and right ventricle (RV)

Function: Opens during diastole → allows RA → RV filling. Closes during ventricular systole → prevents backflow from RV to RA.

Pulmonary Valve

Class: Semilunar valve (pulmonic) | Cusps: 3 cup-shaped

Location: Between right ventricle (RV) and pulmonary artery

Function: Opens during ventricular systole → allows RV → lungs ejection. Closes during diastole → prevents blood from flowing back into RV.

Mitral Valve (Bicuspid)

Class: Atrioventricular (AV) valve | Cusps: 2 leaflets

Location: Between left atrium (LA) and left ventricle (LV)

Function: Opens during diastole → allows LA → LV filling. Closes during ventricular systole → prevents backflow from LV to LA. S1 heart sound from closure.

Aortic Valve

Class: Semilunar valve | Cusps: 3 cup-shaped

Location: Between left ventricle (LV) and aorta

Function: Opens during ventricular systole → allows LV → aorta ejection. Closes during diastole → prevents blood from flowing back into LV. S2 heart sound from closure.

Located between the left atrium and left ventricle; only two leaflets; also called the bicuspid valve; the AV valve on the left side of the heart
Located between the right ventricle and pulmonary artery; semilunar type; prevents backflow of blood from the pulmonary artery into the right ventricle during diastole

The Cardiac Cycle — Four Phases of One Heartbeat

One complete heartbeat cycles through four distinct phases. The key to mastering the cardiac cycle is knowing which valves are open and which are closed during each phase — and understanding the two special "isovolumetric" phases where ALL four valves are simultaneously closed.

1 — Late Diastole (Atrial Systole)OPENCLOSEDLow → RisingAtria contract; ventricles fill with blood
2 — Isovolumetric ContractionCLOSEDCLOSEDRising rapidlyALL valves closed; pressure builds without volume change

The Two Isovolumetric Phases — The Memory Key: Phases 2 and 4 are both "isovolumetric" (iso = same, volumetric = volume) — meaning no blood enters or leaves the ventricle because ALL four valves are simultaneously closed. Phase 2 is the pressure-building phase before ejection; Phase 4 is the pressure-dropping phase before refilling. These are the only moments in the cardiac cycle when all valves are closed at once.

Hemodynamics — Cardiac Output, Stroke Volume & Blood Pressure

Three foundational equations govern cardiovascular hemodynamics. These parallel the respiratory equations you already know — cardiac output mirrors minute ventilation in its structure.

Cardiac Output (CO)

CO = Stroke Volume × Heart Rate
Normal: ~5 L/min | SV ~70 mL | HR 60–100 bpm

Parallel to: Minute Ventilation = VT × RR. The same multiplication structure — volume per beat × beats per minute = volume per minute.

Blood Pressure (BP)

BP = Cardiac Output × SVR
SVR = Systemic Vascular Resistance (Total Peripheral Resistance)

Increase either CO or SVR → BP rises. Decrease either → BP falls. This is why vasopressors (↑ SVR) and inotropes (↑ CO) both raise blood pressure through different mechanisms.

Frank-Starling Law

↑ Preload → ↑ Stretch → ↑ Actin-Myosin Overlap → ↑ Contraction Force → ↑ SV

Greater ventricular filling = greater stretch of muscle fibers = more forceful contraction = larger stroke volume. Exercise increases venous return → heart fills more → contracts harder. There is a limit — overstretching reduces contractility (heart failure).

Systemic Vascular Resistance — The Fourth-Power Rule

SVR is the primary determinant of blood pressure beyond cardiac output. It is controlled almost entirely by arteriole smooth muscle, and vessel diameter has a disproportionately enormous effect on resistance — far more than length or viscosity.

Vessel Diameter — The Fourth-Power Effect

Resistance is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the vessel radius. If diameter is halved:

Resistance increases = 2⁴ = 16-fold

This is why even small amounts of atherosclerotic plaque buildup can dramatically increase coronary resistance. Controlled by smooth muscle in arterioles — the primary site of resistance regulation.

Three Factors Affecting SVR

  • Vessel Diameter (most important) — smaller = exponentially more resistance; controlled by sympathetic nervous system and local mediators
  • Vessel Length — longer vessels = greater total resistance; obesity increases total vessel length
  • Blood Viscosity — thicker blood = greater resistance; polycythemia ↑ viscosity; anemia ↓ viscosity
Sympathetic activation: Arteriole vasoconstriction → ↑ SVR → ↑ BP → skin turns pale (shunted away). Vasodilation → ↓ SVR → ↓ BP (mechanism of septic shock and anaphylaxis).

Cardiac Action Potential — Ion Movements and Membrane Voltage

The action potential is the electrical event that triggers mechanical contraction. Before it fires, the cardiac cell rests at −70 mV. It fires when a stimulus depolarizes the membrane to threshold (−55 mV), triggering an all-or-nothing cascade driven by specific ion movements through voltage-gated channels.

1
Depolarization
−55 → +30 mV

Threshold reached at −55 mV. Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels OPEN. Sodium rushes INTO the cell. Membrane rapidly becomes positive. Peak: +30 mV.

2
Repolarization
+30 → −70 mV

Na⁺ channels CLOSE. Voltage-gated K⁺ channels OPEN. Potassium flows OUT of the cell. Membrane returns to negative. Absolute refractory period ends.

3
Hyperpolarization
Below −70 mV

K⁺ channels remain open too long. Membrane overshoots to −80 or −90 mV. Relative refractory period — new AP requires stronger-than-normal stimulus.

4
Recovery
Back to −70 mV

K⁺ channels close. Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump active: moves 3 Na⁺ OUT and 2 K⁺ IN using ATP. Resting potential restored to −70 mV.

Absolute Refractory Period (~1 ms, during Phases 1–2): Na⁺ channels are inactivated — cannot open again regardless of stimulus strength. No new action potential possible. This ensures unidirectional signal propagation and prevents tetanic cardiac contraction.
Relative Refractory Period (Phase 3, hyperpolarization): K⁺ channels still open; membrane below resting. A new action potential IS possible but requires a supranormal stimulus. Risk period for early depolarization (R-on-T phenomenon).

Match the Action Potential Phase to Its Ion Movement

Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open; sodium rapidly enters the cell; membrane potential rises to +30 mV peak; occurs when threshold (-55 mV) is reached
Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump actively restores resting potential by moving 3 Na⁺ OUT and 2 K⁺ IN; uses ATP energy; returns membrane to −70 mV
Na⁺ channels CLOSE; voltage-gated K⁺ channels OPEN; potassium exits the cell; membrane potential falls from +30 mV back toward resting; absolute refractory period ending
K⁺ channels remain open longer than necessary; membrane potential overshoots below −70 mV to approximately −80 to −90 mV; relative refractory period — new AP requires stronger-than-normal stimulus

The Electrical Conduction System — From SA Node to Ventricular Muscle

The heart's conduction system is a specialized network of tissue with unique properties: spontaneous depolarization at the SA node, deliberate delay at the AV node, and incredibly rapid distribution through the Purkinje fibers. The sequence is hardwired — disruption at any point creates characteristic arrhythmias.

1
Sinoatrial (SA) Node — Upper wall of right atrium. Primary pacemaker: cells spontaneously depolarize at 60–100 bpm at rest. Innervated by sympathetic (↑ rate) and parasympathetic (↓ rate) nervous systems. ECG: initiation of P wave.
Atria
Atrial Muscle — Electrical signal spreads across both atria via gap junctions. Atria depolarize and contract (~0.1 sec). ECG: entire P wave.
2
Atrioventricular (AV) Node — Lower medial right atrium. Introduces deliberate 0.1-second delay — the "electrical gatekeeper." Allows atrial contraction to complete and blood to enter ventricles before ventricular depolarization begins. ECG: PR segment.
3
Bundle of His (AV Bundle) — Passes through ventricular septum. Rapidly conducts signal from AV node. Divides into right and left bundle branches.
RBB/LBB
Right and Left Bundle Branches — Run down ventricular septum. Right branch → right ventricle. Left branch → left ventricle. Very fast conduction speed.
4
Purkinje Fibers — Below the endocardium of both ventricles. Fastest conduction velocity in all cardiac tissue. Distributes signal throughout ventricular myocardium ensuring rapid, coordinated contraction and complete emptying. ECG: QRS complex begins.
VM
Ventricular Muscle — Final destination. Both ventricles depolarize and contract simultaneously → blood ejected to pulmonary artery (right) and aorta (left). ECG: QRS complex.

Match the Conduction Structure to Its Function

Primary pacemaker of the heart; located in the upper wall of the right atrium; spontaneously depolarizes at 60–100 bpm; initiates every heartbeat
Located in the lower medial right atrium; introduces a deliberate 0.1-second delay; acts as "electrical gatekeeper" allowing atria to finish contracting before ventricles depolarize
Located just below the ventricular endocardium; has the FASTEST conduction velocity in all cardiac tissue; distributes the electrical signal throughout the ventricular myocardium ensuring coordinated contraction
Passes through the ventricular septum; conducts impulse from the AV node toward the ventricles; divides into right and left bundle branches supplying each ventricle

Heart Sounds — S1, S2, S3, S4

Heart sounds are created by valve closures and turbulent blood flow — not by muscle contraction itself. The first two sounds (S1 and S2) create the classic "lub-DUB" of a normal heartbeat. Additional sounds (S3, S4) indicate specific pathological states in adults.

S1 — "lub" (First Sound)

Timing: Beginning of ventricular systole

Cause: Closure of AV valves — Tricuspid and Mitral closing simultaneously

Why they close: Ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure at the start of systole → valves snap shut. Best heard at the apex.

S2 — "DUB" (Second Sound)

Timing: End of ventricular systole / Beginning of diastole

Cause: Closure of semilunar valves — Aortic and Pulmonary closing

Why they close: Ventricular pressure falls below arterial pressure at end of systole → valves close preventing backflow. Best heard at the base.

S3 — Early Diastole

Timing: Early diastole (after S2)

Cause: Rapid ventricular filling — blood rushing into ventricle creates turbulence

Significance: Normal in children and young adults. In adults >40: abnormal — indicates left heart strain, heart failure, or volume overload. "Ventricular gallop."

S4 — Late Diastole

Timing: Late diastole (immediately before S1)

Cause: Atrial contraction pushing blood into a stiff, non-compliant ventricle

Significance: Always abnormal. Indicates ventricular hypertrophy or severe diastolic dysfunction. "Atrial gallop" — the atrium is working harder to fill a stiff ventricle.

Memory Connection — S1 and S2 to Valves: S1 = AV valves close (start of systole: Mitral + Tricuspid). S2 = Semilunar valves close (end of systole: Aortic + Pulmonary). A useful way to remember: at S1 the AV valves "slam shut" as the ventricles start squeezing. At S2 the semilunar valves "snap back" as the ventricles relax and pressure falls.

Electrolyte Effects on ECG — Potassium and Calcium

Because the cardiac action potential is driven by ion movements, abnormal serum electrolyte levels directly alter the shape and timing of ECG waveforms. Recognizing these patterns is a life-saving clinical skill — electrolyte disturbances can cause lethal arrhythmias.

Potassium (K⁺) Abnormalities

Hyperkalemia (K⁺ too high):
• Tall, peaked (tented) T waves — earliest sign
• Widened QRS complex (slow conduction)
• Flattened or absent P wave (atrial suppression)
• Sine wave pattern → ventricular fibrillation risk
Hypokalemia (K⁺ too low):
• Flattened T waves
• Prominent U waves (after T wave)
• ST segment depression
• Increased arrhythmia risk (premature beats, torsades de pointes)

Calcium (Ca²⁺) Abnormalities

Hypercalcemia (Ca²⁺ too high):
• Shortened ST segment (faster repolarization)
• Shortened QT interval
• T wave changes
• Can cause bradycardia and heart block
Hypocalcemia (Ca²⁺ too low):
• Prolonged ST segment (delayed repolarization)
• Prolonged QT interval — critical risk of torsades de pointes
• May see neuromuscular signs (Chvostek's sign, tetany) before ECG changes
• Commonly seen in critically ill and post-surgical patients

Scenario 3 — Calculating the Hemodynamic Consequence

After treatment of the hyperkalemia, the team reviews hemodynamics. Echocardiogram shows the patient's stroke volume is 55 mL/beat. Current heart rate is 52 bpm (the bradycardia is worsening — the underlying conduction disease is progressing). The patient's blood pressure is starting to fall.

HR: 52 bpm (bradycardic) | SV: 55 mL/beat

Case Summary — Three Lessons from One Patient

Scenario 1 — Prolonged PR: First-degree AV block. AV node conducts every beat but slowly. PR >0.20 sec = slow AV node. Caused by drugs (beta-blockers, CCBs) or intrinsic conduction disease.
Scenario 2 — Peaked T waves: Hyperkalemia (K⁺ 6.8). High extracellular K⁺ accelerates ventricular repolarization → tall peaked T waves → widening QRS → sine wave → VFib if untreated. Treat urgently: calcium, insulin/glucose, eliminate K⁺.
Scenario 3 — Low CO: CO = 52 × 55 mL = 2.86 L/min (normal ~5). BP = CO × SVR — severe bradycardia drops CO → hypotension. BP equation and Frank-Starling both explain why pacing is needed: fix HR → restore CO → restore BP.

FAQ

Common Student Questions

Why does the AV node deliberately slow the signal if fast conduction is generally beneficial?

The 0.1-second AV nodal delay is absolutely essential for mechanical efficiency. The heart pumps blood most effectively when the atria finish contracting and transferring their blood into the ventricles BEFORE the ventricles begin contracting. If the ventricles contracted simultaneously with the atria (or before), the AV valves would close immediately, the atria would not be able to add their contribution to ventricular filling (the "atrial kick"), and stroke volume would be significantly reduced — up to 20% in a healthy heart, more in patients who depend on atrial kick (particularly those with stiff ventricles). The AV node also serves as a safety valve during atrial flutter/fibrillation — it blocks some of the rapid atrial impulses from reaching the ventricles, preventing an excessively fast ventricular rate that would impair filling time and reduce stroke volume.

What happens to cardiac output during exercise and why?

During vigorous exercise, cardiac output can increase 4–6× from resting levels (from ~5 L/min to 20–25 L/min in trained athletes). Both components of the CO equation increase simultaneously: (1) Heart rate increases (sympathetic activation, catecholamine release) — from 70 to 180+ bpm in maximum exertion. (2) Stroke volume increases via the Frank-Starling mechanism — increased venous return from exercising muscles fills the ventricles to a greater end-diastolic volume, stretching the myofibers and producing a more forceful contraction. Additionally, sympathetic stimulation directly increases myocardial contractility (positive inotropic effect), contributing to increased SV independent of preload. During recovery, heart rate drops first (within minutes), while stroke volume slowly returns to baseline. This is why trained athletes have lower resting heart rates — their enlarged ventricles (cardiac remodeling) have greater stroke volume, so they need fewer beats per minute to maintain the same resting CO.

Why do the ventricles need Purkinje fibers if the bundle branches can already conduct the signal?

The bundle branches conduct the signal down the ventricular septum, but they reach only a small portion of the total ventricular muscle mass. The Purkinje fibers spread out from the bundle branch terminations like a net covering the entire inner surface (endocardium) of both ventricles. Without this distribution network, the signal would depolarize only a small segment of myocardium at first, creating a slow, peristaltic-like contraction rather than the rapid, near-simultaneous contraction needed for efficient stroke volume ejection. Purkinje fibers have the fastest conduction velocity in cardiac tissue (approximately 1–4 m/s, compared to 0.05 m/s in the AV node and 0.3–0.5 m/s in regular ventricular myocardium). This speed ensures that the entire ventricular wall depolarizes and contracts within about 0.08–0.10 seconds, producing the powerful simultaneous squeeze that generates the stroke volume. A disruption in one bundle branch (bundle branch block) forces the opposite ventricle to depolarize through slower myocardium, widening the QRS above 0.12 seconds.

Why is the left ventricle so much more muscular than the right?

Ventricular wall thickness directly reflects the pressure each ventricle must generate to eject blood against downstream resistance. The right ventricle pumps into the pulmonary circulation — a low-pressure, low-resistance system with normal pulmonary artery pressures of only 20–30 mmHg systolic. The left ventricle pumps into the systemic circulation, which must maintain perfusion pressure to every organ from the brain (requiring ~60–70 mmHg MAP) through the furthest capillary bed. Normal systolic aortic pressure is 90–140 mmHg — roughly 4–5× the pulmonary artery pressure. The myocardium responds to chronic pressure load by hypertrophying (thickening) — the same way skeletal muscle grows with weight training. This is why in pulmonary hypertension (elevated pulmonary pressures), the right ventricle hypertrophies over time (cor pulmonale). In systemic hypertension, the left ventricular wall progressively thickens, eventually impairing its filling (diastolic dysfunction) and producing S4 heart sound.

Is the absolute refractory period clinically relevant to the respiratory therapist?

Yes — in two important ways. First, it explains why cardiac muscle cannot develop tetanic contractions the way skeletal muscle can. The absolute refractory period (~250–300 ms in cardiac muscle, vs. ~1–2 ms in skeletal muscle) ensures that every contraction is followed by complete relaxation — essential for cardiac filling. If the heart could be tetanized like a skeletal muscle, it would stay contracted and never refill: instantaneous cardiac death. Second, the relative refractory period (during Phase 3 hyperpolarization) is clinically dangerous — a premature ventricular contraction (PVC) that falls during this "vulnerable period" can trigger R-on-T phenomenon: the depolarization hits when some cells are repolarizing and some are not, creating electrical chaos → ventricular fibrillation. This is why QT interval prolongation (from certain medications, electrolyte disturbances, or congenital channelopathies) is dangerous — it extends the relative refractory period, increasing the vulnerable window where a PVC can trigger torsades de pointes or VFib.
