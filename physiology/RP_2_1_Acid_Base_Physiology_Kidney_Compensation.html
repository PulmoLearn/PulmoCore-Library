Acid-Base Physiology & Kidney Compensation

Acid-base balance is the most precisely regulated chemical process in the human body — and the respiratory system is at its center. Students learn the CO₂-bicarbonate equilibrium, how CO₂ is transported in three forms, hemoglobin's dual roles via the Bohr and Haldane effects, how the respiratory control centers respond to pH changes, the four primary acid-base disorders, and how the kidneys provide slow but powerful long-term correction through bicarbonate reabsorption and ammoniagenesis.

The Fundamental Equilibrium — Lungs and Kidneys Divide the Work

The entire discipline of acid-base physiology rests on a single reversible reaction. Understanding which organ controls which side of this equation is the conceptual foundation for everything that follows.

Left Side — Lungs Control CO₂

CO₂ + H₂O ↔ H₂CO₃

By changing ventilation rate and depth, the lungs adjust how much CO₂ is retained or exhaled. Every breath is a micro-adjustment to pH. Response is immediate — minutes.

Mediated by: Carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells, which catalyzes the reaction 13,000× faster than spontaneous rate.

Right Side — Kidneys Control H⁺ and HCO₃⁻

H₂CO₃ ↔ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

By selectively excreting or retaining H⁺ and HCO₃⁻, the kidneys can make powerful corrections to pH over days. Unlike the lungs, the kidneys can act on both sides of the equation independently.

Full renal adaptation: 3–5 days for maximum ammoniagenesis — which is why acute changes are so dangerous.

CO₂ Transport — Three Forms from Tissues to Lungs

Every CO₂ molecule produced by cellular metabolism must travel from the tissue capillary back to the lung alveolus. Understanding the three transport forms explains why pH changes, how the chloride shift maintains electrical neutrality, and why hemoglobin is both an oxygen carrier and an acid buffer.

Form 1 — Dissolved CO₂ (~5%)
CO₂ dissolves directly in plasma. Provides the diffusion gradient at tissue and alveolar levels. Though a small percentage, dissolved CO₂ drives the entire exchange process — only dissolved gas can diffuse.
Form 2 — Carbaminohemoglobin (~20%)
CO₂ binds to the N-terminus of the globin chain — NOT to the heme iron where O₂ binds. This allows simultaneous transport of both gases. Facilitates the Haldane effect: deoxygenated Hb binds CO₂ more avidly.
Form 3 — Bicarbonate HCO₃⁻ (~70–80%)
The major form. In the RBC: CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. HCO₃⁻ exits the RBC via the chloride shift (Cl⁻ enters). H⁺ is buffered by hemoglobin. At the lungs, the process reverses completely.

The Chloride Shift — Maintaining Electrical Neutrality

When HCO₃⁻ is produced inside the red blood cell and moves into the plasma, it takes a negative charge with it. To maintain electrical neutrality, Cl⁻ (also negatively charged) simultaneously enters the RBC. This ionic exchange is called the chloride shift (Hamburger shift). At the lungs, the entire process reverses: Cl⁻ exits, HCO₃⁻ re-enters, combines with H⁺ to form H₂CO₃, which dissociates to CO₂ and H₂O — and CO₂ diffuses into the alveolus to be exhaled.

The Bohr & Haldane Effects — Hemoglobin as a Precision Instrument

Hemoglobin is not a passive O₂ container — it actively adapts its binding affinity in response to the local chemical environment. Two complementary effects make hemoglobin simultaneously the most efficient O₂ delivery system and CO₂ transport system in biology.

The Bohr Effect — O₂ Release at Active Tissues

Rising PaCO₂ and falling pH in metabolically active tissues cause the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve to shift right — Hb releases O₂ more readily at any given PO₂.

Elegant logic: Tissues producing more CO₂ (high metabolic demand) automatically receive more O₂. The Bohr effect self-regulates O₂ delivery to match tissue demand.

Clinical example: During exercise, working muscle generates CO₂. This CO₂ shifts the curve right exactly where delivery is needed most.

The Haldane Effect — CO₂ Uptake as O₂ Is Released

As Hb releases O₂ in tissues (becomes deoxyhemoglobin), it becomes a better buffer for H⁺ and binds CO₂ more avidly — carrying it back toward the lungs as either carbaminohemoglobin or bicarbonate.

At the lungs: When O₂ binds Hb again, Hb releases H⁺ and CO₂, which reforms CO₂ + H₂O — and CO₂ is exhaled.

Clinical application: Anemia impairs Haldane effect — less Hb means less CO₂ carrying capacity, potentially elevating PaCO₂.

Metabolic Acidosis — Anion Gap is the Critical Divide

Metabolic acidosis is the most clinically complex acid-base disorder because the same pH and HCO₃⁻ pattern can arise from wildly different causes. The anion gap immediately divides them into two fundamentally different pathophysiological categories — each with its own diagnostic mnemonic and treatment approach.

High Anion Gap (AGMA)

AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻)  |  Normal: 8–16 mEq/L

Mechanism: Organic acids accumulate (lactate, ketones, methanol metabolites). Their anions replace HCO₃⁻ in the calculation while Cl⁻ stays normal — so the gap widens.

MUDPILES: Methanol, Uremia, DKA, Propylene glycol, Isoniazid, Lactic acidosis, Ethylene glycol, Salicylates

Normal Anion Gap (NAGMA / Hyperchloremic)

HCO₃⁻ is lost → Cl⁻ rises proportionally → AG stays 8–16

Mechanism: HCO₃⁻ is lost from the GI tract or kidneys. As HCO₃⁻ falls, Cl⁻ is retained to maintain electroneutrality — so the anion gap stays normal.

HARDUPVOMIT: Hyperalimentation, Acetazolamide, Rectosigmoidostomy, Diarrhea, Ureteral diversions, Pyelonephritis (RTA), Vomiting small bowel, Other RTA, Medications, Interstitial nephritis, Thiazides

The Delta Ratio — Detecting Concurrent Disorders

When high anion gap metabolic acidosis is present, the delta ratio detects whether a concurrent disorder is hiding behind it — because the expected 1:1 relationship between AG rise and HCO₃⁻ fall can be distorted by a second process.

Delta Ratio = ΔAG / ΔHCO₃⁻  |  ΔAG = Measured AG − 12  |  ΔHCO₃⁻ = 24 − Measured HCO₃⁻
Ratio 1–2 → Appropriate
AG rise matches HCO₃⁻ fall proportionally. Pure high-AG metabolic acidosis.
Ratio >2 → Concurrent Met. Alkalosis
HCO₃⁻ is higher than expected — something is generating extra base (vomiting, contraction alkalosis, volume depletion).
Ratio <1 → Concurrent Normal AG Acidosis
HCO₃⁻ has fallen more than the AG has risen — a second HCO₃⁻-losing process is present (diarrhea, RTA).

Metabolic Alkalosis — The Two-Phase Paradox

Metabolic alkalosis has a deceptive quality: even when the body "should" be excreting the excess bicarbonate, it often cannot — because the very mechanisms that generated the alkalosis also prevent the kidneys from fixing it. Understanding the generation and maintenance phases explains this clinical paradox.

Phase 1 — Generation

H⁺ is lost (vomiting of gastric HCl, diuretics pushing H⁺ excretion) OR HCO₃⁻ is added (antacids, blood transfusion citrate). Either way, HCO₃⁻ rises and pH increases.

Common causes: Vomiting, loop/thiazide diuretics, hyperaldosteronism, hypokalemia, contraction alkalosis, milk-alkali syndrome.

Phase 2 — Maintenance (The Paradox)

The kidneys should excrete excess HCO₃⁻ — but volume depletion reduces GFR and increases proximal reabsorption of HCO₃⁻. Hypokalemia causes cells to retain H⁺. Aldosterone (elevated from volume depletion) drives further H⁺ secretion in the collecting duct.

Clinical pearl: Vomiting-induced alkalosis is maintained by volume depletion — NOT by ongoing vomiting. Fix the volume and the hypokalemia, and the kidneys can finally excrete the excess HCO₃⁻.

Why Doesn't the Body Simply Excrete the Extra Bicarbonate?

A patient has been vomiting for 4 days and stopped vomiting yesterday. She has low blood pressure, flat neck veins, and hypokalemia (K⁺ 2.8 mEq/L). Her HCO₃⁻ is 38 mEq/L and pH is 7.52. Despite no ongoing H⁺ loss, why does the alkalosis persist?

Renal Compensation — The Kidney as an Acid-Base Machine

The kidneys are the primary long-term regulators of acid-base balance. Unlike the lungs, which can only adjust CO₂, the kidneys can independently control both H⁺ excretion and HCO₃⁻ reabsorption. They do this through three distinct mechanisms with different time courses and capacities.

Three Mechanisms — From Immediate to Maximum

HCO₃⁻ ReabsorptionProximal tubule (>99%)Immediate–hoursPrevents HCO₃⁻ loss; does not add new HCO₃⁻Maximized — nearly all filtered HCO₃⁻ reclaimed; very little appears in urine

Ammoniagenesis — The Powerful Slow Response

Glutamine (an amino acid) is the substrate. Glutaminase in the proximal tubule cell cleaves glutamine to produce:

  • NH₃ (ammonia — diffuses freely into the tubule lumen)
  • α-ketoglutarate (enters the TCA cycle)

In the lumen, NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ (ammonium — cannot diffuse back). NH₄⁺ is excreted in urine, carrying H⁺ out of the body.

For each NH₄⁺ excreted → 1 H⁺ removed from body → net ↑ HCO₃⁻

Chronic adaptation: Glutaminase expression takes 3–5 days to upregulate maximally — which is why COPD patients' kidneys have had time to raise HCO₃⁻, but an acute COPD exacerbation produces life-threatening acidosis.

Aldosterone's Role

Aldosterone increases H⁺ and K⁺ secretion in the collecting duct — and indirectly increases HCO₃⁻ reabsorption. This is adaptive in volume depletion but pathological in hyperaldosteronism.

In vomiting: Volume depletion → renin → angiotensin II → aldosterone → ↑ H⁺ secretion → perpetuates metabolic alkalosis even after vomiting stops.

↑ Aldosterone → ↑ H⁺ secretion → ↑ HCO₃⁻ retention → Alkalosis maintained

The 3–5 Day Timeline

  • Seconds–minutes: Bicarbonate buffering system
  • Minutes–hours: Respiratory compensation
  • Hours–1 day: Titratable acid begins ↑
  • 1–3 days: Ammoniagenesis begins rising
  • 3–5 days: Maximum renal compensation

H⁺ binds to phosphate in the collecting duct forming H₂PO₄⁻ — accounts for ~30% of acid excretion and increases in acidosis

ABG Interpretation — A 5-Step Systematic Framework

A systematic ABG approach prevents errors and detects concurrent disorders that an intuitive "look" would miss. Apply these five steps to every ABG — the compensation formulas are the mathematical test for whether the compensation is appropriate or whether a second disorder is hiding.

StepActionKey Formula or Rule
1 — pHIs pH low (acidemia), high (alkalemia), or normal?<7.35 = acidemia; >7.45 = alkalemia; 7.35–7.45 = compensated or mixed
2 — Primary DisorderMatch the pH direction to PaCO₂ or HCO₃⁻pH↓ + PaCO₂↑ = Resp. Acidosis; pH↓ + HCO₃⁻↓ = Met. Acidosis; pH↑ + PaCO₂↓ = Resp. Alkalosis; pH↑ + HCO₃⁻↑ = Met. Alkalosis
3 — CompensationIs the other variable in the expected range?Met. Acidosis: Expected PaCO₂ = 1.5[HCO₃⁻] + 8 ± 2
Resp. Acidosis Acute: HCO₃⁻ ↑ 4 per 20 mmHg; Chronic: ↑ 4 per 10 mmHg
Met. Alkalosis: PaCO₂ ≈ 40 + 0.6(ΔHCO₃⁻)
Resp. Alkalosis Acute: HCO₃⁻ ↓ 2 per 10 mmHg; Chronic: ↓ 5 per 10 mmHg
4 — Anion GapIf metabolic acidosis: AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻)>16 = high AG (MUDPILES); 8–16 = normal AG (NAGMA/hyperchloremic)
5 — Delta RatioIf high AG: ΔAG / ΔHCO₃⁻1–2 = pure; >2 = concurrent Met. Alkalosis; <1 = concurrent Normal AG Acidosis
Compensation Rule for Reading Any ABG: Compensation moves the secondary variable in the same direction as the primary variable — but never overcorrects past normal pH. If PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ move in opposite directions, that is NOT compensation — that is a second concurrent disorder pointing that direction.

Case 3 — pH 7.32, PaCO₂ 34, HCO₃⁻ 14, Na⁺ 135, Cl⁻ 109

This is metabolic acidosis (low HCO₃⁻, low pH). Expected PaCO₂ by Winter's formula = 29 ± 2 mmHg. Actual PaCO₂ = 34 mmHg. Anion Gap = 12. What is the complete interpretation?

Case 4 — pH 7.25, PaCO₂ 25, HCO₃⁻ 10, Na⁺ 140, Cl⁻ 77

pH 7.25 | PaCO₂ 25 | HCO₃⁻ 10 | Na⁺ 140 | Cl⁻ 77

Severe metabolic acidosis. AG = 53 (very high). ΔAG = 53 − 12 = 41. ΔHCO₃⁻ = 24 − 10 = 14. Delta ratio = 41/14 = 2.93. What does the elevated delta ratio indicate in this context?

Both PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ elevated — yet pH is nearly normal

pH is within normal range (barely). PaCO₂ 58 should cause acidemia. HCO₃⁻ 29 should cause alkalemia. How can pH be normal with both values abnormal?

Case Analysis Summary

Cases 1 & 5 — Mixed Disorders: When pH is near-normal but both PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ are abnormal in the same direction → mixed disorder. The two processes cancel each other's pH effect. Normal pH ≠ no disorder.
Cases 2 — Acute vs. Chronic: Normal HCO₃⁻ in respiratory alkalosis = acute (kidneys haven't compensated). Fallen HCO₃⁻ = chronic. Time course is embedded in the HCO₃⁻ value.
Cases 3 & 6 — Anion Gap is Critical: Same pH and HCO₃⁻ can be AGMA (MUDPILES) or NAGMA (diarrhea, RTA). The anion gap immediately separates them and directs the entire workup.
Case 4 — Delta Ratio Reveals Hidden Disorder: High delta ratio >2 in DKA indicates concurrent metabolic alkalosis masking the full severity of acidosis. Always check the delta ratio in high-AG metabolic acidosis.

FAQ

Common Student Questions

Why does pH go DOWN when CO₂ goes UP?

The fundamental equation: CO₂ + H₂O ↔ H₂CO₃ ↔ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. When CO₂ rises, this equilibrium is pushed rightward — generating more H⁺. More H⁺ means a lower pH (more acidic). Think of it this way: CO₂ is essentially "potential acid" — as soon as it dissolves in blood and hits carbonic anhydrase, it generates H⁺.

What is the difference between the Bohr effect and the Haldane effect?

They are complementary effects happening in different directions. The Bohr effect is about O₂ release: rising CO₂ and falling pH at tissues cause Hb to release O₂ more readily (right shift of the dissociation curve). The Haldane effect is about CO₂ uptake: when Hb releases O₂ (becomes deoxygenated), it becomes a better buffer for H⁺ and binds CO₂ more avidly. Both serve the same purpose — efficient delivery of O₂ and removal of CO₂ at tissues, with reversal at the lung.

Why do the kidneys take so much longer than the lungs to compensate?

The lungs compensate by simply changing ventilation rate and depth — a mechanical adjustment that takes minutes. Renal compensation, especially ammoniagenesis, requires upregulating the glutaminase enzyme in proximal tubule cells. This is a gene expression change — the cells must synthesize more enzyme protein, which requires transcription (DNA → mRNA), translation (mRNA → protein), and cellular adaptation. Gene expression changes take hours to days, and maximum glutaminase activity requires 3–5 days of sustained acidemia to develop. This is why COPD patients who have had chronically elevated CO₂ for years have high HCO₃⁻ — their kidneys have had years to upregulate ammoniagenesis and reabsorb maximum HCO₃⁻.

When should I calculate the delta ratio versus just the anion gap?

The anion gap is calculated whenever metabolic acidosis is present (to classify AGMA vs. NAGMA). The delta ratio is only calculated when the anion gap is HIGH (AGMA). The purpose of the delta ratio is to detect concurrent disorders hiding within the high-AG acidosis — because a pure high-AG process produces a predictable 1:1 relationship between AG rise and HCO₃⁻ fall. If the ratio deviates from 1–2, something else is present. No need to calculate the delta ratio in normal AG metabolic acidosis.

How does vomiting cause alkalosis if you're losing both acid and base?

The stomach secretes HCl (hydrochloric acid) into the gastric lumen. When HCl is secreted, the parietal cells generate HCO₃⁻ that exits into the blood — this is the "alkaline tide" that occurs after every meal. Normally, when HCl is absorbed in the small intestine, HCO₃⁻ from the intestine neutralizes it. With vomiting, the HCl is lost before it reaches the intestine — so the HCO₃⁻ generated by parietal cells accumulates in the blood without being neutralized. Net result: HCl loss = HCO₃⁻ gain = metabolic alkalosis. The maintenance by volume depletion and hypokalemia then sustains it.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Acid-base physiology integrates chemistry, respiratory physiology, and nephrology into a unified system. The CO₂-bicarbonate equilibrium is the organizing principle; compensation is always in the same direction as the primary disorder but never overcorrects.

Equilibrium pearl:
CO₂ + H₂O ↔ H₂CO₃ ↔ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. Lungs control the left; kidneys control the right. Carbonic anhydrase catalyzes it all.
CO₂ transport pearl:
70–80% as bicarbonate (chloride shift). 20% as carbaminohemoglobin (globin N-terminus, not heme iron). 5% dissolved (drives diffusion).
Hemoglobin pearl:
Bohr = high CO₂/low pH → O₂ released at tissues. Haldane = deoxygenated Hb → better CO₂ carrier. Both effects serve the same tissues.
Renal compensation pearl:
HCO₃⁻ reabsorption is immediate but only conserves existing buffer. Titratable acid is ~30%. Ammoniagenesis is 60–70% — takes 3–5 days and can ↑ 3–4× in chronic acidosis.
