Newborn Assessment & APGAR Scoring

Birth is the most dramatic physiological transition in human development. Within seconds, the newborn must shift from placenta-dependent fetal circulation to independent lung breathing. The APGAR score — developed by Virginia Apgar in 1952 — gives every clinician in the delivery room a shared, rapid language for assessing how well that transition is going. This lesson builds the physiology behind each APGAR criterion and the scoring skills you'll need before clinical rotations.

Learning Objectives

This lesson focuses on a single core skill: given clinical findings of a newborn, accurately calculate the APGAR score and determine the appropriate level of care. The physiology behind each criterion explains why certain findings are scored as they are.

1. Explain the neonatal transition
Describe the three fetal shunts and how they close at birth. Explain why PVR drops dramatically at first breath and why this is essential for survival.
2. Define all five APGAR criteria
Identify what each letter represents (Appearance, Pulse, Grimace, Activity, Respiration) and the score for each finding (0, 1, or 2). Explain the physiology behind each criterion.
3. Calculate APGAR scores accurately
Given a clinical description of a newborn, assign the correct score for each of the 5 criteria and sum to a total. Interpret the total as normal (7–10), moderate distress (4–6), or critical (0–3).
4. Determine intervention level
Match the APGAR score range to the appropriate response: routine care (7–10), supplemental O₂ and stimulation (4–6), or full resuscitation including PPV and chest compressions (0–3).

Neonatal Transition — The Most Dramatic Physiological Change in Human Life

In utero, the placenta performs every function the lungs, kidneys, liver, and gastrointestinal tract will eventually handle independently. At the moment of birth, the placenta is severed — and within seconds to minutes, the newborn must independently manage gas exchange, circulation, temperature, and acid-base balance for the first time. The APGAR score is a rapid window into how well this transition is succeeding.

0–1 minute
  • First cry/breath triggered by cold, hypoxia, touch
  • Acrocyanosis common (hands/feet blue)
  • Irregular respirations normal
  • Heart rate increases from stress
  • Temperature begins dropping
1–5 minutes
  • Breathing becomes more regular
  • Acrocyanosis usually resolves
  • HR stabilizes (120–160 bpm)
  • Muscle tone improves with stimulation
  • Reflexes become more responsive
First hour
  • Circulation essentially neonatal
  • Foramen ovale functionally closed
  • Breathing pattern established
  • Brown fat thermogenesis activating
  • Most acid-base abnormalities resolving
24 hours
  • Ductus arteriosus functionally closed
  • Temperature regulation improving
  • Feeding reflexes active
  • BP stabilizing
  • Anatomical shunt closure ongoing
The Key Oxygen Shift: Intrauterine PO₂ = 35–50 mmHg (placental delivery). Postnatal PO₂ (healthy infant) = 80–100 mmHg. This 2–3× increase in available oxygen represents an enormous metabolic shift — from anaerobic-tolerant fetal metabolism to aerobic neonatal metabolism — that must occur within minutes.

Fetal Shunts — The Three Bypasses That Close at Birth

In utero, the lungs are fluid-filled and non-functional. To prevent blood from wastefully circulating through non-ventilating lungs and the developing liver, the fetus has three vascular shunts that reroute blood. At birth, all three must close — failure to close creates the pathological states called patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) and persistent fetal circulation.

Foramen Ovale

Location: Opening between right and left atria

Fetal function: Allows blood to bypass the right ventricle and fluid-filled lungs; blood flows RA→LA

Closure at birth: As PVR drops, LA pressure rises and RA pressure falls (loss of placental circulation) → flap seals shut. Functionally closed within minutes to hours.

Patent in ~25% of adults (PFO) — usually clinically silent but can be route for paradoxical emboli

Ductus Arteriosus

Location: Connects pulmonary artery to descending aorta

Fetal function: Diverts ~90% of RV output away from fluid-filled lungs directly to systemic circulation

Closure at birth: Rising oxygen + falling prostaglandins contract smooth muscle in ductus wall. Functional closure: 12–24 hours. Anatomical: 3–7 days.

PDA (Patent Ductus Arteriosus) — common in preterm infants; may require indomethacin or surgical ligation

Ductus Venosus

Location: Within liver; connects umbilical vein to inferior vena cava

Fetal function: Allows oxygenated blood from placenta (umbilical vein) to bypass hepatic sinusoids and reach the heart directly

Closure at birth: Umbilical cord clamping eliminates umbilical blood flow → ductus loses its purpose immediately. Becomes the ligamentum venosum.

Anatomical closure: days to weeks postnatally

The First Breath — Why PVR Drops and Why It Matters

In utero, pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) is very high — blood is actively steered away from fluid-filled, non-ventilating lungs. The first breath triggers a cascade that transforms this in seconds: PVR plummets, pulmonary blood flow surges, and the lungs take over as the organ of gas exchange. This physiological pivot explains why breathing effort is the most critical APGAR criterion for the respiratory therapist.

Stimuli for the First Breath

  • Cold/thermal stimulation — room air vs. 37°C intrauterine environment
  • Hypoxia and hypercarbia — cord clamping removes CO₂ elimination pathway; CO₂ rises, driving brainstem respiratory centers
  • Physical stimulation — touch, pressure changes during delivery
  • Sensory input — skin receptors activate brainstem
RT Implication: Stimulation (drying, rubbing, tactile stimulation) is the first intervention for a poorly transitioning newborn — before supplemental oxygen or PPV. It mimics the natural stimuli the delivery process provides.

Why PVR Drops — The Cascade

  1. Lungs expand → pulmonary vessels physically stretch and dilate
  2. Oxygen enters alveoli → powerful pulmonary arteriolar vasodilator → PVR falls dramatically
  3. Low PVR → blood now flows preferentially through pulmonary vessels (not through shunts)
  4. Increased pulmonary blood flow → increased LA return → LA pressure rises above RA → Foramen Ovale closes
  5. Higher systemic BP + lower PVR → Ductus Arteriosus flow reverses and then constricts

Surfactant's role: Type II alveolar cells release surfactant (already produced in term infants) → reduces surface tension → prevents alveolar collapse between breaths → establishes functional residual capacity (FRC).

What Is the APGAR Score?

The APGAR score is a rapid, standardized, reproducible tool for assessing newborn condition immediately after birth. It was developed by Dr. Virginia Apgar in 1952 — an anesthesiologist who recognized the need for an objective way to assess newborn status before routine monitoring equipment existed. The name is both an acronym and an eponym.

What APGAR measures: The degree to which the newborn has successfully transitioned from fetal to neonatal life. Each criterion reflects a different system's participation in this transition. The total score communicates urgency of response to every clinician in the room with a single number.
What APGAR does NOT measure: Long-term neurological prognosis. A low 1-minute score does not predict long-term disability. It specifically assesses immediate adaptation. A low score means intervention is needed now — not necessarily that the baby will have problems later.
A — Appearance (Skin Color)  |  P — Pulse (Heart Rate)  |  G — Grimace (Reflex Irritability)
A — Activity (Muscle Tone)  |  R — Respiration (Breathing Effort)
Each criterion scored 0, 1, or 2. Maximum total score = 10.
RT Clinical Context: In smaller hospitals, one respiratory therapist may cover both floor assignments AND the delivery room. You may be the first — or only — provider assessing the newborn. APGAR scoring and neonatal resuscitation are essential RT competencies, not just obstetric team skills.

The Five APGAR Criteria — Score 0, 1, or 2 for Each

Each criterion represents a physiological system participating in the birth transition. Understanding why each finding is scored as it is — the physiology behind the number — is what turns memorization into clinical reasoning.

A
Appearance — Skin Color

Physiology: Reflects oxygenation and peripheral perfusion. Acrocyanosis (blue hands/feet, pink body) is normal in the first minutes — peripheral vessels constrict in cold stress while central oxygenation is adequate. Score 1 is not alarming at 1 minute; it should resolve by 5 minutes. Central cyanosis (blue trunk, lips, mucous membranes) is always abnormal.

0Pale or centrally blue (shock, severe hypoxia)
1Blue hands/feet only — pink body (acrocyanosis = normal early)
2Pink all over — complete transition; adequate oxygenation
P
Pulse — Heart Rate

Physiology: Most critical indicator of cardiac output. Normal newborn HR = 120–160 bpm. Bradycardia (<100 bpm) is ominous — hypoxia triggers the vagal diving reflex, dropping HR → CO drops → tissue perfusion worsens → acidosis deepens. This is a vicious cycle requiring immediate intervention. HR <60 after PPV → chest compressions.

0Absent — cardiac arrest; no output; immediate CPR
1<100 bpm — bradycardia; inadequate CO; stimulate and oxygenate
2>100 bpm — adequate cardiac output; normal transition
G
Grimace — Reflex Irritability

Physiology: Reflects CNS integrity and intact neurological pathways. Assessed by response to nasal catheter suctioning or physical stimulation. Crying/withdrawing vigorously = intact CNS. No response = severe CNS depression (hypoxia damages cortex and brainstem preferentially). Absent grimace = baby cannot protect its own airway.

0No response — severe CNS depression; hypoxia or sedation
1Grimace/withdrawal only — some CNS function; depressed
2Crying/actively pulling away — intact protective reflexes; good CNS
A
Activity — Muscle Tone

Physiology: Reflects CNS oxygenation status. Hypoxia depresses the motor cortex and pyramidal tracts → flaccidity. A well-oxygenated newborn maintains flexion (the intrauterine position) and actively resists extension. Flaccid baby cannot protect its airway, coordinate breathing, or swallow safely. Hypoxic injury progression: decreased tone → flaccidity → no response.

0Flaccid/limp — severe CNS depression; cannot maintain airway
1Some flexion of extremities — mild CNS depression
2Well flexed, active movement of all limbs — intact CNS and oxygenation
R
Respiration — Breathing Effort

Physiology: The most critical criterion for the respiratory therapist. A strong cry requires intact brainstem respiratory centers, vagus nerve, laryngeal muscles, and respiratory musculature. Apnea = immediate need for PPV. Gasping is not a substitute for regular breathing — it indicates severe brainstem hypoxia. Normal neonatal RR = 30–60 breaths/min.

0Apnea — no spontaneous effort; immediate PPV required
1Irregular, slow, shallow, or gasping — stimulate; supplemental O₂
2Strong cry, regular breathing (30–60/min) — intact respiratory drive

Assign the Correct Score for Each Clinical Finding

Infant has a vigorous, lusty cry that is sustained for several seconds. Respiratory rate 44 breaths/minute, regular pattern.
Arms and legs show some passive flexion but baby does not actively resist extension. Not completely limp, but tone is reduced.
Body and trunk are pink. Both hands and feet are bluish. Lips and mucous membranes are pink.
When a nasal catheter is inserted for suctioning, the infant immediately begins crying loudly and pulls both legs up toward the abdomen.

Score Interpretation — Three Ranges, Three Responses

7–10
Normal
Baby adapting well to extrauterine life. Routine newborn care. Skin-to-skin with mother. No special interventions for score-related concerns.
4–6
Moderate Distress
Struggling with transition. Supplemental oxygen, gentle stimulation (rub back, tap soles), suctioning if needed, warmth. Reassess at 5 minutes.
0–3
Critical/Severe Distress
Failing to transition. Immediate PPV (bag-mask). If HR <60 after 30s PPV → chest compressions. Consider intubation + epinephrine. Reassess every 5 minutes.
Neonatal Resuscitation Trigger: HR <60 bpm after 30 seconds of effective PPV → start chest compressions at 3:1 ratio (3 compressions to 1 breath). If HR remains <60 → epinephrine (IV or intraosseous). This is one of the most time-critical resuscitation situations in medicine.

Total APGAR = 9. HR 148 bpm (2), strong cry at 44 breaths/min (2), well flexed active movement (2), crying and pulling away on suction (2), acrocyanosis only (1). Baby vigorous and crying.
Total APGAR = 5. HR 86 bpm (1), weak irregular respiratory effort (1), some flexion (1), grimace only to suction (1), blue hands and feet pink body (1). Baby not vigorous but has some effort.

1-Minute vs. 5-Minute APGAR — Timing Tells the Story

1-Minute APGAR

Purpose: Assess immediate adaptation at birth. Reflects the baby's response to the birth process itself — delivery stress, brief hypoxia, residual amniotic fluid in airways, and the initial thermal shock.

Clinical significance: A low 1-minute score does NOT necessarily predict long-term outcome. Many healthy infants transiently score lower in the first minute due to normal physiological lag in completing the transition. What matters is the TREND.

Action: Determines initial intervention level. Guides whether to start supplemental O₂/stimulation or move directly to PPV.

5-Minute APGAR

Purpose: Assess ongoing transition and response to any interventions. More predictive of neonatal outcome than the 1-minute score.

Clinical significance: Shows whether baby is adapting (score improving) or deteriorating (score unchanged or worse). A 5-minute score ≥7 generally indicates successful transition. A 5-minute score <7 is more concerning than a 1-minute score <7.

If 5-minute score <7: Reassess APGAR every 5 minutes during resuscitative efforts. Continue until score improves or resuscitation decisions are made.

The Clinical Story: A baby with 1-minute APGAR of 5 who responds to stimulation and supplemental oxygen with a 5-minute APGAR of 8 is having a completely normal transition — just slightly delayed. A baby with 1-minute APGAR of 5 whose 5-minute score is 3 despite intervention is actively deteriorating and needs escalation to PPV, compressions, and medications. The number alone means nothing without the trend.

Normal Transition Findings vs. Abnormal Findings Requiring Intervention

One of the most important clinical distinctions: some findings that look alarming to the untrained eye are physiologically normal in the first minute. Others that appear subtle are actually emergencies.

Normal — May Be Present in the First 1–2 Minutes

  • Acrocyanosis (blue hands/feet, pink body) — Score 1 for Appearance; resolves by 5 min
  • Irregular, gasping-then-improving respirations — Score 1 for Respiration initially; normal as airways clear
  • Mildly decreased tone initially — improves rapidly with stimulation and oxygenation
  • HR initially elevated or variable — normalizes within 1–5 minutes
  • 1-minute APGAR 6–7 — very common in healthy infants; most improve to 8–10 by 5 minutes

Abnormal — Requires Immediate Intervention

  • Central cyanosis (blue lips, trunk, mucous membranes) — not acrocyanosis; indicates hypoxemia
  • Apnea — zero respiratory effort past the first 30 seconds → PPV immediately
  • Gasping that does not transition to breathing — gasping alone is NOT breathing; it indicates brainstem hypoxia
  • HR <100 bpm — bradycardia; ominous even at 1 minute; requires immediate O₂ and stimulation
  • HR <60 bpm — after 30 seconds of PPV → chest compressions immediately
  • Flaccidity — complete loss of muscle tone → CNS injury; cannot protect airway
  • Worsening trend — APGAR score declining from 1 to 5 minutes = deteriorating; escalate immediately

Scoring the 1-Minute and 5-Minute APGAR

Stage 1 — The 1-Minute Assessment

You are the respiratory therapist covering the delivery room at a small rural hospital. A 36-week premature infant is delivered. The team immediately hands the infant to you for assessment. You observe the following at 1 minute:

1-MINUTE ASSESSMENT FINDINGS:
Heart Rate: 82 bpm (palpable, irregular)
Respiratory Effort: Irregular, shallow — no cry (RR ~12/min with pauses)
Muscle Tone: Some flexion in arms, legs are flaccid
Reflex (to nasal suction): Grimace only — no cry, no withdrawal
Color: Blue hands and feet; pink trunk and face

Calculate the 1-minute APGAR score (P + R + A + G + A = ?) and identify the appropriate level of care.

Stage 2 — The 5-Minute Reassessment

You applied free-flow oxygen, dried the infant vigorously, placed under the radiant warmer, and stimulated by rubbing the back and flicking the soles. The team reassesses at 5 minutes:

5-MINUTE ASSESSMENT FINDINGS:
Heart Rate: 128 bpm (regular)
Respiratory Effort: Regular breathing at 36/min; weak but audible cry
Muscle Tone: Well-flexed extremities; active movement when stimulated
Reflex: Crying and pulling away with nasal suction
Color: Blue hands and feet still present; pink trunk

Calculate the 5-minute APGAR score. What does the change from 5 to this score indicate, and what is the next clinical decision?

Case Summary — Trending and Decision-Making

1-Minute APGAR = 5: HR 82 (1) + Irregular resp (1) + Some tone (1) + Grimace (1) + Acrocyanosis (1) = 5. Moderate distress. Response: O₂, stimulation, warmth, suction. Not yet PPV.
5-Minute APGAR = 9: HR 128 (2) + Weak cry/regular (2) + Well flexed (2) + Cry/pulls away (2) + Acrocyanosis (1) = 9. Successful transition. No further APGAR reassessment needed.
The Trend Lesson: Score 5 → 9 in 4 minutes = infant responding well to simple interventions. If score had stayed at 5 or dropped → PPV, then compressions if HR still <60. Worsening trend = escalate immediately.

FAQ

Common Student Questions

Can a healthy newborn ever score less than 7 at 1 minute?

Yes — this is very common and does not indicate a problem if the score rapidly improves. Many healthy, vigorous term infants score 7–9 at 1 minute because of the physiological lag in completing the transition. The acrocyanosis alone often keeps healthy infants from scoring 10 at 1 minute (scoring 1 for appearance instead of 2). A baby who is vigorous but has mild acrocyanosis might score 9 at 1 minute and 10 at 5 minutes — this is completely normal. The guidelines emphasize that the APGAR score should not be used to determine whether to begin resuscitation (you should begin resuscitation if indicated based on clinical findings, without waiting for the 1-minute APGAR), and a low 1-minute score alone does not predict long-term neurological outcome. The 5-minute score and the trend between 1 and 5 minutes are more clinically meaningful than the 1-minute score alone.

Why is gasping worse than complete apnea in terms of APGAR scoring?

Gasping and apnea both score 0 for Respiration in the APGAR scoring system, but gasping is clinically considered a sign of severe brainstem hypoxia, not an intermediate state between absent breathing and regular breathing. Here is why: regular breathing requires complex coordination from multiple brainstem nuclei (pre-Bötzinger complex, nucleus tractus solitarius, etc.) and intact respiratory pattern generators. Gasping is a primitive reflex generated by a more caudal brainstem structure that persists even when the higher respiratory centers are severely depressed — it represents a last-gasp attempt to overcome an airway obstruction or severe hypoxia. Gasping does not provide effective ventilation because the pattern (sudden large inspiration, then no effort) does not create sustained tidal exchange. An infant who is only gasping is as much in need of immediate PPV as an infant who is apneic — the gasp does not provide oxygenation. Clinically, seeing a gasping infant requires the same response as seeing apnea: positive pressure ventilation without delay.

How does maternal narcotic analgesia during labor affect APGAR scores?

Opioids given to the mother during labor cross the placenta and affect the fetus. The fetal brain and respiratory centers are affected proportionally to the dose and timing — opioids given close to delivery (within 2–4 hours) may result in a depressed newborn. The primary effects on APGAR criteria: (1) Respiration: opioids depress the brainstem respiratory centers → reduced respiratory drive → weak/irregular breathing or apnea (score 0–1). (2) Grimace: opioid-induced CNS depression reduces reflex responses → grimace only or no response. (3) Activity/muscle tone: general CNS depression → reduced tone. (4) Pulse: usually preserved (opioids don't primarily affect the heart in this context), but severe depression may cause bradycardia from hypoxia. Treatment: For respiratory depression from maternal narcotics, naloxone (Narcan) can be administered to the infant to reverse opioid effects — it blocks opioid receptors and restores respiratory drive. However, PPV should NOT be delayed while waiting for naloxone to work — if the infant is not breathing, start PPV first. Naloxone use in neonates should be with caution in infants of opioid-dependent mothers, as it can precipitate acute withdrawal (seizures).

What is Persistent Fetal Circulation (PFC) and how does it affect APGAR scores?

Persistent Fetal Circulation (PFC), also called Persistent Pulmonary Hypertension of the Newborn (PPHN), occurs when the fetal vascular shunts (primarily the foramen ovale and ductus arteriosus) fail to close appropriately after birth — pulmonary vascular resistance remains high despite successful lung inflation. Causes include: severe meconium aspiration (meconium chemicals cause pulmonary arteriolar constriction), congenital diaphragmatic hernia (hypoplastic lungs maintain high PVR), perinatal asphyxia, sepsis, and idiopathic cases. In PFC, despite what appears to be adequate ventilation, the baby remains severely cyanotic because: blood flows right-to-left through the still-open ductus arteriosus and foramen ovale (bypassing the lungs) → deoxygenated blood enters the systemic circulation → central cyanosis that DOES NOT IMPROVE with supplemental oxygen alone. The APGAR score in PFC: poor appearance (central cyanosis, score 0), poor saturation, and likely poor respiration depending on the underlying cause. A hallmark of PFC is failure to respond to supplemental oxygen — the SpO₂ remains low despite 100% FiO₂ because the blood is bypassing the oxygenated lungs through the fetal shunts. Treatment: pulmonary vasodilators (inhaled nitric oxide — iNO — is the most effective), high-frequency ventilation, ECMO in severe cases. This is one of the most challenging neonatal conditions and requires NICU-level care.

Summary — The APGAR Score at a Glance

Neonatal Transition: 3 fetal shunts close at birth: Foramen Ovale (RA→LA bypass), Ductus Arteriosus (PA→Aorta bypass, PDA if stays open in preterm), Ductus Venosus (umbilical vein→IVC, closes at cord clamping). First breath drops PVR dramatically via O₂ vasodilation + mechanical capillary stretch.
APGAR Criteria (0, 1, or 2 each): A=Appearance (2=pink all over; 1=acrocyanosis; 0=pale/central cyanosis). P=Pulse (2=>100; 1=<100; 0=absent). G=Grimace (2=cry+pull; 1=grimace; 0=none). A=Activity (2=well flexed; 1=some flex; 0=flaccid). R=Respiration (2=strong cry; 1=irregular; 0=apnea).
Score Ranges and Response: 7–10 = Normal → Routine care. 4–6 = Moderate → O₂ + stimulation + warmth; reassess at 5 min. 0–3 = Critical → PPV immediately; if HR <60 after 30s PPV → chest compressions 3:1; if HR still <60 → epinephrine. Reassess every 5 min if score <7.
Timing and Trends: 1-minute APGAR assesses immediate adaptation. 5-minute APGAR is more predictive of outcome. Trend (improving or worsening) matters more than either single score. Acrocyanosis at 1 min = normal (score 1). Central cyanosis at any time = abnormal (score 0). Apnea and HR <60 always require immediate intervention.
