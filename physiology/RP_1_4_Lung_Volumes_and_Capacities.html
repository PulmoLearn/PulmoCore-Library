Lung Volumes and Capacities

This lesson introduces the basic lung volumes and capacities that describe how much air the lungs move, hold, and reserve. Students learn to read a simple spirogram, identify volume labels, calculate common capacities, and connect volume patterns to obstructive and restrictive disease.

Why Lung Volumes Matter

Lung volumes are a way of describing how much air is moving in and out of the lungs, how much extra air is available, and how much air remains after exhalation. These numbers help respiratory therapists understand breathing patterns, pulmonary function tests, and disease changes.

Clinical Bridge

When students later learn spirometry and pulmonary function testing, these same terms explain whether the patient has low lung volume, air trapping, reduced reserve, or difficulty moving air out.

Reading a Spirogram

A spirogram is a graph of lung volume over time. Small waves show normal quiet breathing. A large rise and fall show a forced maneuver where the person inhales fully and exhales as much as possible.

The Four Basic Lung Volumes

Start by learning the four single lung volumes. Think of them as individual pieces that can later be added together to form capacities.

VolumePlain ExplanationClinical Meaning
Tidal Volume (VT)A normal quiet breath.Baseline air movement during rest.
Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV)Extra air you can breathe in after a normal breath in.Shows extra inspiratory reserve.
Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV)Extra air you can push out after a normal breath out.Shows extra expiratory reserve.
Residual Volume (RV)Air left after you exhale as hard as possible.Prevents complete collapse; increases with air trapping.

Lung Capacities: Adding Volumes Together

A capacity is not a separate piece of air. A capacity is a combination of two or more lung volumes.

CapacityFormulaMeaning
ICIRV + VTMaximum air inhaled after a normal exhale.
FRCERV + RVAir remaining after a normal exhale.
VCIRV + VT + ERVMaximum air that can be moved in and out.
TLCVC + RVTotal air in lungs after maximum inhale.

Capacity Calculation Practice

Use these values: IRV = 3000 mL, VT = 500 mL, ERV = 1100 mL, RV = 1200 mL.

IC = IRV + VT
FRC = ERV + RV
VC = IRV + VT + ERV

Clinical Patterns: Obstructive vs Restrictive

Lung volume patterns help explain what is happening in disease. Obstructive diseases often trap air, increasing residual volume. Restrictive diseases limit lung expansion, reducing total lung capacity and vital capacity.

Normal
Lungs move air in and out with normal reserve volumes.
Obstructive
Air gets in but has trouble getting out; RV and FRC may increase.
Restrictive
Lungs cannot expand normally; TLC and VC may decrease.

FAQ

Common Student Questions

What is the difference between a volume and a capacity?

A volume is one piece of air. A capacity is a combination of two or more volumes.

Why can’t we exhale residual volume?

Residual volume remains after maximum exhalation. It helps keep small airways and alveoli from fully collapsing.

Why does air trapping increase residual volume?

If air cannot leave the lungs easily, more air remains after exhalation. This increases RV and may increase FRC.

Why is vital capacity important?

Vital capacity reflects the maximum amount of air the patient can move in and out. It is reduced when lung expansion is limited.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Lung volumes describe individual pieces of air. Lung capacities describe combinations of those pieces. These concepts help explain spirometry, pulmonary function testing, and disease patterns.

Must know:
VT is a normal quiet breath.
Formula pearl:
VC = IRV + VT + ERV.
Clinical pearl:
Obstruction can increase RV because air is trapped.
Restriction pearl:
Restriction often lowers TLC and VC.
