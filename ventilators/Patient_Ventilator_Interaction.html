Objectives

What You Will Be Able to Do

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain trigger, control/limit, cycle, and baseline variables in a ventilator breath.
  • Differentiate mandatory, assisted, and spontaneous breaths.
  • Recognize common trigger, flow, and cycling asynchronies from patient findings and waveform descriptions.
  • Identify the most likely cause of ineffective triggering, auto-triggering, flow starvation, double triggering, premature cycling, and delayed cycling.
  • Select a patient-centered ventilator adjustment and reassess its effect.
Board-exam mindset: Do not identify a waveform pattern in isolation. Link the pattern to the patient's effort, ventilator settings, disease process, and the safest next action.
Clinical Entry Case

The Patient Is “Fighting the Vent”

You are called to the ICU for a 54-year-old patient with severe pneumonia receiving volume assist-control ventilation. The nurse reports that the patient is restless and the ventilator is alarming frequently.

ModeAC-VC
VT / Rate420 mL / 18
PEEP / FiO₂8 / 0.50

The patient is awake, using accessory muscles, and appears to inhale forcefully before each breath. The pressure waveform shows a deep concave dip during inspiration. Two breaths occasionally occur back-to-back with little exhalation between them.

Pause before changing anything. “Fighting the ventilator” is not a diagnosis. It may reflect pain, anxiety, inadequate support, excessive support, auto-PEEP, an insensitive trigger, poor flow delivery, inappropriate cycling, or a new clinical problem.
Foundational Language

Every Breath Has Four Key Variables

To understand patient–ventilator interaction, first identify what starts the breath, what the ventilator controls during inspiration, what ends inspiration, and what pressure remains during exhalation.

Trigger

The event that starts inspiration. It may be time-triggered by the ventilator or patient-triggered by pressure or flow.

Control / Limit

The variable the ventilator targets or limits during inspiration, such as flow, pressure, or volume.

Cycle

The event that ends inspiration and allows exhalation to begin. Common cycling variables include time, volume, and flow.

Baseline

The pressure maintained during exhalation. In most ventilated patients, baseline is PEEP.

Breath Classification

Who Starts the Breath, and Who Ends It?

Breath type is determined by the relationship between trigger and cycle—not simply by the mode name.

Mandatory Breath

The ventilator starts and ends the breath. The patient does not initiate it.

Typical pattern: time-triggered and ventilator-cycled.

Assisted Breath

The patient triggers the breath, but the ventilator controls delivery and determines when inspiration ends.

Typical pattern: patient-triggered, volume- or time-cycled.

Spontaneous Breath

The patient starts and ends the breath. The ventilator may add pressure support, but the patient retains control over timing.

Typical pattern: patient-triggered and flow-cycled.

Triggering

Can the Patient Start the Breath Comfortably?

During a patient-triggered breath, the ventilator must detect the patient's inspiratory effort. The trigger should be sensitive enough to respond promptly—but not so sensitive that random circuit disturbances trigger breaths.

TIME
Time trigger

The ventilator initiates a breath after a preset interval based on the mandatory rate. This is appropriate when no patient effort is present or detected.

PRESSURE ↓
Pressure trigger

The ventilator senses a drop in circuit pressure. A more negative setting generally requires greater patient effort.

FLOW Δ
Flow trigger

The ventilator senses a change in continuous bias flow. Flow triggering often reduces patient effort and trigger delay.

When Triggering Goes Wrong

Ineffective triggering: The patient makes an inspiratory effort, but no ventilator breath follows. Common causes include auto-PEEP, an insensitive trigger, respiratory muscle weakness, excessive sedation, or excessive support.
Auto-triggering: The ventilator delivers breaths without true patient effort. Common causes include overly sensitive settings, circuit leaks, condensation, cardiac oscillations, or movement.

Flow and Cycling

Does the Ventilator Match the Patient's Inspiratory Demand?

Once a breath begins, synchrony depends on whether the ventilator supplies enough inspiratory flow and whether inspiration ends when the patient's neural inspiration ends.

Flow starvation

The patient wants more flow than the ventilator provides. Clues include accessory muscle use, a scooped or concave pressure waveform during inspiration, tachypnea, and discomfort.

Possible response: increase inspiratory flow, adjust rise time, or use a mode that better matches variable demand.

Excessive flow

Flow is delivered too rapidly, causing overshoot, discomfort, or a sharp pressure spike.

Possible response: reduce flow or slow rise time.

Premature cycling

The ventilator ends inspiration before the patient is ready. The patient may immediately trigger another breath, producing double triggering or breath stacking.

Possible response: lengthen inspiratory time or reduce the flow-cycle threshold in pressure support.

Delayed cycling

The ventilator continues inspiration after the patient wants to exhale. This is common in obstructive disease when inspiratory time is too long.

Possible response: shorten inspiratory time or increase the flow-cycle threshold.

Clinical Reasoning

Fix the Cause, Not the Alarm

When dyssynchrony appears, first make sure it is not caused by an urgent patient problem.

Assess the patient. Check oxygenation, hemodynamics, mental status, pain, anxiety, breath sounds, tube position, and work of breathing.
Inspect the airway and circuit. Look for obstruction, secretions, biting, water, leaks, disconnection, or a kinked tube.
Identify the interaction problem. Is the problem triggering, flow delivery, cycling, auto-PEEP, or excessive respiratory drive?
Change one factor first. Adjust the setting most directly linked to the pattern, then reassess.
Treat the underlying cause. Bronchospasm, fever, acidosis, pain, anxiety, pulmonary edema, and worsening disease can all increase respiratory demand.

Summary

Clinical Pearls

Start with the breath variables
Trigger starts inspiration, control/limit shapes it, cycle ends it, and baseline is the expiratory pressure.
Match the patient's demand
Synchrony depends on timely triggering, adequate flow, and cycling that matches neural inspiratory time.
Waveforms need context
Use the patient's effort, disease process, mode, and timing—not the waveform shape alone.
Fix one cause and reassess
Correct urgent patient or circuit problems first, then make the most targeted ventilator adjustment.
