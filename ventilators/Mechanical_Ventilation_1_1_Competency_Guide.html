Mechanical Ventilation · 1.1 Competency Guide

From Respiratory Failure to Ventilatory Support

This week focuses on recognizing respiratory failure, identifying what is failing, choosing the least invasive support that is both effective and safe, and recognizing when the patient needs escalation. Students should be able to explain the physiology behind the decision—not simply match a diagnosis to a device.

How to use this guide

Teach the two competencies as one clinical reasoning sequence: look at the patient → identify the failure → choose the support → reassess early → continue, modify, or escalate.

Board-prep connection: The 2027 Respiratory Therapy Examination emphasizes application, interpretation, Information Gathering, and Decision Making. The content below provides the prerequisite knowledge students need before they can make those decisions reliably.

Competency 1 · Analyze

Determine whether ventilatory support is needed.

Students integrate bedside findings, gas exchange, spontaneous ventilation, respiratory-muscle function, and airway protection to identify the type and severity of respiratory failure.

Competency 2 · Analyze / Decide

Select the safest support and recognize when escalation is required.

Students match conventional oxygen, heated high-flow nasal cannula, CPAP, bilevel NIV, or invasive ventilation to the patient's physiology, airway safety, and response.

Clinical question What is failing, how severe is it, and is the patient still able to sustain spontaneous breathing safely?

What you need to know

Failure type 1Oxygenation failureOxygen is not moving adequately from the lungs into arterial blood. PaO₂ and SpO₂ are central data.
Failure type 2Ventilation failureEffective alveolar ventilation is inadequate, so PaCO₂ rises and respiratory acidosis may develop.
Failure type 3Respiratory-pump failureThe drive, nerves, muscles, or chest wall cannot generate an effective breath even when the lungs are not the only problem.
Failure type 4Airway-protection failureThe patient cannot maintain a safe airway, swallow, cough, prevent aspiration, or manage secretions.
Core distinction: Oxygenation and ventilation are different jobs. A patient can have an acceptable SpO₂ while receiving oxygen and still have severe ventilatory failure with a rising PaCO₂.

Recognize deterioration before the patient crashes

EarlyWorking harderFaster breathing, accessory-muscle use, difficulty speaking, anxiety or restlessness, increasing oxygen need.
ProgressingTiringBreaths become shallower, cough weakens, air movement decreases, and the patient can no longer sustain the work of breathing.
FailureCompensation is lostMental status worsens, gas exchange deteriorates, spontaneous volume falls, or airway protection becomes unsafe.
Clinical warning: A quieter patient is not always an improving patient. A respiratory rate that falls after prolonged distress can represent fatigue if breathing becomes shallow or mental status worsens.

Choose the data that answer the clinical question

Bedside assessmentMental status, respiratory rate and depth, work of breathing, chest movement, breath sounds, cough, secretions, ability to speak, and trend over time.
Oxygenation dataSpO₂, PaO₂, FiO₂, increasing oxygen requirement, PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio, and the A-a gradient when oxygen response is unexpectedly poor.
Ventilation dataPaCO₂, pH, spontaneous tidal volume, respiratory rate, work of breathing, fatigue, and mental status.
Respiratory muscle dataVital capacity (VC), maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) or negative inspiratory force (NIF), maximal expiratory pressure (MEP), cough strength, and serial trends.
Airway safetySwallowing, bulbar function, vomiting, aspiration risk, cough, secretion burden, ability to cooperate, and ability to maintain airway patency.
Trend over a cutoffSerial deterioration is often more important than one isolated number. Do not let one value become an automatic intubation rule.

Respiratory muscle weakness requires different thinking

Vital capacity and inspiratory strength

  • VC is the largest volume exhaled after a maximal inspiration and can be followed serially.
  • MIP/NIF reflects inspiratory muscle strength.
  • A negative inspiratory pressure that moves closer to zero represents weaker inspiratory force.
  • A falling VC plus weaker MIP/NIF is more concerning than either value viewed once in isolation.

Cough and airway protection

  • MEP helps estimate expiratory muscle strength and contributes to cough effectiveness.
  • Bulbar weakness can impair swallowing and secretion handling before the ABG becomes dramatic.
  • Normal SpO₂ does not rule out neuromuscular ventilatory failure.
  • Do not wait for severe hypercapnia when the respiratory pump or airway is clearly deteriorating.

When oxygen does not respond as expected

PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio

The P/F ratio compares arterial oxygen pressure with the inspired oxygen fraction.

P/F ratio = PaO₂ ÷ FiO₂

A low value indicates poor oxygenation despite a substantial oxygen concentration.

A-a gradient

The alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient compares estimated alveolar oxygen with measured arterial oxygen.

A-a gradient = PAO₂ − PaO₂

A widened gradient indicates impaired oxygen transfer but does not by itself distinguish V/Q mismatch, diffusion limitation, or shunt.

Alveolar gas equation refresher

PAO₂ = FiO₂ × (PB − PH₂O) − (PaCO₂ ÷ R)

At sea level, PB is about 760 mm Hg, PH₂O is 47 mm Hg, and R is commonly estimated as 0.8.

PAO₂ = FiO₂ × (760 − 47) − (PaCO₂ ÷ 0.8)

The goal in this lesson is to recognize when the A-a gradient helps explain an unexpectedly poor PaO₂ response. Deeper oxygenation optimization comes later.

Why this matters clinically

Mechanical ventilation decisions can be delayed when clinicians focus on a reassuring SpO₂, one blood-gas value, or one muscle-strength cutoff. C1 requires students to integrate the whole patient: physiology, work of breathing, trend, mental status, respiratory muscle function, and airway safety.

Common traps to avoid
  • Assuming a normal SpO₂ means ventilation is adequate.
  • Assuming a lower respiratory rate always means improvement.
  • Waiting for a severely abnormal ABG before recognizing neuromuscular deterioration.
  • Using one VC, MIP/NIF, pH, or PaCO₂ value as an automatic intubation threshold.
  • Forgetting that inability to protect the airway may require invasive support even when gas exchange is not yet extreme.

Apply it

Neuromuscular weakness

A patient with myasthenia gravis has SpO₂ 96% but progressively weaker cough, dysphagia, a falling VC, and MIP becoming less negative. The correct interpretation is worsening respiratory-pump and airway risk—not reassurance from the oxygen saturation.

Masked ventilatory failure

A patient receiving oxygen has SpO₂ 97%, shallow respirations, PaCO₂ 76 mm Hg, and increasing somnolence. The oxygen saturation does not rule out severe ventilatory failure. The therapist should focus on ventilation and airway safety.

Clinical question Which support best matches the patient's physiology now, and what findings would make the current strategy no longer safe or effective?

What you need to know

Do not teach this as a mandatory ladder. Patients do not need to “fail” every less-invasive device before receiving more advanced support. Choose the least invasive option that can safely meet the patient's current need.
Oxygenation
Conventional oxygen

Raises FiO₂ when ventilation remains adequate. Does not provide meaningful inspiratory assistance.

Oxygenation
Heated high-flow nasal cannula

Provides warmed, humidified high flow, stable FiO₂, dead-space washout, some work-of-breathing reduction, and a small positive-pressure effect.

Recruitment
CPAP

Provides one continuous positive pressure during spontaneous breathing. Useful when recruitment and oxygenation are the main needs.

Ventilatory assistance
Bilevel NIV

Uses a higher inspiratory pressure and lower expiratory pressure, adding inspiratory assistance while preserving a noninvasive interface.

Airway + full support
Invasive ventilation

Uses an artificial airway when noninvasive support is unsafe, inadequate, or failing, or when airway protection is lost.

Clinical reasoning rule: The diagnosis provides context. The support choice comes from physiology, severity, airway safety, respiratory drive, secretion handling, and response.

CPAP and bilevel NIV are not the same therapy

Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP)

CPAP maintains one positive pressure while the patient breathes spontaneously.

Inspiration
Same CPAP pressure
Expiration
Same CPAP pressure

Main strength: alveolar recruitment and oxygenation. CPAP does not provide a separate inspiratory pressure boost.

Bilevel Noninvasive Ventilation

Bilevel NIV uses two pressure levels.

Inspiration
IPAP · higher pressure
Expiration
EPAP · lower pressure
Pressure support = IPAP − EPAP

Main strength: expiratory pressure can support recruitment while the IPAP-to-EPAP difference provides inspiratory assistance.

Understand what changes when the pressures change

Increase IPAP only

The pressure-support difference increases. Inspiratory assistance usually increases, tidal volume often rises, and PaCO₂ may fall if ventilation improves.

Increase EPAP only

Baseline expiratory pressure rises and recruitment may improve, but the pressure-support difference becomes smaller if IPAP is unchanged.

Increase both equally

The baseline pressure rises while the pressure-support difference stays the same.

Pressure is targeted, volume is not guaranteed

Tidal volume also depends on patient effort, resistance, compliance, inspiratory time, and leak.

Use breath-delivery language to understand the mode

TriggerThe event that starts inspiration. A breath can be patient-triggered or machine/time-triggered.
LimitThe variable controlled during inspiration without being the event that ends inspiration. Bilevel support is pressure-targeted/limited.
CycleThe event that ends inspiration. Spontaneous bilevel breaths are commonly flow-cycled; timed backup breaths are time-cycled.
BaselineThe variable controlled during expiration. In bilevel NIV, EPAP is the pressure baseline.

Common spontaneous bilevel breath

Patient-triggered → pressure-limited → flow-cycled → EPAP baseline.

The patient's inspiratory effort is detected through a small pressure or flow change. More detailed trigger sensitivity and cycling behavior will be developed later.

Timed backup bilevel breath

Machine/time-triggered → pressure-limited → time-cycled → EPAP baseline.

The machine initiates a breath when the backup interval is reached and ends inspiration after the set or device-defined inspiratory time.

Choose the NIV mode by asking what the patient can do

Spontaneous mode

The patient reliably initiates supported breaths. The device responds to the patient's trigger and provides pressure support.

Spontaneous/Timed (S/T)

The patient can trigger supported breaths, but a backup rate provides timed breaths if spontaneous triggering becomes too slow or absent.

Timed / controlled patterns

Mode names vary by manufacturer. Focus first on who triggers the breath, what pressure is delivered, what ends inspiration, and whether a backup breath is guaranteed.

Important limitation: A backup rate does not protect an unsafe airway. Vomiting, severe obtundation, poor secretion handling, or inability to protect the airway can make NIV inappropriate even when timed breaths are available.

The interface is part of the therapy

Oronasal / full-faceCovers the nose and mouth and is often useful in acute care or with significant mouth breathing.
Nasal interfaceCovers or seals at the nose. Comfort may be better for some patients, but mouth leak can reduce effectiveness.
Fit and tolerance matterConsider seal, facial anatomy, skin pressure, anxiety or claustrophobia, secretions, mouth breathing, and airway-protection ability.

Recognize whether NIV is reasonable, working, or failing

Reasonable NIV candidate

  • Breathing spontaneously
  • Able to protect the airway
  • Able to cooperate with the interface
  • Able to manage secretions
  • Stable enough for a closely monitored trial
  • Can be reassessed rapidly with invasive support available if needed

NIV failure / escalation warnings

  • Work of breathing continues to worsen
  • Gas exchange or acidosis worsens
  • Mental status declines
  • Airway protection or secretion handling deteriorates
  • Hemodynamic instability develops
  • The patient cannot tolerate the interface
  • There is no meaningful improvement despite support

Disease examples: follow the physiology as it changes

COPD exacerbation

Controlled oxygen → bilevel NIV if acute hypercapnic respiratory acidosis develops → reassess → invasive ventilation if NIV fails or airway safety deteriorates

An elevated PaCO₂ alone does not prove acute ventilatory failure. Look for acute acidosis, increased work of breathing, mental status, and trend.

Cardiogenic pulmonary edema

Supplemental oxygen → CPAP for recruitment/oxygenation → bilevel if ventilatory assistance is also needed → invasive ventilation if noninvasive support becomes unsafe or fails

Monitor blood pressure and perfusion because positive pressure can affect hemodynamics.

Neuromuscular weakness

Trend VC/MIP/cough/airway safety → bilevel NIV while airway protection is intact → S/T if triggering becomes unreliable → invasive airway if secretion handling or airway protection fails

Do not wait for severe hypercapnia when bulbar function, cough, or respiratory mechanics are clearly deteriorating.

Why this matters clinically

C2 is a Decision Making competency. The safest answer is not always the least invasive device, the device traditionally associated with the diagnosis, or the device the patient was already using. Students must continuously ask whether the current support still matches the patient's physiology and whether the airway remains safe.

Common traps to avoid
  • Teaching support as oxygen → high-flow → CPAP → bilevel → intubation for every patient.
  • Using “pulmonary edema = CPAP” or “COPD = bilevel” as rigid disease-mode matching rules.
  • Assuming CPAP and bilevel provide the same type of assistance.
  • Forgetting that raising EPAP without raising IPAP decreases the pressure-support difference.
  • Assuming pressure-targeted bilevel guarantees a fixed tidal volume.
  • Believing a backup rate makes NIV safe for a patient who cannot protect the airway.
  • Waiting too long to escalate when the patient is clearly failing the current strategy.

Apply it

COPD with acute respiratory acidosis

An awake patient with marked work of breathing, pH 7.28, and PaCO₂ 68 mm Hg is a strong bilevel NIV candidate if airway protection and secretion handling are intact. If pH improves, PaCO₂ trends down, and work of breathing decreases, continue and reassess rather than intubating because PaCO₂ remains above normal.

Pulmonary edema that changes physiology

A patient may begin with severe hypoxemia and adequate ventilation, making CPAP reasonable. If the same patient later becomes hypercapnic and tired, bilevel NIV may offer an advantage because the problem now includes ventilatory failure.

Neuromuscular weakness and airway safety

A patient may initially benefit from bilevel NIV and later need an S/T backup rate as triggering becomes unreliable. If bulbar weakness progresses and the patient can no longer clear secretions or protect the airway, increasing the backup rate does not solve the safety problem; invasive airway management should be considered.

Before the weekly quiz and competency exam

Students should be able to explain and apply the relationships below—not just recognize the vocabulary.

  • C1: Distinguish oxygenation, ventilation, respiratory-pump, and airway-protection failure from patient data and clinical trends.
  • C1: Select the most useful assessment data for the clinical question, including SpO₂, PaO₂, PaCO₂, pH, VC, MIP/NIF, cough, airway safety, P/F ratio, and A-a gradient when appropriate.
  • C1: Explain why normal SpO₂ or one reassuring blood-gas value does not rule out respiratory failure.
  • C2: Choose conventional oxygen, high-flow support, CPAP, bilevel NIV, or invasive ventilation based on physiology and airway safety.
  • C2: Explain IPAP, EPAP, pressure support, trigger, limit, cycle, baseline, spontaneous mode, and Spontaneous/Timed mode well enough to predict what the device is doing.
  • C2: Recognize when the patient's response supports continuing the current strategy and when worsening work of breathing, gas exchange, mental status, secretions, airway safety, or hemodynamics should prompt escalation.
  • C2: Apply the support progression appropriately in COPD, cardiogenic pulmonary edema, and neuromuscular weakness without treating those progressions as mandatory ladders.
