Objectives

What You Will Be Able to Do

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Prioritize the patient before the ventilator when an alarm sounds.
  • Differentiate high-pressure, low-pressure, low-volume, apnea, and oxygen-supply alarms.
  • Use the DOPE framework to organize sudden deterioration.
  • Recognize when to disconnect and manually ventilate.
  • Use pressure trends, auscultation, circuit inspection, and manual ventilation findings to locate the problem.
  • Select the safest first intervention and reassess the response.
Clinical Entry Case

The Alarm Is Not the Diagnosis

You are covering the ICU when a high-priority ventilator alarm sounds. Your patient, a 66-year-old man with pneumonia, was stable ten minutes ago. The monitor now shows SpO₂ 84%, heart rate 128/min, and blood pressure 92/58 mmHg. The ventilator displays a high-pressure alarm.

The alarm tells you that the ventilator encountered a limit. It does not tell you whether the cause is secretions, bronchospasm, coughing, biting, a kinked tube, water in the circuit, pneumothorax, worsening compliance, or something else.

Core principle: An alarm is a clue. The patient assessment determines urgency and the next action.

Decision Checkpoint: What do you do first?

First Priority

Patient First, Then the System

When a ventilator alarm sounds, divide your thinking into two questions:

1. Is the patient unstable?Look for severe distress, cyanosis, altered mental status, hypotension, absent chest rise, or rapidly falling SpO₂.
2. Is ventilation occurring?Observe chest movement, exhaled volume, breath sounds, capnography, and the patient’s color and effort.
3. Is the airway/circuit intact?Check the tube, cuff, connections, humidifier, filters, water traps, and tubing.
4. What changed?Compare current pressures, volumes, waveforms, breath sounds, and hemodynamics with the prior trend.

A stable patient with a brief cough may only require observation and reassessment. An unstable patient with absent chest rise requires immediate support before a detailed search for the cause.

High-Pressure Alarm

Separate Resistance from Compliance

High pressure means the ventilator encountered increased opposition to gas flow or lung inflation. The fastest clinical distinction is to compare peak inspiratory pressure (PIP) with plateau pressure (Pplat).

  • PIP rises while Pplat stays stable: suspect increased airway resistance—secretions, bronchospasm, tube obstruction, biting, kinked tubing, or water.
  • PIP and Pplat both rise: suspect reduced compliance—ARDS, pulmonary edema, atelectasis, pneumothorax, abdominal distention, or severe patient-ventilator asynchrony.

Never use pressure numbers alone. Confirm with breath sounds, chest movement, suction catheter passage, waveforms, hemodynamics, and manual ventilation resistance.

Baseline: PIP 24, Pplat 19, PEEP 5. Current: PIP 39, Pplat 20, PEEP 5. Coarse rhonchi are present, and the patient coughed just before the alarm.

Low-Pressure and Low-Volume Alarms

Think Leak or Disconnection First

When the ventilator cannot generate expected pressure or the exhaled volume suddenly falls, rapidly inspect every connection from the ventilator to the patient. Check the endotracheal tube depth, cuff pressure, pilot balloon, tubing, humidifier, filters, and water traps.

A low-pressure alarm can be deceptively quiet. Unlike a high-pressure alarm, the ventilator may be delivering gas into the room instead of into the patient.

Guided Case: Low pressure and low exhaled volume

The patient is awake but suddenly distressed. PIP falls from 22 to 8 cmH₂O, exhaled VT falls from 450 to 90 mL, and you hear a loud air leak near the mouth.

Sudden Deterioration

Use DOPE Without Becoming Mechanical

DOPE is a rapid framework for an intubated patient who suddenly deteriorates:

D — Displacement
Tube migrated into the right mainstem, became partially extubated, or was removed.
O — Obstruction
Secretions, blood, biting, kinked tube, mucus plug, or blocked filter.
P — Pneumothorax
Sudden unilateral breath-sound loss, hypotension, rising pressure, and possible tracheal shift.
E — Equipment failure
Disconnection, gas failure, malfunctioning ventilator, blocked circuit, or faulty valve.

DOPE helps organize possibilities, but it does not replace immediate assessment. For example, unilateral absent breath sounds may represent right mainstem intubation or pneumothorax; tube depth, chest movement, percussion, hemodynamics, and pressure trends help distinguish them.

Manual Ventilation

When the Bag Becomes a Diagnostic Tool

If the patient is unstable, ventilation appears ineffective, or the ventilator may be malfunctioning, disconnect the circuit and manually ventilate with a resuscitation bag and appropriate oxygen source.

Manual ventilation does two things at once:

  1. It immediately supports the patient.
  2. It helps localize the problem.
  • Easy bagging and rapid improvement: suspect a ventilator or circuit problem.
  • Difficult bagging: suspect airway obstruction, bronchospasm, severe compliance loss, auto-PEEP, or pneumothorax.
  • No chest rise despite bagging: think dislodged tube, complete obstruction, severe leak, or major thoracic emergency.

Interpret the finding

The patient improves immediately after disconnection from the ventilator. Manual breaths are easy to deliver, chest rise is symmetric, and SpO₂ rises from 82% to 94%.

Summary

Clinical Pearls

Assess before adjusting
The alarm identifies a threshold, not the cause.
Use PIP and Pplat together
They help separate resistance from compliance problems.
Use DOPE for sudden decline
Displacement, obstruction, pneumothorax, and equipment failure organize the search.
Bag when unstable
Manual ventilation supports the patient and helps localize the problem.
