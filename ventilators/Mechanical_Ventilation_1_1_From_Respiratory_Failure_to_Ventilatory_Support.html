Start With the Patient, Not the Machine

Before you choose a device or ventilator, answer three questions:

1. What is failing?
Oxygenation, ventilation, respiratory muscle function, airway protection, or more than one?
2. How sick is the patient?
Stable, working harder, tiring, or immediately unsafe?
3. What support fits?
Choose support that addresses the problem and is safe for this patient.
4. Is it working?
Reassess early. The patient's response decides what happens next.
How to use this lesson: You will first learn the pieces, then use them together. The separate practice activity will give you more repetition before you are expected to manage complex cases independently.
2Core concept

Oxygenation and Ventilation Are Different Jobs

Oxygenation is the movement of oxygen from the lungs into the blood. Ventilation is the movement of air into and out of the lungs so carbon dioxide can be removed.

A patient can have an oxygenation problem, a ventilation problem, or both.

Oxygenation

Getting oxygen into arterial blood
Useful data
SpO₂, PaO₂, FiO₂
Peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) estimates hemoglobin saturation. Partial pressure of arterial oxygen (PaO₂) measures dissolved oxygen in arterial blood. Fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) is the fraction of oxygen in the gas the patient breathes.
Common pattern
PaO₂ remains low even though the patient is moving air.
Think about
Pneumonia, pulmonary edema, acute respiratory distress syndrome, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, diffusion problems, or shunt.

Ventilation

Moving air and removing carbon dioxide
Useful data
PaCO₂, pH, tidal volume, effort
Partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO₂) helps show how well carbon dioxide is being removed.
Common pattern
Effective ventilation falls, PaCO₂ rises, and respiratory acidosis may develop.
Think about
Fatigue, severe airflow obstruction, reduced respiratory drive, or respiratory muscle weakness.

4Identify the failure

What Is Actually Failing?

The same symptom—“I can't breathe”—can come from very different problems. Support works best when it matches the physiology.

O₂

Oxygenation failure

Enough oxygen is not moving into arterial blood. Think pneumonia, pulmonary edema, acute respiratory distress syndrome, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, diffusion limitation, or shunt.

CO₂

Ventilation failure

The patient is not removing enough carbon dioxide. Think low respiratory drive, severe obstruction, fatigue, or inadequate respiratory muscle force.

M

Respiratory-pump failure

The brain, nerves, muscles, or chest wall cannot produce an effective breath. Neuromuscular disease is an important example.

A

Airway-protection failure

The patient cannot keep the airway safe, manage secretions, swallow safely, or protect against aspiration. Blood gases may not be dramatic yet.

Airway protection is its own problem. A patient can have a fairly acceptable PaO₂ and PaCO₂ and still need an invasive airway because they cannot protect against aspiration or clear secretions.

6Oxygen transfer

When More Oxygen Does Not Produce the Expected Response

If PaO₂ stays unexpectedly low while FiO₂ rises, ask whether oxygen is having trouble moving from the alveoli into arterial blood.

Alveolar–arterial oxygen gradient

A–a gradient
What it compares
Estimated oxygen pressure in the alveoli with measured PaO₂ in arterial blood.
What a wide gradient means
Oxygen transfer from alveoli to arterial blood is impaired.
What it does not prove
It does not by itself tell you whether the cause is ventilation-perfusion mismatch, diffusion limitation, or shunt.

PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio

P/F ratio
What it compares
PaO₂ with the amount of oxygen being delivered as FiO₂.
Quick example
PaO₂ 60 ÷ FiO₂ 0.60 = P/F ratio 100
Clinical meaning
Oxygenation is poor despite a substantial inspired oxygen level.
How we will use this later: Unit 1 uses these values to recognize the size of an oxygenation problem. Unit 2 will use them more deeply during oxygenation optimization and acute respiratory distress syndrome management.
Need an A–a gradient refresher?

First estimate alveolar oxygen pressure (PAO₂) with the alveolar gas equation:

PAO₂ = FiO₂ × (PB − PH₂O) − (PaCO₂ ÷ R)

At sea level: barometric pressure (PB) is about 760 mm Hg, water-vapor pressure (PH₂O) is 47 mm Hg, and the respiratory quotient (R) is usually estimated as 0.8.

PAO₂ = FiO₂ × (760 − 47) − (PaCO₂ ÷ 0.8)

Then subtract the measured arterial oxygen pressure:

A–a gradient = PAO₂ − PaO₂

You do not need to calculate this for every hypoxemic patient. In this lesson, focus on recognizing when the A–a gradient can help explain why PaO₂ is lower than expected for the amount of oxygen being delivered.

7Match support to physiology

There Is No Required “Support Ladder”

Patients do not have to move through every device in order. Choose the support that meets the problem now and can be used safely.

In this lesson, high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) means heated, humidified high-flow oxygen delivered through nasal prongs. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) provides one continuous pressure level. Noninvasive ventilation (NIV) provides ventilatory support without an endotracheal tube or tracheostomy.

8Go deeper with noninvasive support

CPAP and Bilevel NIV: Choose the Pressure Pattern That Matches the Problem

Knowing that a patient needs noninvasive support is only the first decision. Next, ask what the patient needs the pressure to do.

One pressure level

Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP)

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) maintains one positive pressure while the patient breathes spontaneously.

Inspiration
Same CPAP level
Expiration
Same CPAP level
Think: recruitment and oxygenation. CPAP does not add a separate inspiratory pressure boost.
Two pressure levels

Bilevel Noninvasive Ventilation

Bilevel noninvasive ventilation (NIV) uses a higher pressure during inspiration and a lower pressure during expiration.

Inspiration
IPAP · higher pressure
Expiration
EPAP · lower pressure
Pressure support = IPAP − EPAP
Think: oxygenation support plus inspiratory assistance when the pressure difference is large enough to help the patient take a bigger breath.
Terminology: You may hear clinicians say “BiPAP.” BiPAP is a familiar product name. In this course, we will usually say bilevel NIV and then identify the actual mode or breath pattern being used.

What Changes What?

Do not memorize IPAP and EPAP as isolated numbers. Think about what changes when one pressure moves and the other stays the same.

Increase IPAP only
Inspiratory assistance usually increases because the pressure-support difference becomes larger.
IPAP ↑ → pressure support ↑ → tidal volume often ↑ → PaCO₂ may ↓
Increase EPAP only
Baseline expiratory pressure rises and recruitment may improve, but the pressure-support difference becomes smaller if IPAP does not change.
EPAP ↑ → recruitment may ↑ + pressure support ↓
Increase IPAP and EPAP equally
The baseline pressure rises while the pressure-support difference stays the same.
IPAP ↑ + EPAP ↑ equally → pressure support unchanged
Clinical check: These relationships predict the direction of change. The actual patient response still depends on effort, lung mechanics, leaks, and disease.

Four Words That Describe What Happens During a Breath

Before we compare bilevel modes, we need four breath-delivery terms. You will use these words throughout mechanical ventilation.

1

Trigger

Trigger is what starts inspiration.

A breath can be patient-triggered or machine-triggered.

2

Limit

Limit is the variable held or restricted during inspiration without being the event that ends inspiration.

In bilevel NIV, inspiratory pressure is the main controlled/limited variable.

3

Cycle

Cycle is what ends inspiration and allows expiration to begin.

Different breaths may cycle by flow or by time.

4

Baseline

Baseline is the variable controlled during expiration.

For bilevel NIV, the pressure baseline is the expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP).

During inspiration Limit What variable is controlled while inspiration is occurring?
End inspiration Cycle What tells the device to stop inspiration?

Follow one spontaneous bilevel breath

Patient-Triggered, Pressure-Limited, Flow-Cycled

A common spontaneous bilevel breath can be described by what happens at each phase. Device behavior varies, but this gives you the framework.

TriggerThe patient makes an inspiratory effort. The device senses a small change in pressure or flow and begins support.
LimitPressure rises to the set inspiratory positive airway pressure (IPAP). The device controls pressure rather than guaranteeing a set tidal volume.
CycleAs inspiratory flow falls to the device's cycling threshold, inspiration ends and the device returns to EPAP.
BaselineEPAP is maintained during expiration until the next breath begins.
Follow one timed backup breath

Machine-Triggered, Pressure-Limited, Time-Cycled

If the patient does not trigger often enough in a mode with a backup rate, the machine can deliver a timed breath.

TriggerTime starts the breath because the set backup interval has been reached.
LimitThe device raises pressure to the set IPAP or inspiratory pressure target.
CycleThe breath ends after the set inspiratory time or device-defined timed interval.
BaselineThe device returns to EPAP during expiration.
Important: pressure is set, volume is not guaranteed.

With pressure-targeted bilevel support, the tidal volume the patient receives can change even when IPAP and EPAP stay the same. Tidal volume depends on the pressure difference, the patient's effort, airway resistance, lung/chest-wall compliance, inspiratory time, and leak.

Resistance describes how difficult it is for gas to flow through the airways. Compliance describes how easily the respiratory system expands. We will break these relationships down in detail in Lesson 1.2.

The Interface Is Part of the Therapy

A good pressure strategy can still fail if the patient cannot tolerate the interface or if the seal is poor.

Oronasal / full-face

Covers the nose and mouth. Often useful when a patient is mouth breathing or needs acute support.

Nasal interface

Covers or seals at the nose. Comfort can be better for some patients, but mouth leak can reduce effectiveness.

Choose for the patient

Consider seal, facial anatomy, skin pressure, comfort, anxiety, mouth breathing, secretions, and airway-protection ability.

A backup rate does not make NIV safe when the airway is unsafe. If the patient cannot protect the airway, is vomiting, or cannot manage secretions, adding timed breaths does not solve the main problem.

9Safety and escalation

Noninvasive Support Is Only Useful While It Is Safe and Working

A trial of noninvasive support is not the endpoint. The next decision is whether the patient is improving enough to continue it.

Noninvasive support may be reasonable when the patient…

  • is breathing spontaneously,
  • can protect the airway,
  • can cooperate with the interface,
  • can manage secretions,
  • is stable enough for a trial, and
  • can be reassessed closely.

Escalation becomes more urgent when the patient…

  • has worsening work of breathing,
  • has worsening gas exchange or acidosis,
  • is losing airway protection,
  • cannot manage secretions,
  • develops hemodynamic instability,
  • cannot tolerate the interface, or
  • fails to improve with an appropriate trial.
Do not memorize “altered mental status = automatic intubation.” Ask why mental status is abnormal and whether the patient can still protect the airway and safely tolerate the interface. Hypercapnic somnolence may improve with effective ventilation; progressive loss of airway protection is a major warning sign.

What should be automatic now?

Low oxygen and low ventilation are not the same problem.
A reassuring SpO₂ does not rule out hypercapnia.
A lower respiratory rate can represent fatigue.
One blood-gas value does not decide intubation.
Airway protection matters as much as gas exchange.
Starting support is followed by reassessment—not autopilot.
