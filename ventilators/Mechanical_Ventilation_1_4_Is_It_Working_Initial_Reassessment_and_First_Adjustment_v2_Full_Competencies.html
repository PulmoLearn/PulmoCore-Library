1 · Reassess before changing anything

The First Question Is Not “Which Knob?”

The first question is: What problem is still present after support was started?

Patient
Chest rise, work of breathing, mental status, comfort, color, perfusion, blood pressure.
Airway + circuit
Tube position, patency, secretions, circuit connection, humidification, oxygen source.
Ventilator
VTE, V̇E, total rate, PIP, Pplat when appropriate, PEEP, alarms, waveforms, expiratory flow.
Objective response
SpO₂, ABG, hemodynamics, trend from baseline, and whether the original goal is being met.
Look at the patient → identify the failure → match the change → reassess early.
Do not “treat the ABG” before checking the patient and equipment. A disconnected circuit, obstructed tube, bronchospasm, secretion burden, or hemodynamic problem can make a blood gas abnormal without the ventilator prescription being the primary cause.

2 · Use the first ABG to test the initial plan

The First ABG Answers Two Different Questions

Ventilation question
Is PaCO₂/pH acceptable for this patient and disease process?
Oxygenation question
Is PaO₂/SpO₂ acceptable on the current FiO₂ and PEEP?

After initial settings are established and the patient has had time to reach a new steady state, obtain/review an ABG according to clinical need and local practice. In this course, think of the first ABG as an early reassessment rather than a reason to change several settings at once.

Unit 1 goal: make the first essential change. Do not solve every abnormality simultaneously unless immediate instability requires it.
FindingFirst-decision meaning
Acidemia + high PaCO₂Ventilation is inadequate for the current goal. Decide whether rate or VT should change.
Alkalemia + low PaCO₂Ventilation may be excessive. Consider reducing minute ventilation.
Low PaO₂ / low SpO₂Oxygenation support is inadequate. Decide whether FiO₂, PEEP, recruitment, or another cause is the priority.
Acceptable gas exchange but unsafe pressure/graphicsDo not ignore mechanics. Pressure, auto-PEEP, or dyssynchrony may require a change even when the ABG looks acceptable.
3 · Correct ventilation with a proportional calculation

PaCO₂ Moves Inversely With Alveolar Ventilation

When CO₂ production is reasonably stable, use the inverse relationship to estimate the new target minute ventilation.

Desired V̇E = Current V̇E × (Current PaCO₂ ÷ Desired PaCO₂)
If VT stays the same
New f = Desired V̇E ÷ VT
If rate must stay limited
New VT = Desired V̇E ÷ f
Always recheck safety
PBW-based VT, disease-specific rate, expiratory time, pressure, and patient response constrain the answer.
The calculation gives a target, not permission to use an unsafe setting. If the calculated rate is inappropriate for severe COPD/asthma, or the calculated VT is too large for ARDS, choose the safer variable and accept a different PaCO₂ goal if necessary.

5 · Correct oxygenation without changing everything

FiO₂ and PEEP Solve Different Parts of the Oxygenation Problem

FiO₂
Raises oxygen concentration delivered to the alveoli. Often the simplest first move for modest hypoxemia.
PEEP
Raises end-expiratory pressure and supports recruitment. More important when hypoxemia reflects collapse, edema, or shunt-like physiology.
Do not chase SpO₂ with FiO₂ alone forever. If significant hypoxemia persists despite substantial FiO₂, reassess recruitment, PEEP, hemodynamics, secretions, positioning, pneumothorax, edema, and disease severity.
SituationFirst-adjustment thinking
Mild low SpO₂ on low FiO₂A modest FiO₂ increase may be simplest.
ARDS / edema with significant hypoxemiaFiO₂ and PEEP should be considered together; recruitment support matters.
COPD with acceptable target saturationDo not increase FiO₂ just because PaO₂ is below a healthy adult normal.
Post-ROSC after reliable oxygen data appearTitrate from FiO₂ 1.0 rather than leaving 100% indefinitely.

6 · Use pressure patterns before changing VT

High PIP Does Not Automatically Mean “Lower the Tidal Volume”

High PIP + normal/unchanged Pplat
Think resistance: secretions, kinked tube, biting, bronchospasm, high flow, obstruction.
High PIP + high Pplat
Think compliance/distending pressure: ARDS, edema, atelectasis, pneumothorax, abdominal pressure, excessive VT.
This is why severe asthma can have a high PIP without an automatic VT reduction. If Pplat is acceptable, much of the extra pressure may be resistive.

Final framework

The First Adjustment Algorithm

1 · Verify
Patient, airway, circuit, monitored parameters.
2 · Name the problem
Ventilation, oxygenation, resistance, compliance, timing, or support failure.
3 · Make one change
Choose the variable that best addresses that problem without violating disease-specific safety.
4 · Reassess
Patient, SpO₂, pressures, volumes, graphics, hemodynamics, and ABG as appropriate.
One change → one expected effect → one reassessment.
