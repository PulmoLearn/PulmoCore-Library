1Clinical orientation

A Ventilator Breath Is a Sequence of Decisions

You do not need to memorize every manufacturer's mode name. Start by asking the same questions about every breath.

1 · Start

What triggers inspiration?

Did the patient initiate the breath, or did the ventilator start it because time reached the next scheduled breath?

2 · During inspiration

What is being controlled?

Is the ventilator targeting volume/flow or pressure? Which variable is allowed to change when mechanics change?

3 · End inspiration

What cycles the breath?

Does inspiration end because time is reached, inspiratory flow falls, or another cycling rule is met?

4 · Expiration

What is the baseline?

Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) is the pressure maintained at the end of expiration before the next breath.

Important: A patient-triggered breath is not automatically a “spontaneous breath.” In assist-control ventilation, a patient can trigger the breath and still receive the full preset mandatory breath target.
2Mechanics model

Think of Pressure as the Cost of Moving a Breath

The ventilator and the patient's respiratory muscles create pressure. That pressure must overcome two major loads: elastic load and resistive load, plus the baseline pressure already present in the system.

Pressure available Patient muscle pressure (Pmus)
Pressure generated by respiratory muscle effort. Ventilator pressure (Pvent)
Positive pressure supplied by the ventilator.
=
Pressure required Elastic pressure
Pressure needed to stretch the lungs and chest wall. Resistive pressure
Pressure needed to move gas through the airways and artificial airway. PEEP
Baseline pressure already present at end-expiration.
Pmus + Pvent ≈ elastic pressure + resistive pressure + PEEP
Plain-language version: Stiffer lungs increase the pressure cost of stretching. Narrower or obstructed airways increase the pressure cost of moving flow.
3Control variable

Volume Control and Pressure Control Protect Different Things

The control variable tells you which major inspiratory variable the ventilator is trying to keep at the selected target. The other variable becomes dependent on the patient's mechanics.

Explore all four

What Changes When Mechanics Change?

Explore the control variable and its consequence. You will use this relationship throughout the course.

0 of 4 explored
Explore all four relationships to continue.
Volume-controlled breath

Volume target stays consistent; pressure varies.

The ventilator delivers the selected volume target using the programmed flow pattern. If the respiratory system becomes harder to inflate, the pressure required to deliver that volume rises.

Pressure-controlled breath

Pressure target stays consistent; volume varies.

The ventilator applies the selected inspiratory pressure for the selected inspiratory time. If compliance or resistance worsens, delivered tidal volume may fall.

Do not translate this into “pressure control is safe and volume control is dangerous.” Either strategy can be used safely when settings, pressures, tidal volume, patient response, and lung-protective goals are monitored appropriately.
4Pressure landmarks

PIP, Plateau Pressure, and PEEP Tell Different Parts of the Story

Peak

Peak inspiratory pressure (PIP)

The highest airway pressure reached during inspiration. During a flow-delivered breath, PIP reflects pressure needed to overcome both resistance and elastic load.

Hold

Plateau pressure (Pplat)

Pressure measured during an inspiratory hold when flow is briefly stopped. In a passive patient without active effort, it approximates alveolar/static respiratory-system pressure.

Baseline

Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP)

Pressure maintained at end-expiration. PEEP helps prevent complete alveolar derecruitment and establishes the pressure baseline for the next breath.

Difference

PIP − Pplat

This difference represents the pressure required to overcome flow resistance under the conditions of the measurement.

PEEP
5 cm H₂O · baseline
Pplat
20 cm H₂O · elastic/static pressure
PIP
30 cm H₂O · elastic + resistive pressure
Resistive pressure ≈ PIP − Pplat
Elastic driving pressure ≈ Pplat − PEEP

5Compliance and resistance

Two Different Reasons a Breath Can Become Harder to Deliver

Elastic load

Compliance = how easily the respiratory system expands

Low compliance means a stiff respiratory system. More pressure is required to create the same volume.

Static compliance = exhaled VT ÷ (Pplat − PEEP)
Compliance ↓ → pressure needed for the same VT ↑

Examples include acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), pulmonary edema, atelectasis, pneumothorax, and reduced chest-wall compliance.

Resistive load

Airway resistance = opposition to gas flow

Resistance rises when the airway or artificial airway becomes narrower or when higher flow encounters more opposition.

Airway resistance ≈ (PIP − Pplat) ÷ flow in L/s
Resistance ↑ → pressure needed to move the same flow ↑

Examples include bronchospasm, mucus, airway edema, a kinked or narrowed tube, biting, or circuit obstruction.

Keep the concepts separate: compliance is a pressure–volume relationship. Resistance is a pressure–flow relationship. A patient can have one problem or both at the same time.
Worked example: Exhaled VT is 450 mL, Pplat is 20 cm H₂O, and PEEP is 5 cm H₂O.
Cstat = 450 ÷ (20 − 5) = 30 mL/cm H₂O
The calculation matters because it turns the pressure-volume relationship into a value you can trend—not because calculation itself is the endpoint.

7Time constants and exhalation

The Lung Needs Enough Time to Fill—and Enough Time to Empty

A time constant describes how quickly a lung unit fills and empties. It is influenced by both resistance and compliance.

Time constant (τ) = resistance × compliance
Longer time constant

Obstruction slows emptying

Higher airway resistance, such as in severe COPD or asthma, can make lung units fill and empty more slowly. Short expiratory time increases the risk of incomplete exhalation and air trapping.

Shorter time constant

Stiff lungs change volume quickly

Low compliance can produce a shorter time constant. These lungs may fill and empty quickly but accept less volume for a given pressure.

Auto-PEEP / intrinsic PEEP: If the next breath begins before exhalation is complete, pressure can remain trapped above the set PEEP. On the flow-time waveform, a key clue is expiratory flow that has not returned to zero before the next inspiration begins.

8Patient triggering and sensitivity

The Ventilator Has to Notice the Patient's Effort

A patient-triggered breath begins when the ventilator detects the patient's inspiratory effort. Trigger sensitivity determines how much change the ventilator requires before it responds.

Pressure trigger

The patient creates a small pressure drop

If the threshold is −2 cm H₂O relative to baseline, the patient must generate enough inspiratory effort to reach that threshold.

Less negative threshold (for example −1 vs −3) → easier pressure trigger
Flow trigger

The ventilator detects a change in circuit flow

Modern ventilators commonly use flow triggering because the patient can often trigger with less pressure effort. The exact implementation is device-specific.

Smaller required flow change → generally more sensitive trigger
Too insensitive

Missed or ineffective efforts

The patient makes an effort but the ventilator does not trigger. Work of breathing can increase. Intrinsic PEEP can also make triggering harder.

Too sensitive

Auto-triggering

The ventilator delivers breaths without true patient effort. Leaks, water in tubing, movement, cardiac oscillation, or an overly sensitive threshold can contribute.

9Cycling and breath timing

Trigger Starts Inspiration; Cycle Ends It

Time cycling

Inspiration ends after the selected inspiratory time

Pressure-controlled mandatory breaths are commonly time-cycled. The set pressure is maintained for the selected inspiratory time, then expiration begins.

Flow cycling

Inspiration ends when inspiratory flow falls to a threshold

Pressure-support breaths are commonly flow-cycled. The patient and respiratory mechanics influence how quickly inspiratory flow decays.

Total cycle time

Respiratory rate determines the time available for each breath cycle

Total cycle time = 60 seconds ÷ respiratory rate

Inspiratory time plus expiratory time must fit inside that cycle.

I:E relationship

Timing changes can create or relieve air trapping

In obstructive disease, preserving enough expiratory time is especially important. Later lessons will cover specific setting adjustments in more detail.

Do not over-memorize labels: Modern ventilators can implement control and cycling in different ways. Learn the physiologic question: what starts inspiration, what is controlled during it, and what makes inspiration stop?
10Basic ventilator graphics

Waveforms Turn Patient–Ventilator Interaction Into Something You Can See

This week, use graphics to recognize broad patterns. Detailed waveform troubleshooting comes in Unit 2.

Pressure-time

Patient trigger notch

A small pressure deflection before positive pressure rises can indicate a patient-triggered breath.

Flow-time

Complete exhalation

Expiratory flow returning to zero before the next inspiration supports complete exhalation.

Flow-time

Air-trapping clue

If the next inspiration begins while expiratory flow is still below zero, exhalation is incomplete and air trapping should be assessed.

Pressure-time

Possible flow mismatch

In a volume-targeted breath, a concave or “scooped” inspiratory pressure pattern can suggest that patient inspiratory demand exceeds the delivered flow. Confirm with the patient and other graphics before changing settings.

12Clinical reasoning framework

Read Every Breath in the Same Order

1 · PatientWhat is the patient doing? Are they comfortable, working hard, or asynchronous?
2 · TriggerDid the patient or the machine start inspiration? Is the trigger being recognized correctly?
3 · Control + mechanicsIs volume or pressure being targeted? What happens if compliance or resistance changes?
4 · Cycle + timingWhat ends inspiration, and is enough time available for expiration?
5 · Graphics + reassessmentDo pressures, volumes, flow, and waveforms match what you see at the bedside?
Carry forward: Volume control protects the selected volume target by allowing pressure to vary. Pressure control protects the selected pressure target by allowing volume to vary. Patient mechanics determine the price.
