Section 1 · Visual literacy

Learning to See the Breath

You're performing your next ventilator assessment. Your patient's SpO₂ is 96%, the total respiratory rate is 18/min and the ventilator isn't alarming. But the patient looks uncomfortable and seems to be making an effort between breaths. The numbers look reassuring; the patient does not. What information is missing?

In 2.1, you learned to check the patient before changing the machine. Here, the graphics give you an additional window into the interaction between the patient and ventilator. They show how pressure, flow and volume change throughout each breath, including changes that a single number or a minute-average may hide.

Memory hook · The numbers tell you what happened; the graphics help show you how it happened. Neither replaces your assessment of the patient.

What is a scalar? What is a loop?

A scalar plots one measured variable against time. Moving from left to right means moving forward in time. A loop plots two measured variables against one another instead; the inspiratory and expiratory limbs trace the relationship as a breath progresses. Loops do not have time on either axis, although each point is acquired at a moment in time.

Where do the values come from?

Pressure sensors measure airway or circuit pressure at a device-specific location. Flow sensors measure gas movement; some are internal and others sit near the patient. The ventilator integrates flow over time to estimate volume. Leak compensation, circuit compliance, sensor location, calibration and condensation may affect the displayed values. Airway pressure is not the same as alveolar pressure during flowing gas.

Read the baseline before reading the shape

Pressure baselineUsually the set PEEP during expiration. A displayed baseline of 5 cmH₂O does not exclude intrinsic PEEP inside the lungs.
Flow baselineZero L/min means no net measured flow at that instant. In our convention positive flow enters and negative flow leaves.
Volume baselineUsually the device's relative zero for a breath, not an empty lung. Functional residual capacity and residual gas still exist.

Read the axis scale and units before comparing shapes. Zooming the pressure axis can make a small change look large; changing the time sweep can make the same expiration look short. Compare numeric values or use identical scales when trending.

Read the breath in this order

1 · PressureWhere is baseline? How high does pressure rise? Is a hold present?
2 · FlowIs flow entering or leaving? Is flow zero during a pause? Has expiration finished?
3 · VolumeHow much volume has entered? How much remains relative to the breath baseline?

Notice that during the inspiratory pause, flow is zero but the volume remains at its maximum. Pressure can fall from peak toward plateau as the resistive component disappears. During expiration, flow is negative, volume falls and airway pressure returns toward set PEEP. These relationships help you distinguish the phases rather than treating three traces as three separate breaths.

Do not overinterpret a single graphic. A high peak pressure does not identify resistance versus compliance by itself. A volume discrepancy suggests a need to check for leaks or measurement issues; a flow pattern may provide additional evidence. Always correlate with the patient, settings and measurements.
Section 2 · Breath anatomy

Anatomy of a Mechanical Breath

Your preceptor asks, “The patient started that breath. Does that mean it was spontaneous?” Not necessarily. A patient can trigger a mandatory assist-control breath whose ending is determined by the ventilator. To answer correctly, you need to separate how the breath starts from how it is delivered and how it ends.

Four questions to ask about every breath

Open each question to explore the explanation, examples and controls.

1Trigger · What starts inspiration?

A time-triggered mandatory breath starts when the machine's timing logic calls for it. In a patient-triggered breath, inspiratory effort changes pressure or flow enough to meet the trigger criterion. A pressure trigger responds to a drop below baseline; a flow trigger detects a change in bias flow or measured flow according to device design. Trigger sensitivity describes how much signal the ventilator requires. A smaller required effort generally means greater sensitivity, but the displayed setting and direction depend on the device.

Time-triggered breath

No visible preceding effort in this idealized example.

Patient-triggered breath

A small pressure dip may precede assistance. Flow triggering may show little or no visible dip.

Do not conclude that every breath without a visible pressure dip is time-triggered. Check the ventilator's breath markers, flow waveform and patient effort. A mandatory breath can be patient- or time-triggered. A spontaneous breath is patient-triggered and patient-cycled (the ventilator may use flow as a surrogate for the patient's inspiratory timing).

2Control · What is controlled during inspiration?

In volume control, the machine delivers a prescribed volume using a specified flow pattern. Pressure changes with resistance, compliance and patient effort. In pressure control, the machine targets airway pressure during inspiration; flow and tidal volume respond to mechanics and effort. In both, safety limits and leaks may prevent the intended target from being reached.

Memory hook: Set volume → pressure varies. Set pressure → volume varies. Always ask which variable is controlled within the breath, not simply which number is displayed on the settings screen.
3Cycle · What ends inspiration?

In time cycling, inspiration ends when the designated inspiratory time expires. In flow cycling, the ventilator ends assistance when inspiratory flow falls to the programmed threshold (often a percentage of peak inspiratory flow). In volume cycling, reaching a designated volume ends inspiration. The exact behavior of volume-controlled modes varies, especially when a pause is included or a ventilator derives inspiratory time from other settings.

Worked example: the flow-cycle threshold

Peak inspiratory flow during a pressure-supported breath is 60 L/min. A 25% cycle threshold is:

60 L/min × 0.25 = 15 L/min

When flow declines to 15 L/min, the ventilator normally ends support, subject to minimum/maximum inspiratory time and other device-specific rules. A higher percentage is reached earlier on the same declining curve; a lower percentage is reached later. We will apply this to asynchrony later.

Explore the cycle threshold

Same idealized decelerating flow curve; not a prediction of a particular patient or ventilator.

4Baseline · What remains between breaths?

PEEP maintains positive airway pressure at end expiration. Expiratory flow can continue even while airway pressure at the ventilator is close to PEEP. When flow has stopped in a relaxed, unobstructed system, alveolar and airway pressures may equilibrate. Incomplete exhalation can leave alveolar pressure above the displayed baseline.

Limit is a separate idea: a limit constrains a variable during inspiration without necessarily ending the breath. A pressure limit is not automatically a cycling criterion; a safety alarm or device-specific termination rule can end delivery.

Section 3 · Establish the normal pattern

Recognizing Normal Graphics Across Modes

Your next patient is receiving pressure control. The pressure waveform looks square and the inspiratory flow declines as the breath progresses. If you expected the square flow pattern from your first patient, you might mistake a normal breath for a problem. Start with the mode and settings before judging its appearance.

In 2.1 you reviewed set versus measured parameters. Now ask which waveforms the machine constrains and which ones reflect the patient's response.

Volume-controlled mandatory breaths (VC-CMV / A/C-VC)

For a constant-flow breath, the flow scalar forms a rectangular inspiratory segment. Volume rises linearly because each equal interval of time adds the same volume. Pressure usually jumps as flow begins and then rises as the respiratory system fills. At the end of flow, a pause (if used) allows pressure to fall toward plateau. With a decelerating flow pattern, the volume curve is no longer linear and the pressure shape changes. VC does not require a square flow waveform on every ventilator.

Representative mandatory breath pattern · cyan inspiration, magenta expiration.

Alternative VC pattern: decelerating inspiratory flow.

Pressure-controlled mandatory breaths (PC-CMV / A/C-PC)

Pressure rises toward its target and is maintained for the set inspiratory time. The initial pressure gradient between airway and alveoli produces higher flow, which typically declines as alveolar pressure rises. Volume rises rapidly at first and then more slowly. A change in compliance, resistance, effort or inspiratory time can change delivered volume even with the same pressure setting.

Pressure terminology matters Some ventilators display pressure above PEEP and others display an absolute inspiratory pressure. For PEEP 5 and pressure control 15 above PEEP, the airway target is 20 cmH₂O. Verify the device's convention.

Representative mandatory breath pattern · cyan inspiration, magenta expiration.

Pressure support (PSV)

In conventional PSV, the patient starts the breath, the ventilator targets support pressure above PEEP and flow usually decelerates. The breath ends when flow meets the cycle criterion, subject to safety and timing limits. The patient and respiratory mechanics influence tidal volume and inspiratory duration. Apnea backup may deliver mandatory breaths if configured. A square pressure shape alone cannot distinguish pressure control from pressure support; check trigger, cycling and mode labels.

Representative patient-initiated breath pattern · cyan inspiration, magenta expiration.

CPAP without pressure support

CPAP maintains one positive baseline pressure during spontaneous breathing without adding a separate inspiratory pressure support target. Flow and volume follow patient effort and respiratory mechanics. The pressure scalar is approximately flat at CPAP with small fluctuations. The patient supplies the inspiratory effort; the machine maintains baseline pressure and may compensate for circuit-related demands. Backup ventilation, if available and active, changes the observed pattern.

Representative patient-initiated breath pattern · cyan inspiration, magenta expiration.

SIMV: distinguish the breath, not just the mode

SIMV allows spontaneous breaths between synchronized mandatory breaths. In VC-SIMV the mandatory breath has volume-control characteristics; in PC-SIMV it has pressure-control characteristics. Spontaneous breaths may be pressure supported or unsupported. You can therefore see two different patterns on the same screen. Use the mode, breath markers and timing to identify them.

Representative mandatory and spontaneous breath pattern · cyan inspiration, magenta expiration.

PC-SIMV example: pressure-controlled mandatory breath followed by pressure support.

Adaptive pressure control targeting volume (e.g., PRVC)

PRVC and related vendor-specific modes deliver pressure-controlled breaths, then adapt the inspiratory pressure target between breaths to approach a selected tidal volume. Flow generally decelerates and the pressure trace resembles pressure control. A volume target does not make it a conventional fixed-flow volume-controlled breath. Pressure limits, effort, leaks and changing mechanics affect the achieved volume. Exact algorithms differ across devices.

Section 4 · Mechanics in motion

When pressure tells a different story

Your patient from 2.1 has a rising peak pressure. Before changing a setting, ask what pressure is paying for. The ventilator must overcome resistance to move gas and elastic recoil to expand the respiratory system. A pressure increase is a clue, not a diagnosis.

Bring it forward: in a passive patient with reliable holds, PIP − plateau reflects the resistive pressure at the measured inspiratory flow, and plateau − total PEEP reflects elastic driving pressure.

Build the pressure waveform from its components

At constant flow in VC, pressure rises rapidly when flow begins because resistance must be overcome. As volume accumulates, elastic pressure rises. When flow stops during an inspiratory hold, the resistive component falls away. The remaining stable pressure approximates alveolar pressure under valid conditions.

Paw = PEEP + (R × flow) + (V / C)

This simplified equation assumes a passive linear system without intrinsic PEEP. Use flow in L/s, volume in L and compliance in L/cmH₂O. Actual pressure can also reflect effort, nonlinear mechanics and circuit effects.

Increased resistance

At matched volume and flow, the peak-to-plateau gap widens. Think bronchospasm, secretions, a narrowed tube or circuit obstruction. Assess the airway, auscultate and inspect the circuit; do not assume every high peak requires a lower VT.

Decreased compliance

At matched VT and total PEEP, plateau and driving pressure rise. Consider edema, atelectasis, consolidation, pneumothorax or chest-wall/abdominal loading. Assess the patient and trends; a sudden deterioration requires urgent evaluation.

What changes in pressure control?

Because the ventilator maintains a pressure target, increased resistance can slow filling and decrease VT during the same inspiratory time. Lower compliance decreases the volume attained at the same pressure difference. In PC, watch flow and volume alongside pressure; the pressure target itself may remain unchanged.

Mean airway pressure: more than a peak

Mean airway pressure (P̄aw) is the area under the pressure–time waveform divided by cycle duration. A longer time at elevated pressure can increase mean airway pressure even if the peak stays the same. Do not confuse P̄aw with mean arterial pressure.

20 cmH₂O for 1 s and 5 cmH₂O for 2 s: (20 × 1 + 5 × 2) / 3 = 10 cmH₂O.

20 cmH₂O for 1.5 s and 5 cmH₂O for 1.5 s: (30 + 7.5) / 3 = 12.5 cmH₂O.

These examples assume ideal square pressure and a 3-second cycle. Real devices integrate the actual trace. Changes in mean airway pressure can affect oxygenation and circulation; reassess both.

Section 5 · Follow the gas

Reading the flow and volume scalars

Now the same patient is receiving a bronchodilator. You want to know whether gas is leaving the lungs more easily. Look at the expiratory flow trace, not just the peak pressure. The flow scalar shows movement; the volume scalar shows the accumulated volume moved through the sensor.

Incomplete exhalation and intrinsic PEEP

In passive expiration, elastic recoil drives gas out through airway resistance. Flow typically approaches zero as the pressure gradient disappears. If the next inspiration begins while expiratory flow is still negative, some gas is still leaving the communicating airways. This suggests incomplete exhalation and possible dynamic hyperinflation. Zero flow does not exclude trapped gas behind closed airways.

Enough time to empty

Expiratory flow returns to baseline before the next inspiration begins. Notice the flat zero-flow interval before the second square inspiratory breath.

Incomplete expiration

Flow remains negative at the next onset. The next square inspiratory breath starts directly from negative expiratory flow, without a zero-flow interval. Assess total PEEP with a valid expiratory hold when appropriate.

Memory hook · Give obstructed lungs time to empty

C-BABE: cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis, asthma, chronic bronchitis and emphysema. Different mechanisms, shared potential for impaired expiratory flow. This section is about time to empty; the scoop-in-the-loop graphic returns in the loop section below.

Time constants explain why

A time constant is resistance multiplied by compliance. In a passive single-compartment model, about 63% of the initial excess volume empties after one time constant, 95% after three and 99% after five. Real lungs contain regions with different time constants. High resistance or high compliance can prolong emptying.

Volume discrepancy: leak or incomplete exhalation?

Compare inspired and expired VT and inspect both flow and volume. A persistent volume deficit may reflect a cuff or circuit leak, a fistula, incomplete exhalation, or measurement artifacts. Oscillations and breath stacking

Sawtooth pattern

Irregular oscillations may reflect airway secretions, condensate or other disturbances. Inspect tubing and assess breath sounds, secretions and airway patency. Suction when indicated, using local procedures; remove condensate safely away from the patient.

Two breaths with little exhalation

Section 6 · Two variables, one breath

Understanding ventilator loops

Scalars tell you when an event occurred. Loops show how two variables relate during the same breath. A loop is traced in sequence from inspiration to expiration. Identify axes, baseline and direction before interpreting its shape; display conventions vary by manufacturer.

Flow–volume loops

In our convention, volume is horizontal and flow vertical. Cyan inspiration moves from left to right above zero; magenta expiration returns from right to left below zero. The following examples use a rounded inspiratory limb; constant-flow VC may look rectangular instead.

Normal loop

Expiratory flow peaks early at high volume, then approaches zero as volume returns toward baseline.

Scoop in the loop

The expiratory limb curves toward zero flow compared with the reference. This suggests expiratory flow limitation or obstruction; assess C-BABE conditions, bronchospasm, secretions and airway patency.

Sawtooth

Oscillations on either limb may reflect secretions or condensate. Correlate with the patient and circuit before intervening.

Leak / incomplete closure

Returned volume fails to match delivered volume in this schematic display. Compare measured volumes, cuff and circuit, and whether expiratory flow persists.

Memory hook · Scoop in the loop

Picture an ice cream scoop removing part of the expiratory limb. Less flow at a given volume is your clue. Confirm with the patient, not just the picture.

Pressure–volume loops: slope tells the story

Pressure is horizontal and relative volume vertical. At the same volume, a less compliant respiratory system needs more elastic pressure. The slope ΔV/ΔP is steeper when compliance is greater and flatter when compliance is lower. Dynamic loops also reflect resistive pressure, flow, patient effort and hysteresis; compare conditions and use valid holds for static compliance.

Inflection points, bird's beak and fish tail

Compliance may change over the course of a breath. At low volumes, a flatter initial region can become steeper as units open. At higher volumes, a flattening upper region suggests that extra pressure produces little extra volume. A dynamic loop is influenced by flow and effort; it cannot identify an exact optimal PEEP on its own. We will revisit recruitment and PEEP titration in the oxygenation lesson.

Lower inflection point (LIP)

On the inspiratory limb, the transition from a flatter slope to a steeper slope suggests that additional pressure is allowing more lung units to accept volume. Traditionally, PEEP was set near or slightly above the LIP (often 2 cmH₂O above it) to help maintain recruitment between breaths. In the schematic, LIP is approximately 16 cmH₂O, so the historical teaching estimate would be 18 cmH₂O.

Upper inflection point (UIP)

The inspiratory slope flattens near the top (approximately 25 cmH₂O in this schematic). Additional pressure produces little volume gain. A bird's beak suggests possible overdistension; consider reducing tidal volume and reassess plateau pressure.

Clinical distinction: Opening pressure is not necessarily the pressure needed to keep units open. Recruitment can occur across a range of pressures. A routine dynamic loop cannot determine optimal PEEP; use oxygenation, recruitability, plateau and driving pressure, compliance and hemodynamics. The LIP + 2 example is a historical teaching heuristic, not a bedside prescription.

Bird's beak

Near the top, pressure continues to increase with minimal volume gain. Consider possible overdistension and assess VT/PBW, plateau, driving pressure, total PEEP and patient condition. Reassess after appropriate lung-protective adjustments.

Fish tail

A leftward pressure deflection near the start of inspiration can reflect patient effort before assistance. It may suggest trigger difficulty, but confirm with pressure and flow scalars, effort, sensitivity and intrinsic PEEP.

Memory hook · Bird's beak

Pressure keeps climbing, volume barely changes. The flattened tip is the clue; it is not a standalone diagnosis.

Memory hook · Fish tail

A leftward tail at the start of the loop suggests patient effort before assistance. Think about trigger sensitivity, patient demand and intrinsic PEEP — then confirm with the pressure and flow scalars.

Guided case · Status asthmaticus

A passive VC patient has VT 450 mL, flow 30 L/min, PIP 40, plateau 20 and total PEEP 10 cmH₂O (set PEEP 5). Section 7 · When the patient and ventilator disagree

Patient–Ventilator Asynchrony

At your next assessment the patient is grimacing, making inspiratory efforts between delivered breaths and occasionally receiving two breaths close together. The set rate is unchanged. The problem may not be the amount of support alone, but when and how it is delivered.

Asynchrony is a mismatch between the patient's respiratory effort and the ventilator's assistance. It can increase work of breathing, discomfort and unintended tidal volume. Assess the patient first: respiratory drive, pain, anxiety, fever, acidemia, airway patency, mechanics and level of assistance. If unstable, summon help and follow emergency procedures. Do not automatically sedate a patient to make the waveform look normal.

Memory hook · Start, supply, stop. Did assistance start when the patient needed it? Did it supply enough flow? Did it stop when the patient finished inspiring?

Triggering · Getting the breath started

Patient effort must create a signal that the ventilator detects. With a pressure trigger of −2 cmH₂O relative to baseline, the patient must lower circuit pressure by 2 cmH₂O; a −1 threshold is easier to reach. With flow triggering, a smaller required flow change is generally more sensitive. Verify the device's terminology rather than assuming that a higher numerical setting always means greater sensitivity.

Ineffective triggering

A small pressure dip and/or expiratory flow deflection occurs without a delivered breath. Consider weak effort, excessive support, insensitive triggering or intrinsic PEEP. Trapped gas creates an extra threshold load before the patient can generate a detectable airway signal. Assess total PEEP when appropriate and address the cause; adjust sensitivity cautiously.

Delayed triggering

Effort begins but assistance arrives late. The patient must work longer before support begins. Look for a prolonged pre-trigger pressure dip, compare with observed effort and assess intrinsic PEEP, sensitivity and respiratory muscle strength.

Auto-triggering

A breath is delivered without a matching patient effort or intended timed trigger. Leaks, water in the circuit, cardiac oscillations or an overly sensitive trigger can create false signals. Inspect the circuit and airway first; make triggering less sensitive if appropriate and reassess for missed efforts.

Normal triggering

A brief effort signal precedes prompt assistance. Flow-triggered breaths may have little visible pressure dip. A ventilator marker is useful but does not prove that actual patient effort occurred.

Delivery · Meeting inspiratory demand

During constant-flow VC, a patient who wants more flow may generate additional negative pleural pressure. Measured airway pressure falls below its expected contour, even while the machine delivers the prescribed flow. In pressure-targeted modes the machine can vary flow; a slow rise time may still fail to provide adequate early assistance.

Memory hook · Air-hungry shark

The scooped inspiratory pressure contour looks like a bite taken out of the breath. In constant-flow VC, think unmet inspiratory flow demand: the goal is to meet valid patient effort without assuming every scoop means more tidal volume is needed.

Expected constant-flow VC

Pressure rises as volume increases; constant flow produces a predictable contour in a passive patient.

Flow starvation

Inspiratory effort depresses the pressure contour. Evaluate respiratory drive and flow demand. A higher flow or different flow pattern may help; check resulting inspiratory time, pressures and comfort.

Slow pressurization

In PC or PSV, pressure builds slowly toward its target. If the patient needs rapid early flow, a faster rise may improve comfort.

Excessively rapid pressurization

An early pressure overshoot can accompany overly aggressive rise settings. Consider slower pressurization, checking device behavior and patient effort.

Cycling · Ending assistance at the right time

Neural inspiratory time is the interval during which the patient wants to inhale. Mechanical inspiratory time is how long the ventilator delivers inspiration. The two may not match. In PC, evaluate set inspiratory time; in PSV, evaluate the flow-cycle threshold, leaks and mechanics. A higher percentage of peak flow normally ends PSV sooner, provided other limits do not intervene.

Premature cycling

Assistance stops while the patient is still inspiring. Continued effort can distort early expiratory flow or initiate another breath. In PSV, consider a lower cycling percentage to extend inspiration, after assessing drive and VT.

Delayed cycling

Assistance continues after patient inspiration ends. Active exhalation against support may cause a late pressure spike. Obstructive mechanics and leaks can delay flow cycling; consider a higher cycling percentage or shorter PC inspiratory time as appropriate.

Breaths interacting with other breaths

Double triggering

Two assisted breaths occur with little expiration between them, often during one prolonged effort. Stacked volume can exceed the intended VT. Evaluate neural versus machine timing, respiratory drive and lung protection; do not simply raise VT.

Reverse triggering · advanced recognition

A machine-initiated breath is followed by entrained patient effort. It may distort the trace or cause a second breath. Surface graphics alone are not definitive; repeated timing relationships and additional effort monitoring may help the clinical team distinguish this from ordinary patient triggering.

All atlas waveforms are schematic teaching examples. Gold bands in cycling examples represent illustrative patient effort timing, not a measured neural signal.

Essential Notes & Common Questions

ScalarsPressure, flow and volume versus time. Read them on a shared time axis.
LoopsTwo variables plotted against each other; identify both limbs.
Breath anatomyTrigger starts; control shapes delivery; cycle ends inspiration; baseline remains.
Normal depends on modeVerify settings and breath type before judging a shape.
Can I identify a mode using only the pressure waveform?

No. PC and PSV can look similar; adaptive pressure control also uses pressure-targeted delivery. Read the mode label, flow, timing and breath markers.

Does zero flow mean the lungs are empty?

No. Zero measured flow means no net movement at the sensor. Lung volume remains. Intrinsic PEEP can exist in regions that are not communicating with the airway.

Why does the volume stay high during a pause?

The ventilator has stopped delivering flow but expiration has not started. Volume stays approximately constant in a closed system.

Does patient-triggered mean spontaneous?

No. A patient can initiate a mandatory breath that is terminated by the ventilator's timing or volume logic.

Is a decelerating flow pattern always pressure control?

No. Some volume-control modes allow decelerating flow. Compare all signals and verify settings.

PressureCompare peak, plateau, total PEEP and flow under valid conditions.

Can I set PEEP using a loop inflection point alone?

No. Dynamic loops reflect flow and effort; PEEP decisions require oxygenation, mechanics, hemodynamics and clinical context.

Clinical pearls · Asynchrony

Start, supply, stop: trigger, delivery and cycle. Match the control to the mode. A higher PSV flow-cycle percentage generally shortens Ti; a lower required trigger signal generally makes triggering easier. Correct leaks and condensate before attributing every extra breath to sensitivity. Assess drive and timing before increasing sedation or VT.

Why can a patient miss breaths even with a sensitive trigger?

Intrinsic PEEP may impose a threshold load; effort may not reach the sensor. Assess mechanics and patient effort before adjusting sensitivity.

Why does a higher PSV cycle percentage shorten inspiration?

Decelerating flow reaches the higher threshold sooner, unless timing limits or other cycling criteria override it.

Is every double breath reverse triggering?

No. Double triggering may follow a prolonged spontaneous effort. Reverse triggering starts with ventilator-induced effort; confirmation may require additional monitoring.
