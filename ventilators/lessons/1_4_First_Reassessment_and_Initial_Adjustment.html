1 · Name the problem before changing a setting

Classify Ventilation, Oxygenation, or Combined Failure

The first reassessment asks whether the initial plan produced the intended physiologic result. Read the ABG with the current settings and the patient—not as an isolated set of laboratory values.

Ventilation questionAre pH and PaCO₂ appropriate for this patient and disease process?
Oxygenation questionAre SpO₂ and PaO₂ acceptable on the current FiO₂ and PEEP?
Delivery questionAre VT, V̇E, pressures, alarms, hemodynamics, and patient response acceptable?
Picture the patient

The same PaCO₂ can mean different things in different patients

PaCO₂ 60 with pH 7.20 in a previously normal postoperative patient signals acute ventilatory failure. PaCO₂ 60 with HCO₃⁻ 33 and near-baseline pH in severe COPD may be close to the patient's chronic state. Classification begins with the numbers but ends with the clinical context.

First-adjustment discipline: identify the dominant problem and make the first essential change. Multiple simultaneous changes make it difficult to know what corrected—or worsened—the response.

The same low VT leads to different knob choices

Volume controlThe ventilator targets VT. If exhaled VT is unexpectedly low, think leak or delivery failure before simply changing the set VT.
Pressure controlThe ventilator targets pressure and time. Low exhaled VT may require more inspiratory pressure after mechanics and safety are evaluated.
Do not use an oxygenation control to treat isolated hypercapnia. FiO₂ and PEEP may be appropriate for a simultaneous oxygenation problem, but they do not substitute for adequate effective ventilation.

3 · Calculate rather than guess

Solve for Desired V̇E, Desired Rate, and Expected PaCO₂

Desired V̇E = Current PaCO₂ × Current V̇E ÷ Desired PaCO₂
Desired RR = Desired V̇E ÷ selected safe VT in liters
Predicted PaCO₂ = Current PaCO₂ × Current V̇E ÷ New V̇E
Worked sequence

Turn a hypercapnic ABG into a testable plan

Current PaCO₂ is 60 mm Hg at V̇E 6.0 L/min. If desired PaCO₂ is 45, desired V̇E is 8.0 L/min. With VT 0.500 L, the calculated rate is 16/min. If the new measured V̇E is truly 8.0 L/min, predicted PaCO₂ is 45 mm Hg. The repeat ABG can now be compared with an explicit expectation.

The math creates a candidate—not permission to violate physiology. A calculated rate may be unsafe in severe obstruction, and a calculated VT may be excessive for ARDS. Preserve PBW-based VT, expiratory time, and pressure safety.

4 · Adjust volume control without abandoning lung protection

Protect VT First, Then Use Rate When Expiration Permits

VT already appropriateFor hypercapnia with complete exhalation, increase rate toward the calculated target.
VT excessiveReduce VT to a PBW-based target, then recalculate the rate needed to preserve or increase V̇E.
Expiration incompleteDo not reflexively raise rate. Reassess flow, Ti, I:E, VT, and the acceptability of permissive hypercapnia.
Compare two hypercapnic patients

PaCO₂ 60 does not dictate one universal change

Postoperative patientProtective VT, low rate, and complete exhalation make a rate increase reasonable.
Severe COPDIncomplete exhalation means the same rate increase could worsen air trapping, hemodynamics, and effective ventilation.
Carry forward: before increasing ventilation, verify that VT is appropriate for PBW. Then choose a safe way to change V̇E.

5 · Adjust pressure-targeted support by the volume it actually produces

Use Exhaled VT to Titrate Pressure Control and Bilevel NIV

Pressure controlIncrease inspiratory pressure when VT is below target; reduce pressure when improved mechanics produce excessive VT. Reassess VT after every change.
Bilevel NIVIncrease IPAP relative to EPAP for ventilation. Increase EPAP for oxygenation while preserving or widening pressure support as the problem requires.
Mechanics change the result

A pressure that was appropriate an hour ago may become excessive

Bronchodilation or improved compliance can increase VT at the same inspiratory pressure. The correct response is not to celebrate the larger number automatically—it is to compare the new VT with the PBW-based target and reduce pressure when necessary.

Pressure display reminder: understand whether the ventilator displays inspiratory pressure above PEEP or total inspiratory pressure. The pressure available to inflate the lung depends on the difference above baseline.

6 · Choose a patient-specific endpoint

Do Not Automatically Target PaCO₂ 40 mm Hg

Patient patternReasonable target thinkingWhat to avoid
Previously normal ventilationMove pH and PaCO₂ toward an appropriate acute targetIgnoring persistent acidemia
Chronic compensated COPDUse baseline PaCO₂, HCO₃⁻, pH, and air-trapping riskRapidly forcing PaCO₂ to 40
ARDS with pressure limitationPrioritize lung protection and acceptable pHIncreasing VT solely to normalize PaCO₂
Picture chronic compensation

Normalizing PaCO₂ can create a new problem

A patient whose baseline PaCO₂ is 60 and HCO₃⁻ is 33 has renal compensation. Rapidly ventilating PaCO₂ to 40 may produce marked alkalemia and require a rate that worsens air trapping. The safer endpoint may be improved pH and movement toward baseline.

Prediction makes reassessment meaningful: state the expected direction and approximate magnitude before changing the ventilator. Then compare the measured VT, V̇E, pH, PaCO₂, oxygenation, pressure, and patient tolerance with that prediction.

7 · Separate pulmonary oxygenation from oxygen delivery

Choose FiO₂ or PEEP—Then Ask Whether Oxygen Can Be Carried and Delivered

FiO₂Raises the oxygen concentration delivered to the alveoli. A reasonable first move for modest hypoxemia on a low FiO₂.
PEEPSupports end-expiratory recruitment. More important when substantial FiO₂ is already present and collapse, edema, or shunt-like physiology persists.
P/F = PaO₂ ÷ FiO₂
A–a gradient = PAO₂ − PaO₂
CaO₂ = (1.34 × Hb × SaO₂) + (0.003 × PaO₂)
DO₂ = Cardiac output × CaO₂ × 10
Link prior physiology to the ventilator

Improved SpO₂ does not guarantee adequate DO₂

PaO₂ 60 on FiO₂ 0.80 gives a P/F ratio of 75. If calculated PAO₂ is 500, the A–a gradient is 440 mm Hg—evidence of major pulmonary oxygen-transfer impairment. If hemoglobin is 7 g/dL and cardiac output is 3 L/min, CaO₂ and DO₂ may remain low even after SpO₂ improves. FiO₂ and PEEP address pulmonary oxygenation; transfusion decisions, perfusion, and cardiac output involve additional clinical management.

Reassess hemodynamics after PEEP changes. Better recruitment is useful only if the patient tolerates the associated intrathoracic-pressure effect.

8 · Verify the response and protect the patient during deterioration

Close the Loop: Alarms, Positioning, Reassessment, and Emergency Support

AlarmSet and interpret limits from stable patient values and the mode.
PositionElevate the head of bed 30–45° when not contraindicated.
Expected responseCompare measured change with the predicted VT, V̇E, PaCO₂, pH, or oxygenation response.
EmergencyIf support is failing and the cause is unclear, manually ventilate with oxygen while evaluating patient, airway, circuit, and ventilator.
The manual breath is diagnostic as well as supportive

What does the bag feel like?

If manual ventilation is difficult, resistance, obstruction, bronchospasm, pneumothorax, or poor compliance becomes more likely. If manual ventilation is easy and oxygenation improves, the ventilator or circuit becomes more suspicious. Continue supporting the patient while localizing and correcting the cause.

Do not solve acute deterioration by silencing or widening the alarm. Alarm changes do not restore ventilation, reconnect a circuit, remove an obstruction, or correct worsening mechanics.

Carry-forward framework

The First-Adjustment Algorithm

1 · VerifyReview patient, airway, circuit, mode, settings, measured delivery, pressures, and alarms.
2 · ClassifyName ventilation, oxygenation, combined failure, or a delivery/safety problem.
3 · ChangeSelect the mode-specific variable that addresses the problem without violating PBW, pressure, or timing safety.
4 · Close the loopPredict, measure, compare, and decide whether the response is acceptable.

Why Hemodynamics Must Be Reassessed After a PEEP Change

Blood flows from an area of higher pressure toward an area of lower pressure. Systemic venous blood returns to the right atrium because a pressure gradient drives it back to the chest. Adding PEEP raises intrathoracic pressure and can raise the pressure surrounding the right atrium. When the pressure at the receiving end rises, the gradient for venous return becomes smaller.

1 · Pressure gradientVenous return depends on blood moving from relatively higher systemic venous pressure toward lower right-atrial pressure.
2 · PEEP raises chest pressureHigher intrathoracic pressure increases the pressure opposing blood returning to the right side of the heart.
3 · Preload may fallA smaller pressure gradient can reduce venous return, right-ventricular filling, and ultimately left-ventricular preload.
4 · Perfusion may fallCardiac output and blood pressure can decrease even while recruitment, PaO₂, and SpO₂ improve.
Picture the response

Better oxygenation does not automatically mean the PEEP increase was tolerated

If SpO₂ rises after PEEP increases but blood pressure falls, the oxygenation effect occurred while circulation worsened. The effect is often more pronounced in a patient who is hypovolemic, already hypotensive, or has right-ventricular dysfunction. Higher lung volumes can also increase pulmonary vascular resistance and right-ventricular afterload.

After increasing PEEP, reassess both oxygenation and circulation. Check blood pressure or MAP, heart rate, perfusion, mental status, urine output when available, and other signs of cardiac-output tolerance. The goal is improved gas exchange without sacrificing systemic perfusion.
