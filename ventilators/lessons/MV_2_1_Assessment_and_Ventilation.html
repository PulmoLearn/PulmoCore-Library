From ventilator setup to clinical optimization

▶Instructor videoRecording pending · V00
Read the draft transcript

The ventilator is running, but the patient is changing. Imagine two patients with the same PaCO₂: one has obstructed airways, the other has stiff lungs. Would you turn up the rate on both? Not necessarily. Our first job is to assess the patient and compare what we set with what is actually delivered. Then ask why gas exchange is inadequate. Is minute ventilation low, is dead space increasing, or is the acid–base problem metabolic? Next, look at the pressures: peak and plateau help distinguish resistance from elastic load, and an expiratory hold helps identify intrinsic PEEP. Now calculate a desired minute ventilation, but do not treat it as an order. If the rate is limited by obstruction, work backward to a tidal volume; if volume is limited by lung protection, work backward to a rate. Check both against the patient’s limits. A faster rate leaves less time to exhale; a higher constant inspiratory flow may shorten inspiration. Sometimes the mode itself needs to change: assist-control supports every triggered mandatory breath, SIMV permits spontaneous breaths between mandatory breaths, and pressure control targets pressure rather than volume. Finally, reassess. A better CO₂ is not a success if pressures, trapping or blood pressure worsen. In this lesson we will practice each step, then bring them together in evolving patient cases. The goal is not to memorize which button to press. It is to explain why a change fits this patient, and what evidence would make you keep or revise it.

Bridge from Unit 1

The ventilator is set up. Now what?

In Unit 1, you chose initial settings and checked the first response. That gave the patient a starting level of support. It didn’t tell you whether those settings would still be right at the next assessment.

Consider two patients: one needs short-term support after surgery and may be ready for extubation in less than four hours; another with acute respiratory failure may need days of reassessment and adjustment. The timeline varies. In both cases, the team starts support, optimizes and maintains it while it’s needed, and evaluates readiness to wean and extubate when appropriate. These phases can overlap or repeat as the patient’s condition changes.

Unit 2 focuses on optimizing and maintaining support: Is the patient getting what we intended? Is it working? Do we leave the settings alone, troubleshoot a problem, or recommend a change? Weaning and extubation are later topics.

At the bedside: the patient–ventilator assessment

It’s time for your next check. In this teaching case, your unit schedules routine assessments every two hours. In practice, follow local policy and patient acuity, and reassess after changes or new concerns rather than waiting for the next scheduled check.

Assess the patientListen to breath sounds and assess breathing effort, comfort and synchrony, chest movement, and vital-sign trends. Compare with the previous check and review available gas-exchange data.
Verify settings and responseConfirm the mode, ordered settings and alarm limits. Compare measured rate, exhaled volume, pressures and waveforms with the plan and prior values.
Check the airway and equipmentCheck airway security, circuit connections, humidification and condensate, and any alarms or evidence of leaks or obstruction. Perform routine equipment care per policy; report and troubleshoot new findings with your preceptor.

The check gives you a decision, not just a chart entry. If the patient is stable and support meets the goals, document and continue it. If the patient deteriorates, assess promptly and get help. A measurement that doesn’t match what you expected is a reason to investigate, not automatically a reason to change a setting.

Bedside reasoning

1 · Decide why ventilation is inadequate

You finish the vent check and report an exhaled minute ventilation of 7.6 L/min. Your preceptor asks, “Does that tell us the patient is clearing enough CO₂?” It tells you how much exhaled gas the ventilator counted. To judge whether ventilation is meeting the patient’s needs, you still have to look at the patient and the PaCO₂ and pH trend.

When an ABG changes, decide what is driving it

PaCO₂ reflects the balance between CO₂ production and alveolar ventilation. If CO₂ production rises or effective alveolar ventilation falls, PaCO₂ can rise even though the ventilator display is unchanged. Before changing settings, determine whether the disturbance is primarily respiratory or metabolic.

PatternLikely interpretationWhat to investigate
pH low, PaCO₂ highRespiratory acidosis (if primary)Low effective ventilation, obstruction, leaks, increased dead space, increased CO₂ production.
pH high, PaCO₂ lowRespiratory alkalosis (if primary)Excess mandatory support, patient-triggered breaths, pain, anxiety, hypoxemia, fever or auto-triggering.
pH low, HCO₃⁻ lowMetabolic acidosis (if primary)Underlying metabolic cause; low PaCO₂ may be appropriate compensation.
pH high, HCO₃⁻ highMetabolic alkalosis (if primary)Underlying metabolic cause; elevated PaCO₂ may be compensation.

Use pH, PaCO₂ and bicarbonate together, compare with previous ABGs and consider expected compensation or mixed disorders. In metabolic acidosis, expected PaCO₂ ≈ 1.5 × HCO₃⁻ + 8 ± 2 mmHg (Winter’s formula). Do not blindly normalize a compensatory PaCO₂. In a chronically hypercapnic patient, the baseline and pH may be more useful than a target of 40.

Less CO₂ eliminationAirway obstruction, dynamic hyperinflation, low tidal volume, reduced rate, apparatus dead space or perfusion abnormalities.
More CO₂ productionFever, shivering, seizures, increased metabolic demand and agitation. Address the underlying cause with the care team.

Apparatus dead space is added by components between the patient and circuit wye, such as some adapters or HMEs. Avoid unnecessary added volume; changes to humidification or circuit components must follow policy and patient needs. This is distinct from anatomic and alveolar dead space.

Step 3 · Make the ventilator decision

The actual PaCO₂ is 29 mmHg. Should you reduce ventilation just to push the PaCO₂ toward 40?

Follow one breath. Gas first fills the conducting airways, where no gas exchange takes place. That portion is anatomic dead space. The rest reaches the alveoli, but some alveoli may receive air and too little blood flow for that air to contribute much to gas exchange. That adds alveolar dead space. Both portions can still come back through the circuit and count toward exhaled minute ventilation.

A leak raises a different question: did the sensor count all the exhaled gas? If some escapes before reaching the sensor, the recorded volume can be lower than the patient actually exhaled. Compare inspired and expired volumes and check the circuit or interface before assuming the patient needs more ventilator support.

Worked case · The number on the screen didn’t change

Returned tidal volume answers “How much gas came back?” It doesn’t answer “How much of that breath exchanged CO₂?” Two patients can both return 420 mL. If more of one breath goes to ventilated but poorly perfused alveoli, that gas still returns to the sensor but contributes less to CO₂ elimination. Measuring the CO₂ in the returned gas adds information that volume alone cannot provide.

Where the two CO₂ values come from

  • PaCO₂ comes from an arterial blood gas. It tells you the CO₂ partial pressure in arterial blood.
  • PĒCO₂ is the CO₂ partial pressure averaged over the whole exhaled volume, including low-CO₂ gas from the conducting airways. Obtain it from a representative mixed-expired gas sample or a compatible volumetric capnography system that measures CO₂ against exhaled volume. Use a measurement taken near the ABG under comparable conditions. Do not substitute end-tidal CO₂ (PETCO₂), which samples the end of exhalation rather than the whole breath.

Gas that picks up little CO₂ dilutes the CO₂ in the mixed exhaled breath. The Enghoff calculation expresses the arterial-to-mixed-expired difference as a fraction of the arterial value: estimated VD/VT = (PaCO₂ − PĒCO₂) ÷ PaCO₂.

If airway and apparatus dead space are unchanged, increased alveolar dead space is one possible explanation. But the Enghoff value estimates overall physiologic dead-space fraction; it does not directly isolate alveolar dead space. Shunt and other ventilation–perfusion mismatch can also raise it. At the bedside: Check for leak or sampling problems, verify that delivered ventilation and the circuit setup are comparable, and interpret the trend with the patient’s perfusion, clinical changes, and ABG. If you only have VTe and an ABG, you can suspect inefficient ventilation, but you cannot calculate this fraction without mixed-expired CO₂.

Reference: Dead space in acute respiratory distress syndrome.

What would point elsewhere? If exhaled VT and total rate both fell without a new leak, low measured minute ventilation is a more direct lead. If inspired VT is similar but exhaled VT falls and a new leak appears, verify the measurement and investigate the leak before attributing the problem to insufficient support. A true leak can also impair delivery; it isn’t necessarily just a display error.

Bedside reasoning

2 · Read the pressures before changing the mode

Pressure, volume and flow: what each measurement means

Think of the ventilator as doing two kinds of work: moving gas through resistance and expanding the respiratory system. At a given tidal volume and constant flow, greater resistance raises the pressure needed while gas is moving; reduced compliance raises the pressure needed to expand the system. These relationships are easiest to isolate when the patient is passive and measurements are reliable.

MeasurementMeaning and acquisitionHow to use it
PIPHighest airway pressure during inspiration; read the pressure trace/monitor.Includes resistive and elastic loads plus baseline pressure; not a direct measure of alveolar pressure.
PplatStable pressure at zero inspiratory flow during a valid hold.Estimate elastic load with total PEEP and VT; commonly aim ≤30 cmH₂O in lung-protective ventilation.
Set / total PEEPSet baseline vs pressure measured during a valid end-expiratory hold.Total minus set PEEP estimates intrinsic PEEP.
Driving pressurePplat − total PEEP in passive conditions.Elastic pressure change for the delivered breath; trend alongside VT and compliance.

Three pressures, one end-expiratory starting point

Extrinsic PEEP (applied PEEP)

The pressure intentionally set on the ventilator at end expiration: here, 5 cmH₂O.

Intrinsic PEEP (auto-PEEP)

Additional pressure remaining in the respiratory system because exhalation is incomplete. Airflow obstruction and insufficient expiratory time can contribute.

Total PEEP is estimated during a valid end-expiratory hold in a passive patient without significant leak. Total PEEP = set PEEP + intrinsic PEEP.

If total PEEP is 9 and set PEEP is 5, intrinsic PEEP is 4 cmH₂O. Total PEEP matters when interpreting driving pressure and static compliance.

What a hold actually tells you

Interpretable hold

Peak includes the pressure required to move gas through resistance. When flow stops and pressure settles, plateau estimates the pressure needed to hold that breath above its end-expiratory starting point when interpreted relative to total PEEP.

Distorted hold

Check the synchronized flow trace for zero flow and the patient for effort. A dip or drift during the hold can reflect effort or leak; it is not a number to plug into a compliance calculation without verification.

See what happens when flow stops

Inspiratory hold

Expiratory hold

Interpret the holds: A valid inspiratory hold measures plateau at zero flow; a valid end-expiratory hold estimates total PEEP. Persistent expiratory flow at the next breath suggests incomplete exhalation. Effort or leak can distort either measurement; an unreliable hold cannot rule out trapping.

Read the pressure differences: At matched VT and flow, peak minus plateau reflects resistive pressure, while plateau minus total PEEP reflects elastic driving pressure. If plateau and total PEEP both rise by 8 cm H₂O, driving pressure is unchanged; this alone does not indicate reduced compliance.

Mean airway pressure: the whole breath counts

3 · Same goal, different control

From a single adjustment to a complete ventilation plan

In Unit 1 you learned to adjust one setting after an initial ABG. Now the patient may need a different tidal volume AND a different frequency because their mechanics, gas exchange and safety limits have changed. Start by deciding what ventilation the patient needs, then work backward from the restrictions.

Unit 1 refresher · Single-parameter equations

Desired f = current f × current PaCO₂ ÷ desired PaCO₂ (when VT stays constant).

Desired VT = current VT × current PaCO₂ ÷ desired PaCO₂ (when f stays constant).

These are estimates assuming stable CO₂ production and dead-space fraction. Changing VT may change that fraction.

Use consistent terminology: ideal starting VE = IBW (kg) ÷ 10; measured VE is exhaled ventilation; desired VE is an estimate based on the PaCO₂ target. Report VE to the nearest tenth of a liter per minute.

Your preceptor points to the flow waveform: “The next breath is starting before this patient has finished exhaling. How much time are we giving them to breathe out?” Before you decide which control to change, learn to read the breath clock.

Read the breath clock

  1. Total cycle time (TCT) is the time from the start of one breath to the start of the next. For regularly spaced breaths, TCT = 60 ÷ f, where f is the actual rate in breaths/min. At 12 breaths/min, 60 ÷ 12 = 5 seconds per breath. If the patient triggers irregularly, use the actual start-to-start interval on the trace for the breath you’re assessing.
  2. Inspiratory time (Ti) runs from the start of inspiration to the start of expiration, including any inspiratory pause.
  3. Expiratory time (Te) runs from the start of expiration to the next breath. Te = TCT − Ti.

Practice: TCT is 5 seconds and Ti is 1 second. What is Te?

5 − 1 = 4 seconds.

Turn the two times into an I:E ratio

I:E means inspiratory time compared with expiratory time. Write Ti : Te, then divide both sides by Ti so inspiration is 1 part. Here, 1 second : 4 seconds becomes 1:4: for every 1 part of inspiration, there are 4 parts of expiration.

Practice: f is 15/min and Ti is 0.8 seconds. Find TCT, Te and I:E.

TCT = 60 ÷ 15 = 4 seconds. Te = 4 − 0.8 = 3.2 seconds. Divide both sides of 0.8:3.2 by 0.8 to get 1:4. Same ratio as the waveform, but less time to exhale.

Same goal: more time to exhale. Different controls: check the mode.

With airflow obstruction, such as COPD, the lungs can take longer to empty. Choosing a longer expiratory time gives the patient more time to exhale CO₂-containing gas and can help prevent air trapping. Be sure to check the actual expiratory time, whether flow returns to baseline before the next breath, and the patient’s ventilation response. More time to exhale doesn’t guarantee a lower PaCO₂.

Not every ventilator or mode exposes I:E as a setting. Some let you set the ratio directly; others display it as the result of the rate, inspiratory time, flow or pause. Find what your mode lets you set before deciding how to gain time.

Constant-flow volume control

Increase flow at the same set VT to deliver the breath sooner. For 450 mL with no pause, 27 L/min takes 1.0 second; 36 L/min takes 0.75 seconds. Recheck delivered VT, expiratory flow, and peak versus plateau pressure.

Time-cycled pressure control

Shorten set inspiratory time directly. Recheck expiratory flow and exhaled VT; the shorter time at the pressure target may deliver less volume.

Other controls · Check what your device and mode allow

  • Inspiratory pause: Shorten an active pause to give that time back to expiration at the same rate.
  • I:E or Te: If directly adjustable, allocate more of the cycle to expiration. Verify the resulting Te in seconds.
  • Mandatory rate: Lowering it can lengthen the cycle if the actual rate falls. Recheck minute ventilation.
  • ETS% (expiratory trigger sensitivity): On compatible devices in flow-cycled pressure support, a higher percentage of peak inspiratory flow cycles the breath to expiration sooner. Recheck synchrony, exhaled VT, and actual Te. This is not the inspiratory trigger or the Ti control for time-cycled breaths.

Frequently asked questions

Does a high PIP always mean the alveoli are overdistended?

No. PIP includes resistance. Plateau pressure, VT/PBW and total PEEP help clarify elastic load.

Does flow returning to zero exclude all intrinsic PEEP?

No. It is reassuring but does not rule out regional gas trapping. Interpret with the clinical picture and valid holds.

Can I calculate airway resistance from a decelerating pressure-control breath using peak flow?

Not with the simple constant-flow equation taught here. Flow and pressure vary over the breath; use a suitable measurement method.

Why not simply correct every PaCO₂ to 40?

Baseline hypercapnia, compensation for metabolic disorders, and lung-protective constraints can make a different target appropriate.
