1 · Describe every breath

Use Trigger, Control, Limit, Cycle, and Baseline

Mode names are easier to understand when you first connect ventilator language to what the body normally accomplishes during an unassisted breath.

What this looks like without mechanical ventilation

Your body normally coordinates the whole breath

Start and generate flowBrainstem respiratory drive activates the respiratory muscles. Their contraction lowers pleural and alveolar pressure, creating the gradient that draws air into the lungs.
Shape and end inspirationNeural inspiratory activity, lung and chest-wall mechanics, and sensory feedback shape the breath. Inspiration usually ends when neural activity switches off; stretch feedback contributes more at larger lung volumes.
Return to baselineThe inspiratory muscles relax and elastic recoil produces passive exhalation. In healthy quiet breathing, airway and alveolar pressure return near atmospheric pressure at end expiration while elastic balance maintains functional residual capacity.
When the system cannot do enoughWeakness, sedation, neurologic impairment, obstruction, or lung disease may prevent the patient from initiating, sustaining, or safely completing an effective breath. Mechanical support must assist or replace the missing work.
TriggerWhat starts inspiration? Patient effort or elapsed time can initiate the breath.
ControlWhich variable is targeted throughout inspiration? The major comparison is volume versus pressure.
LimitWhat maximum can be reached during inspiration without normally ending it? Pressure, flow, or volume may be limited.
CycleWhat ends inspiration? Volume, time, or declining flow may cycle the breath.
BaselineWhat pressure remains during expiration? PEEP, EPAP, or CPAP establishes the baseline.
A patient-triggered breath is not automatically a spontaneous breath. In assist-control, patient effort can trigger a full mandatory breath. Classify the breath by how it is delivered, not only by who initiated it.

2 · Read noninvasive pressures

CPAP Has One Level; Bilevel Adds a Pressure Difference

Picture the patient

One pressure platform versus an assisted rise

CPAP 8 cm H₂OThe awake patient still starts and powers every breath. The device keeps the airway on an 8 cm H₂O pressure platform through inspiration and expiration, but it does not add a separate inspiratory boost.
Bilevel 14/6 cm H₂OThe patient's effort triggers the rise from EPAP 6 to IPAP 14. EPAP establishes the expiratory baseline, while the 8 cm H₂O difference assists inspiration.
CPAPThe same continuous pressure is present during spontaneous inspiration and expiration. It can improve recruitment, functional residual capacity, upper-airway patency, and oxygenation, but it does not create separate inspiratory assistance.
Bilevel NIVIPAP is applied during inspiration and EPAP during expiration. EPAP supports baseline recruitment; the difference between IPAP and EPAP provides pressure support for inspiration.
Pressure support = IPAP − EPAP
Example: Bilevel 14/6 provides IPAP 14, EPAP 6, and pressure support 8 cm H₂O. Raising IPAP to 18 while EPAP remains 6 increases pressure support to 12. Raising both to 18/10 keeps pressure support at 8 while increasing the expiratory baseline.
Device language matters. A bilevel device may display IPAP as the total inspiratory pressure. An invasive ventilator may display inspiratory pressure above PEEP. Verify the convention before comparing numbers.

3 · Target volume

Volume Control Targets Tidal Volume; Pressure Responds

Bring it forward · quick mechanics reminder

Compliance and resistance affect the pressure–volume relationship

Compliance: how easily the respiratory system stretchesDecreased compliance means the lungs or chest wall are stiffer. More pressure is required to produce the same tidal volume. Pulmonary edema, atelectasis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and a stiff chest wall can decrease compliance.
Resistance: opposition to airflowIncreased resistance makes it harder to move flow through the airways, artificial airway, or circuit. Bronchospasm, secretions, a narrowed or kinked tube, and high flow demand can increase the pressure needed to move the same flow.

Connect the reminder to the mode: in volume control, changing mechanics appears primarily as a change in required pressure. In pressure control, changing mechanics can alter flow and the delivered tidal volume.

Picture the patient

The ventilator aims for the same volume while pressure adapts

A sedated patient is receiving assist-control volume control with a target tidal volume of 420 mL. When the lungs become stiffer, the ventilator still attempts to deliver 420 mL, but it must generate more pressure. The patient's chest may rise by a similar amount even though the pressure required has changed.

In volume-control ventilation, the ventilator attempts to deliver the selected tidal volume using the chosen flow and timing. The pressure required to do that changes with respiratory-system mechanics, flow, patient effort, and circuit conditions.

TargetedSet tidal volume and the programmed flow delivery.
Expected to varyPeak airway pressure and, when mechanics change, the pressure required to deliver the target volume.
Must be monitoredExhaled tidal volume, peak and plateau pressures when available, synchrony, and patient response.
Volume targeting does not guarantee delivery under every condition. A pressure limit, leak, disconnection, or other interruption can prevent the full set volume from reaching the patient. Compare set and exhaled values.

4 · Target pressure

Pressure Control Targets Inspiratory Pressure; Volume Responds

Picture the patient

The same pressure can produce a different-sized breath

At the same pressure target and inspiratory time, a patient may receive 440 mL while compliance is better but only 330 mL after the respiratory system becomes stiffer. The ventilator delivers the selected pressure pattern; the resulting chest rise and tidal volume change with mechanics and effort.

In pressure-control ventilation, the ventilator raises pressure to a selected target for the selected inspiratory time. Flow is commonly decelerating. Tidal volume is a result—not a directly set guarantee.

TargetedInspiratory pressure for a selected inspiratory time.
Expected to varyDelivered flow, tidal volume, and minute ventilation as mechanics or effort change.
Must be monitoredExhaled tidal volume, minute ventilation, pressure, timing, synchrony, and patient response.
Pressure difference still matters: the driving pressure available for inflation depends on inspiratory pressure relative to PEEP. Confirm whether the ventilator displays pressure above PEEP or total inspiratory pressure.
A lower displayed peak pressure does not guarantee lung protection. If tidal volume becomes excessive because mechanics or effort improve, overdistention can still occur. Monitor delivered volume in every pressure-targeted breath.

5 · Assist spontaneous inspiration

Pressure Support Helps a Patient-Triggered Breath

Picture the patient

The patient sets the rhythm; the ventilator adds assistance

An awake patient initiates each breath and decides when to breathe again. Pressure support acts like power steering: the patient still directs the breath, while the ventilator supplies pressure to reduce the muscular work needed to draw in air. A stronger effort or improved compliance can produce a larger tidal volume even when the support setting is unchanged.

In pressure-support ventilation, the patient initiates inspiration, the ventilator raises pressure above PEEP, and inspiration usually ends when inspiratory flow falls to a cycling threshold. The patient controls respiratory timing and strongly influences tidal volume.

TriggerPatient effort detected by flow or pressure.
ControlPressure targeted above the baseline.
CycleUsually flow cycled as inspiratory flow declines.
Variable resultTidal volume changes with support, effort, mechanics, and timing.
Increasing pressure support commonly: increases inspiratory assistance and tidal volume, reduces patient work, and may lower respiratory rate. It does not guarantee a fixed tidal volume or mandatory rate.
Pressure support requires a breathing patient. A backup mode such as spontaneous/timed bilevel may add timed breaths, but pure spontaneous pressure support does not replace mandatory ventilation for apnea.
6 · Detect patient effort

Trigger Sensitivity Must Balance Effort and Stability

Picture the patient

The ventilator must recognize a real attempt to inhale

Too insensitiveThe patient's abdomen and chest show an inspiratory effort, but no supported breath follows because the effort does not cross the trigger threshold.
Too sensitiveA leak or moving condensate crosses the threshold, so the ventilator delivers breaths the patient did not request.
Trigger methodWhat the ventilator detectsToo insensitiveToo sensitive
Pressure triggerA patient-created fall in circuit pressureVisible effort without a delivered breath; increased trigger workUnintended breaths from small pressure disturbances
Flow triggerA change in bias or circuit flowPatient flow change does not reach the thresholdLeak, water, motion, or cardiac oscillation may autotrigger
Sensitivity numbers do not move in the same direction on every control. For a pressure threshold, −1 cm H₂O is generally more sensitive than −2. For many flow thresholds, a smaller required change is more sensitive. Interpret the device label and observed response.

7 · Match delivery to the goal

Select the Mode by What the Patient Needs the Ventilator to Guarantee

Match the breath to the patient

Four patients can need four different delivery patterns

Deeply sedated with a volume goalAssist-control volume control can provide full breaths while targeting the selected tidal volume.
Needs a pressure-targeted mandatory patternAssist-control pressure control can provide full breaths while the clinician monitors the resulting tidal volume.
Awake and ready to do part of the workPressure support can assist patient-triggered breaths without imposing the full controlled pattern.
Breathing independently but needing recruitmentCPAP can provide continuous distending pressure without a separate inspiratory assist.
Immediate breath-delivery goalMode that can matchCritical monitoring
Full mandatory support with a selected tidal-volume targetAssist-control volume controlAirway pressures, exhaled volume, synchrony, and response
Full mandatory support with a selected inspiratory-pressure targetAssist-control pressure controlExhaled tidal volume, minute ventilation, timing, and response
Patient-triggered partial assistance above PEEPPressure supportTidal volume, rate, effort, triggering, and apnea risk
Continuous distending pressure without inspiratory assistanceCPAPOxygenation, effort, hemodynamics, and continued spontaneous breathing
Assist-control does not mean every breath is time triggered. A patient can trigger a full controlled breath; if the patient does not trigger within the set interval, the ventilator provides a time-triggered breath.

8 · Carry-forward framework

Describe the Breath Before You Judge the Mode

Relatable clinical transition

The same patient may need different breath patterns as recovery progresses

Early in respiratory failure, the patient may need full mandatory support. As sedation decreases and respiratory muscle function improves, the patient may begin triggering controlled breaths and later perform more of the work with pressure support. The mode changes because the patient's capability and support goal change—not because one mode is inherently the best choice for every phase.

1 · Who starts it?Patient trigger or time trigger?
2 · What is targeted?Volume, pressure, or continuous baseline pressure?
3 · What can vary?Pressure in volume control; tidal volume in pressure-targeted support.
4 · What must be monitored?Delivered volume, pressures, timing, effort, gas exchange, and response.
Core reasoning: select the mode from the support goal, then monitor the variable the mode does not guarantee. A mode name is only useful when you can predict what the patient will actually receive.
