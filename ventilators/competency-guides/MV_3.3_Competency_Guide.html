C1: Determine extubation readiness after successful liberation.
Aligned competencies: 3.4.1, 3.4.2

Extubation adds an airway test to the ventilatory test

Extubation domainQuestions to answer
Airway protectionIs mental status adequate? Can the patient manage saliva and reduce aspiration risk?
Cough/clearanceIs cough effective? Are secretions manageable in amount and frequency?
Airway patencyIs upper-airway edema or obstruction risk clinically important?
Post-extubation reserveWhat oxygen or noninvasive support is likely to be needed?
Reintubation riskWould failure be difficult or dangerous because of anatomy or disease?

A successful SBT establishes that the patient can sustain spontaneous breathing under the test condition. It does not establish that the tube can be safely removed. A weak cough, heavy secretions, obtundation, or upper-airway obstruction risk can justify delaying extubation while the airway problem is addressed.

What you are expected to know

  • Separate liberation from extubation.
  • Assess mental status, cough, secretion burden, and patency.
  • Recognize when airway-clearance limitations outweigh SBT success.
  • Plan for difficult reintubation risk before removing the tube.
C2: Use cuff-leak testing and preventive post-extubation support selectively.
Aligned competencies: 3.4.3, 3.4.4, 3.4.5, 3.4.6

Cuff-leak testing is for selected high-risk patients

A cuff-leak test estimates the risk of clinically important upper-airway edema; it is not required for every patient. Use it in patients who otherwise meet extubation criteria and are considered at high risk for post-extubation stridor. A failed test is a risk signal, not a permanent prohibition.

Failed cuff-leak test

For an otherwise ready adult who fails the test, guidelines support systemic steroids before extubation according to protocol, commonly at least several hours beforehand. A repeated cuff-leak test is not necessarily required.

Match post-extubation support to risk

Patients at high risk of extubation failure—such as selected patients with COPD, hypercapnia, heart failure, or serious comorbidity—may benefit from immediate preventive NIV after extubation. This is planned support, not rescue delayed until severe failure develops.

What you are expected to know

  • Select high-risk patients for cuff-leak assessment.
  • Interpret a failed test as modifiable risk rather than permanent failure.
  • Recognize the role of pre-extubation systemic steroids.
  • Identify patients appropriate for immediate preventive NIV and close monitoring.
C3: Monitor and respond after extubation.
Aligned competencies: 3.4.6

The first hours test both breathing and airway function

MonitorConcerning change
Upper airwayStridor, retractions, voice change, reduced air movement.
BreathingIncreasing rate, accessory use, fatigue, paradox, falling VT.
Gas exchangeRising oxygen requirement, hypercapnia, acidemia, declining mental status.
ClearanceWeak cough, retained secretions, frequent suction needs.
HemodynamicsTachycardia, dysrhythmia, hypertension, hypotension, ischemia.

Provide oxygen, humidification, airway clearance, high-flow therapy, or preventive NIV according to the patient plan. Escalate promptly when upper-airway obstruction or respiratory failure develops. Delaying reintubation in progressive failure can increase harm; support decisions should follow the mechanism and local protocol.

What you are expected to know

  • Recognize signs of post-extubation airway obstruction and respiratory failure.
  • Monitor secretion clearance and mental status in addition to SpO2.
  • Implement the planned oxygen or noninvasive support.
  • Escalate promptly when support is failing.
C4: Plan a safe transport and preserve ventilator support.
Aligned competencies: 3.4.7, 3.4.8, 3.4.10, 3.4.13, 3.4.14

Transport starts with a risk-benefit and capability assessment

Confirm that the destination procedure is necessary and that ventilation, oxygenation, hemodynamics, airway control, monitoring, medications, and staffing can be maintained throughout preparation, movement, destination time, and return.

Use the planned transport ventilator before departure

Match the ICU support as closely as the transport device permits, including VT or pressure, rate, PEEP, FiO2, timing, trigger, and alarms. Place the patient on the device while still in the controlled care area and verify exhaled VT, pressures, oxygenation, synchrony, and hemodynamics.

Predeparture confirmationWhy it matters
Airway depth and securementReduces accidental extubation and mainstem movement.
Circuit and line securementPrevents disconnection or loss of critical infusions.
Destination and route readinessReduces avoidable delay.
Team roles and closed-loop communicationClarifies who manages airway, ventilator, medications, and movement.

What you are expected to know

  • Evaluate whether transport benefit justifies risk.
  • Reproduce essential ventilator support and alarms.
  • Trial the transport ventilator before movement.
  • Secure airway, circuit, lines, and team communication.
C5: Prepare oxygen, power, monitoring, alarms, and emergency backup.
Aligned competencies: 3.4.9, 3.4.11, 3.4.12, 3.4.15

Cylinder duration

Minutes = (starting psi - reserve psi) x tank factor / flow
Use the actual device-consumption method required by local policy when ventilator demand is not represented by a simple continuous-flow calculation.

Respond to acute transport deterioration

If the transport ventilator cannot deliver an adequate breath or the patient rapidly deteriorates, manually ventilate with oxygen while assessing airway placement and patency, circuit connections, oxygen supply, and device function. Stable SpO2 does not exclude early ventilation failure; loss of capnography or exhaled VT requires immediate evaluation.

What you are expected to know

  • Calculate cylinder duration while preserving a reserve.
  • Verify oxygen and battery capacity for the entire transport.
  • Maintain ventilator alarms and appropriate physiologic monitoring, including CO2.
  • Use manual ventilation immediately when device or airway failure prevents adequate support.
End-of-reading check: You should be able to decide whether extubation is safe, plan support after tube removal, and transport a ventilated patient while preserving ventilation, oxygenation, monitoring, airway security, and emergency backup.
