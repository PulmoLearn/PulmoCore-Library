C1: Recognize the respiratory problem and the need for support.
Aligned competencies: 1.1.1, 1.1.2, 1.1.3

Begin with the physiologic failure

Before choosing a device, decide what the patient is failing to do. The central distinction is whether the patient cannot oxygenate, cannot ventilate, cannot protect the airway, or cannot sustain the work of breathing. A patient may have more than one problem, but identifying the dominant failure keeps the first intervention focused.

ProblemImportant evidenceWhat the evidence means
Oxygenation failurePaO2, SpO2, current FiO2, PEEP or CPAP, clinical perfusionOxygen is not moving adequately from the alveoli into the blood.
Ventilatory failurePaCO2, pH, VT, minute ventilation, respiratory pattern, fatigueEffective alveolar ventilation is insufficient to remove carbon dioxide.
Airway failureMental status, cough, gag, secretion control, vomiting or obstructionThe patient may be unable to maintain or protect the airway.
Unsustainable effortAccessory-muscle use, paradoxical breathing, declining VT, fatigueThe patient may be maintaining gas exchange temporarily at an unsafe physiologic cost.

Oxygenation and ventilation are related but not interchangeable

A falling PaO2 or SpO2 primarily identifies an oxygenation problem. A rising PaCO2 with a falling pH identifies worsening acute ventilatory failure. Supplemental oxygen can improve PaO2 without correcting carbon dioxide retention. Similarly, a normal oxygen saturation does not prove that ventilation or airway protection is adequate.

Acute versus chronic hypercapnia

Interpret PaCO2 with pH, bicarbonate, previous results, and the clinical trajectory. A stable patient with chronic compensated hypercapnia is different from a patient whose PaCO2 is rapidly rising while pH and mental status deteriorate.

When invasive ventilation becomes necessary

Invasive ventilation is considered when the patient cannot protect the airway, cannot oxygenate or ventilate despite appropriate support, has apnea or respiratory arrest, is becoming exhausted, or is deteriorating too rapidly for noninvasive support. No single pH, PaCO2, respiratory rate, or oxygen saturation should be treated as an automatic intubation threshold.

What you are expected to know

  • Classify a case as oxygenation failure, ventilatory failure, airway failure, excessive work of breathing, or a combination.
  • Use PaCO2 and pH together to recognize acute ventilatory failure.
  • Recognize airway-protection and secretion-clearance findings that support invasive ventilation.
  • Explain why tachypnea or one abnormal ABG value does not independently determine the airway plan.
C2: Select the appropriate level of respiratory support.
Aligned competencies: 1.1.4

Match the device to the job

Respiratory support is a continuum. The best device is the least invasive option that can safely accomplish the required physiologic job. Device selection should not be based on diagnosis alone.

SupportWhat it providesImportant limitation
Supplemental oxygenHigher inspired oxygen concentrationDoes not provide breaths, pressure support, or airway protection.
CPAPOne continuous positive pressure during spontaneous breathingSupports recruitment and oxygenation but does not add inspiratory pressure support.
Bilevel NIVIPAP, EPAP, and a pressure difference that assists inspirationRequires spontaneous breathing, airway protection, cooperation, and secretion control.
Bag-mask ventilationTemporary manual positive-pressure breaths with oxygenA short-term bridge, not a long-term support plan or definitive airway.
Invasive ventilationMode-dependent support through an artificial airwayRequires airway placement and carries invasive risks.

Use the primary problem to narrow the choice

Identify failure→Determine airway safety→Match device capability→Begin support→Reassess response

A patient with pulmonary edema, adequate ventilation, and intact airway protection may benefit from CPAP. A patient with a COPD exacerbation and acute hypercapnic failure may need bilevel support because the inspiratory-to-expiratory pressure difference assists ventilation. An apneic patient requires immediate breaths, making bag-mask ventilation the bridge while a definitive airway is prepared.

Do not confuse oxygen delivery with ventilation

A nonrebreather mask may deliver a high oxygen concentration, but it cannot ventilate an apneic patient or provide inspiratory assistance to a patient with inadequate alveolar ventilation.

What you are expected to know

  • State what oxygen, CPAP, bilevel NIV, bag-mask ventilation, and invasive ventilation can and cannot provide.
  • Choose support based on the required physiologic effect rather than the diagnosis label.
  • Recognize when a temporary bridge is needed while definitive airway support is prepared.
C3: Determine NIV candidacy and recognize success or failure.
Aligned competencies: 1.1.5, 1.1.6, 1.1.7

NIV requires more than an appropriate diagnosis

Noninvasive ventilation is most likely to succeed when the patient is breathing spontaneously, can cooperate, protects the airway, clears secretions, tolerates the interface, and has a reversible problem that can be monitored closely. COPD exacerbation and cardiogenic pulmonary edema are common applications, but the patient must still be safe for mask-based support.

Contraindications and major limitations

  • Respiratory or cardiac arrest
  • Inability to protect the airway
  • Active vomiting or major aspiration risk
  • Copious or unmanageable secretions
  • Severe impairment of consciousness or inability to cooperate
  • Facial injury or inability to fit the interface
  • Severe instability or an immediate need for intubation

Some findings are relative rather than absolute. Moderate acidemia, hypercapnia, immunocompromised status, or severe hypoxemia may still be compatible with NIV when airway protection is intact and the patient can be closely monitored.

Response suggesting successResponse suggesting failure
Respiratory rate and accessory-muscle use decreaseRespiratory distress or fatigue worsens
pH improves and PaCO2 moves toward the goalpH falls and PaCO2 continues to rise
Oxygenation improves on reasonable supportSevere hypoxemia persists despite optimized support
Mental status remains stable or improvesMental status declines
Secretions remain manageableSecretions accumulate or cough becomes ineffective

Setting problem or loss of NIV suitability?

A pressure adjustment may be reasonable when the patient remains cooperative and airway protection is intact. Worsening consciousness, uncontrolled secretions, or rapid global deterioration is not merely a setting problem; it signals that NIV may no longer be safe.

What you are expected to know

  • Identify the features of a good NIV candidate.
  • Recognize contraindications and findings that make mask-based support unsafe.
  • Use changes in work of breathing, gas exchange, mental status, and secretion control to judge response.
  • Distinguish an adjustable NIV problem from NIV failure requiring escalation.
C4: Provide effective bag-mask ventilation and evaluate airway clearance.
Aligned competencies: 1.1.8, 1.1.9, 1.1.10

Bag-mask ventilation is an immediate bridge

Bag-mask ventilation provides temporary manual breaths during apnea, respiratory arrest, airway preparation, loss of a ventilator connection, or another brief period in which the patient cannot ventilate effectively. The goal is visible chest rise with the least pressure and volume needed to support ventilation.

Correct ineffective ventilation systematically

Open and position airway→Place appropriate adjunct→Reposition mask→Improve seal→Use two-person technique→Evaluate obstruction

Minimal chest rise with an audible mask leak points toward airway position or seal. Increasing oxygen flow or squeezing the bag faster does not correct a leak. With a two-person technique, one clinician uses both hands to position the jaw and seal the mask while the second clinician compresses the bag and evaluates chest rise.

Neuromuscular weakness creates two separate problems

Neuromuscular disease may reduce inspiratory strength, expiratory strength, cough, swallowing, and airway protection. NIV can assist inspiration, but it does not restore an effective cough or remove pooled secretions. A patient can temporarily have acceptable gas exchange while losing the ability to clear and protect the airway.

FindingClinical meaning
Low VT or rising PaCO2 with intact coughVentilatory assistance may be needed.
Weak cough, pooling secretions, repeated aspirationAirway clearance and protection are becoming unsafe.
Bulbar dysfunction or inability to swallowMask support may not provide a safe airway plan.

What you are expected to know

  • Describe when bag-mask ventilation is indicated and why it is temporary.
  • Recognize a mask leak and select airway-position, adjunct, seal, or two-person corrections.
  • Separate ventilatory muscle weakness from cough and airway-protection failure.
  • Explain why acceptable gas exchange does not guarantee safe NIV in progressive neuromuscular weakness.
C5: Apply goals of care to respiratory support.
Aligned competencies: 1.1.11

Do-not-intubate does not mean do-not-treat

A do-not-intubate order establishes that endotracheal intubation is outside the treatment plan. It does not automatically prohibit oxygen, medications, secretion management, CPAP, or bilevel NIV. The appropriate ceiling of support should be based on the patient informed preferences and the intended purpose of treatment.

Possible NIV purposeWhat must be clear
Active treatment within a ceiling of careThe patient accepts NIV but does not want intubation if NIV fails.
Time-limited trial for a reversible processThe team defines the desired response, reassessment period, and next step.
Symptom-focused supportComfort is the primary goal and burdens of the mask are weighed against relief.

Clinical communication

Clarify whether NIV is being used to reverse a condition, gain time, or relieve dyspnea. If NIV fails and intubation is not consistent with the patient goals, the plan should shift according to the established ceiling of care rather than continuing burdensome support without a goal.

What you are expected to know

  • Explain the difference between DNI, comfort-only care, and a treatment ceiling.
  • Determine whether NIV fits the stated goals rather than assuming it is automatically allowed or prohibited.
  • Describe what should be established before beginning a time-limited NIV trial.
End-of-reading check: You should be able to classify the primary respiratory failure, select a support level, determine NIV suitability, provide an effective bag-mask bridge, and apply the patient goals of care.
