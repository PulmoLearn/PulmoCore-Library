C1: Interpret pressure-time, flow-time, and volume-time curves.
Aligned competencies: 2.5.1, 2.5.2, 2.5.3

Curves plot one variable against time

CurvePrimary informationPatterns to recognize
Pressure-timeBaseline pressure, peak or target pressure, effort, holds, timingFlow starvation, trigger effort, rise time, cycling, plateau hold.
Flow-timeDirection and rate of inspiratory/expiratory flowIncomplete exhalation, trigger/cycling mismatch, breath stacking.
Volume-timeInspired and exhaled volume over the breathLeak or incomplete return of exhaled volume.

Read every curve in the same order

  • Confirm the axes and scale.
  • Identify baseline and breath start.
  • Locate patient effort relative to machine delivery.
  • Examine the inspiratory contour and timing.
  • Examine the transition to expiration.
  • Confirm expiratory flow or volume returns to baseline.
  • Compare the current breath with previous breaths and numeric data.

Curves are synchronized evidence

A deflection at the same time on pressure and flow curves can clarify whether the event is patient effort, machine triggering, or a cycling mismatch. Avoid interpreting one curve without the others when they are available.

What you are expected to know

  • State what each time curve plots.
  • Use a consistent sequence to inspect a breath.
  • Recognize incomplete exhalation and volume loss.
  • Correlate events across synchronized curves.
C2: Interpret pressure-volume and flow-volume loops.
Aligned competencies: 2.5.4, 2.5.5

Loops compare one changing variable with another

LoopAxesHigh-value interpretations
Pressure-volumePressure and volumeCompliance slope, lower opening/recruitment pattern, upper beaking/overdistention.
Flow-volumeFlow and volumeObstruction, expiratory-flow limitation, secretion artifact, leak, response to bronchodilation.

Pressure-volume loop distinctions

A steeper slope indicates greater compliance; a flatter slope indicates lower compliance. A lower inflection or abrupt opening pattern can suggest tidal recruitment at low volume. Upper beaking or flattening suggests that additional pressure is producing little volume and overdistention may be occurring.

Flow-volume loop distinctions

A scooped expiratory limb is consistent with obstruction. A sawtooth contour can occur with secretions or condensation. The loop must be interpreted with breath sounds, peak and plateau pressure, suction findings, and the display scale.

What you are expected to know

  • Differentiate a curve from a loop.
  • Recognize compliance, recruitment, and overdistention patterns on a pressure-volume loop.
  • Recognize obstruction and secretion patterns on a flow-volume loop.
  • Integrate loop findings with pressures and bedside assessment.
C3: Recognize common graphic signatures and their mechanisms.
Aligned competencies: 2.5.6, 2.5.7, 2.5.8, 2.5.9, 2.5.10, 2.5.11

Translate the pattern into a physiologic category

Graphic patternLikely mechanismCorroborating evidence
Scooped pressure during VC inspirationFlow demand exceeds set flowStrong inspiratory effort; improvement when flow matches demand.
Expiratory flow does not reach zeroIncomplete exhalation / auto-PEEPExpiratory hold, obstructive mechanics, short expiratory time.
Upper pressure-volume beakingOverdistentionHigh VT or pressure, rising plateau, falling compliance.
Lower-loop opening patternTidal recruitment at insufficient end-expiratory pressureHypoxemia, recruitable disease, response to cautious PEEP.
Volume-time curve fails to return to zeroLeakInspired volume greater than exhaled, low pressure, audible leak.
Sawtooth flow-volume contourSecretions or circuit condensationRhonchi, visible secretions, rising resistance.

Avoid pattern matching without confirmation

The same visual abnormality may have more than one cause. Verify the axis scale, compare multiple breaths, inspect the circuit and artificial airway, listen to the patient, and compare peak with plateau pressure. A graphic interpretation is strongest when at least two independent findings support it.

What you are expected to know

  • Identify the major Unit 2 waveform and loop patterns.
  • Name the mechanism rather than merely describing the shape.
  • Select corroborating bedside or numeric evidence.
  • Choose a correction that directly addresses the confirmed mechanism.
C4: Differentiate resistance, compliance, and display-scaling changes.
Aligned competencies: 2.5.12, 2.5.13, 2.5.14, 2.5.15

Peak and plateau pressure localize the mechanical load

Pressure patternMost likely categoryExamples
Peak rises; plateau stableIncreased resistanceSecretions, bronchospasm, kink, biting, high flow.
Peak and plateau riseReduced compliance or larger elastic loadARDS, edema, atelectasis, pneumothorax, overdistention, abdominal pressure.
Apparent waveform height changes; numeric values stableDisplay scale changedAutomatic rescaling or manual axis change.

Pressure alarms are signals, not diagnoses

A high-pressure alarm may arise from coughing, obstruction, secretions, bronchospasm, reduced compliance, or circuit problems. A low-pressure alarm may arise from disconnection, leak, cuff failure, or inadequate pressure generation. Use the alarm with exhaled VT, auscultation, graphics, holds, and circuit inspection.

Check the scale before declaring a new waveform abnormality

If the waveform suddenly appears taller or flatter while peak pressure, plateau pressure, VT, and settings are unchanged, confirm the vertical and horizontal scale. Visual size alone is not a physiologic measurement.

What you are expected to know

  • Separate resistance from compliance using peak and plateau pressure.
  • Generate causes within each category.
  • Recognize display scaling as a nonphysiologic source of visual change.
  • Use alarm type and measured values to guide the next assessment.
C5: Use a systematic alarm response and select a mechanism-specific correction.
Aligned competencies: 2.5.16, 2.5.17, 2.5.18

First determine urgency

If the patient is rapidly desaturating, receiving inadequate VT, becoming unstable, or cannot be ventilated through the ventilator, disconnect and provide manual ventilation with oxygen while evaluating the airway and circuit. This supports the patient and helps determine whether the ventilator/circuit or the patient/airway is responsible.

Use categories to prevent premature closure

CategoryExamplesTargeted response
PatientCoughing, bronchospasm, pneumothorax, worsening complianceTreat the identified physiology.
Artificial airwaySecretions, kink, biting, cuff leak, displacementSuction, reposition, restore patency or seal.
CircuitDisconnection, leak, water, occlusionReconnect, drain, replace, or repair.
VentilatorValve or sensor malfunctionManual support and equipment replacement/service.
SettingsInappropriate pressure limit, flow, timing, trigger, or alarm thresholdCorrect the setting after verifying patient safety.

Do not silence the distinction

Raising an alarm limit without identifying the cause can hide a clinically important change. Mode changes also do not treat a mucus plug, cuff leak, disconnection, or pneumothorax. Stabilize, localize, correct, and verify the response.

What you are expected to know

  • Identify when immediate manual ventilation is required.
  • Classify alarm causes as patient, airway, circuit, ventilator, or setting.
  • Select suctioning, seal repair, reconnection, setting correction, or emergency support based on evidence.
  • Verify that VT, pressure, oxygenation, and the alarm condition improve after intervention.
End-of-reading check: You should be able to interpret curves and loops, identify resistance, compliance, leak, and timing patterns, and use a systematic response to ventilator alarms.
