C1: Differentiate CPAP and bilevel support and match each to the problem.
Aligned competencies: 1.2.1, 1.2.2

CPAP provides one continuous pressure

Continuous positive airway pressure maintains the same baseline pressure during spontaneous inspiration and expiration. It supports alveolar recruitment, functional residual capacity, upper-airway patency, and oxygenation. Because there is no higher inspiratory pressure, CPAP does not add pressure support in the way bilevel ventilation does.

Bilevel support provides two pressure levels

Bilevel ventilation uses IPAP during inspiration and EPAP during expiration. The difference between them is pressure support. EPAP supports recruitment and oxygenation, while the pressure difference assists inspiration and may improve VT, minute ventilation, PaCO2, and work of breathing.

Primary needMore likely supportReason
Recruitment and oxygenation with adequate ventilationCPAPContinuous distending pressure may be sufficient.
Hypercapnia and increased work of breathingBilevel NIVThe IPAP-EPAP difference provides inspiratory assistance.
Both oxygenation and ventilation supportBilevel NIVEPAP and FiO2 address oxygenation; pressure support assists ventilation.
Apnea or loss of airway protectionNeither as definitive supportThe patient requires immediate ventilation and airway management.

A distinction, not an absolute rule

Bilevel ventilation can be used for cardiogenic pulmonary edema, particularly when ventilatory assistance is also needed. The competency is to match added inspiratory support to a clinical need, not to memorize one device for every diagnosis.

What you are expected to know

  • Describe the pressure pattern produced by CPAP and bilevel ventilation.
  • Explain the roles of EPAP and the IPAP-EPAP pressure difference.
  • Match support to oxygenation, ventilation, combined failure, or apnea.
C2: Explain volume-control breath delivery.
Aligned competencies: 1.2.3

Volume control targets tidal volume

In volume-control ventilation, the ventilator attempts to deliver the set VT using the selected flow pattern and timing. Airway pressure is the variable that changes when resistance, compliance, flow, or patient effort changes.

If this changesExpected volume-control response
Compliance decreasesMore pressure is required to deliver the same target VT.
Airway resistance increasesPeak pressure increases as flow meets greater resistance.
Inspiratory flow increasesInspiratory time becomes shorter for the same set VT.
Pressure limit is reachedThe ventilator may stop delivery before the full VT enters.

Volume control provides consistency of the target VT, but it does not guarantee a safe pressure. The clinician must monitor peak pressure, plateau pressure when measured, exhaled VT, and patient response.

Control variable versus monitored variable

Controlled or targeted: volume. Expected to vary: pressure. A change in pressure is information about mechanics, flow, effort, or circuit conditions; it is not automatically a reason to abandon volume control.

What you are expected to know

  • Identify the controlled variable in volume control.
  • Predict how decreasing compliance or increasing resistance affects airway pressure.
  • Explain why exhaled VT and pressures must still be monitored.
C3: Explain pressure-control breath delivery and compare it with volume control.
Aligned competencies: 1.2.4, 1.2.5

Pressure control targets inspiratory pressure

In pressure-control ventilation, the ventilator raises airway pressure to the selected target for the selected inspiratory time. Delivered flow is usually decelerating. VT is not set directly; it changes with the pressure difference, compliance, resistance, inspiratory time, patient effort, and PEEP.

Change while pressure remains fixedExpected effect on exhaled VT
Compliance decreasesVT generally decreases.
Airway resistance increasesVolume delivery slows and VT may decrease during the available inspiratory time.
Inspiratory time increasesVT may increase until flow and pressure equilibrate.
Patient inspiratory effort increasesVT may increase.

Compare what each mode guarantees

FeatureVolume controlPressure control
Primary targetTidal volumeInspiratory pressure
Variable affected most by changing mechanicsAirway pressureTidal volume
Main monitoring priorityPressure plus exhaled volumeExhaled volume plus pressure
Clinical strengthConsistent target VTPressure-targeted breath delivery
Important limitationPressure can riseVT and VE can fall

Peak pressure is not a mode-selection shortcut

Pressure control is a clinical tool, not an automatic response to an isolated peak-pressure threshold. Before changing modes, determine whether the pressure reflects resistance, compliance, flow, coughing, secretions, or another cause.

What you are expected to know

  • Identify the controlled variable in pressure control.
  • Predict how compliance, resistance, time, and effort affect VT.
  • Compare which variable is targeted and which variable changes in volume and pressure control.
  • Explain why pressure control does not eliminate the need to monitor VT.
C4: Explain pressure support and basic patient triggering.
Aligned competencies: 1.2.6, 1.2.7

Pressure support assists spontaneous breaths

During pressure-support ventilation, the patient initiates the breath. The ventilator provides the selected pressure above PEEP and delivers flow to meet demand. The breath typically ends when inspiratory flow falls to a cycling threshold. VT varies with pressure support, effort, compliance, resistance, and inspiratory time.

Increasing pressure support commonly increases VT and reduces inspiratory muscle work, but it does not directly increase FiO2, PEEP, or guarantee a fixed VT. A backup rate or separate mandatory feature is needed when a minimum breath rate must be assured.

Trigger sensitivity determines the effort needed to begin a breath

Trigger problemWhat the patient or ventilator may show
Too insensitiveVisible effort without a supported breath, missed efforts, increased work.
Too sensitiveBreaths triggered by leak, water, circuit movement, or cardiac oscillation.
AppropriateReasonable effort reliably initiates a supported breath without auto-triggering.

Unit 1 focuses on the basic relationship between sensitivity and work. Unit 2 will use pressure-time and flow-time waveforms to distinguish missed efforts, auto-triggering, cycling problems, and flow mismatch.

What you are expected to know

  • Describe who initiates and cycles a pressure-supported breath.
  • Predict the general effect of increasing pressure support.
  • Recognize missed efforts caused by an insensitive trigger and unintended breaths caused by an overly sensitive trigger.
C5: Select an initial mode based on the breath-delivery goal.
Aligned competencies: 1.2.8

Mode selection begins with the support requirement

Ask whether the patient needs full mandatory support, partial assistance, or continuous distending pressure during spontaneous breathing. Then decide whether direct control of VT or pressure-targeted delivery is the more important starting feature.

Clinical goalMode capability that matches
Consistent prescribed VT with full mandatory supportAssist-control volume control
Full mandatory support with pressure-targeted breathsAssist-control pressure control
Spontaneous breathing with added inspiratory assistancePressure support
Spontaneous breathing with continuous distending pressureCPAP

No mode removes the need for reassessment

  • Volume control requires pressure monitoring.
  • Pressure control requires exhaled VT and VE monitoring.
  • Pressure support requires spontaneous effort and monitoring of VT, rate, and work.
  • CPAP requires spontaneous ventilation and monitoring for fatigue or failure.

Clinical reasoning sequence

Define required support→Choose breath target→Set safe starting values→Observe delivered breath→Reassess patient response

What you are expected to know

  • Choose a mode whose capabilities match the required support.
  • Explain why volume control may be selected when consistent VT is the immediate priority.
  • Explain why pressure control remains dependent on delivered VT.
  • Recognize when spontaneous modes do not provide enough mandatory support.
End-of-reading check: You should be able to explain CPAP, bilevel, volume control, pressure control, pressure support, and triggering, then select a mode whose capabilities match the required support.
