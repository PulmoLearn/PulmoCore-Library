C1: Classify the first reassessment and select the correct control family.
Aligned competencies: 1.4.1, 1.4.2

The first reassessment asks two questions

Is ventilation adequate?→Is oxygenation adequate?→Which mode/device is in use?→Which control changes that problem?
ProblemEvidencePrimary controls
VentilationpH, PaCO2, exhaled VT, VE, total rate, effortVT, rate, inspiratory pressure, pressure support, backup rate when applicable
OxygenationPaO2, SpO2, FiO2, PEEP or EPAP, hemodynamicsFiO2 and PEEP or EPAP
Timing or exhalationI:E ratio, rate, flow, incomplete exhalationRate, VT, inspiratory flow, inspiratory time

A low PaO2 is not corrected primarily by increasing respiratory rate. A high PaCO2 is not corrected primarily by increasing FiO2 or PEEP. The intervention should match the physiologic problem and the control available in the current mode.

Interpret the ABG in context

Evaluate pH, PaCO2, and bicarbonate to identify the acid-base problem. Then evaluate PaO2 in relation to current FiO2, PEEP, and the patient oxygenation target. A PaO2 value without the support level does not show how hard the ventilator is working to produce that result.

What you are expected to know

  • Classify an ABG and clinical reassessment as ventilation, oxygenation, both, or another problem.
  • Match VT/rate/pressure controls to ventilation and FiO2/PEEP controls to oxygenation.
  • Interpret oxygenation values in relation to the current support level.
C2: Calculate desired minute ventilation and respiratory rate.
Aligned competencies: 1.4.3, 1.4.4

Use the PaCO2-ventilation relationship

Desired minute ventilation

Desired VE = current PaCO2 x current VE / desired PaCO2

The relationship assumes carbon dioxide production and dead-space fraction remain relatively stable. The result estimates the effective ventilation needed to move PaCO2 toward the selected target.

Convert desired VE into a rate

Desired rate

Desired RR = desired VE / selected safe VT

Use a VT that is appropriate for PBW before solving for rate. Then determine whether that rate is compatible with the patient expiratory needs and total spontaneous rate.

Worked example

Current PaCO2 is 54 mm Hg and current VE is 6.5 L/min. If the patient-specific PaCO2 goal is 45 mm Hg, desired VE is 7.8 L/min. With VT 0.39 L, the calculated rate is 20/min. The rate must still be evaluated for adequate expiration and patient triggering.

What you are expected to know

  • Calculate desired VE with correct formula placement.
  • Select a clinically appropriate desired PaCO2 rather than assuming 40 mm Hg.
  • Calculate desired rate using a safe VT.
  • State the physiologic assumptions and limitations of the calculation.
C3: Make the first mode-specific ventilation adjustment.
Aligned competencies: 1.4.5, 1.4.6, 1.4.7

Volume control: evaluate VT before changing rate

Volume-control findingInitial reasoning
VT below targetIncrease VT toward the PBW-based target when clinically appropriate.
VT appropriate but VE inadequateIncrease rate if sufficient expiratory time is available.
VT excessive and VE inadequateReduce VT to a protective target and coordinate a rate increase.

Reducing an excessive VT and increasing rate are two coordinated setting changes serving one physiologic goal: adequate ventilation with a safer breath size. The rule is one physiologic goal at a time, not one knob regardless of safety.

Pressure control: correct the breath size first

Pressure-control findingInitial reasoning
Exhaled VT below targetIncrease inspiratory pressure in appropriate increments and remeasure VT.
Exhaled VT appropriate but VE inadequateAdjust rate when appropriate.
VT changes after a mechanics changeReevaluate compliance, resistance, effort, pressure, and time.

Mode distinction

Increasing rate may raise VE in pressure control, but it does not correct an inadequate VT produced by each breath. Conversely, changing to pressure control does not solve excessive VT if the new pressure is adjusted to reproduce the same unsafe volume.

What you are expected to know

  • Use PBW to decide whether VT is low, appropriate, or excessive.
  • Increase rate in volume control only after confirming VT is appropriate.
  • Increase inspiratory pressure in pressure control when exhaled VT is below target.
  • Coordinate VT and rate changes when adequate ventilation must be restored without preserving excessive VT.
C4: Individualize PaCO2 goals and choose FiO2 or PEEP for oxygenation.
Aligned competencies: 1.4.8, 1.4.9

A normal PaCO2 is not the goal for every patient

A patient with chronic hypercapnia may have an elevated baseline PaCO2 and renal bicarbonate retention. Aggressively reducing PaCO2 to 40 mm Hg may require excessive rate or VT, worsen air trapping, and produce alkalemia while bicarbonate remains elevated. The initial goal is to improve acute acidemia and move toward the known or likely baseline.

EvidenceHow it affects the goal
Known baseline PaCO2Provides a patient-specific target.
Elevated bicarbonateSuggests chronic renal compensation.
Worsening pH or mental statusIndicates acute ventilation still requires treatment.
Severe obstructionLimits how aggressively rate can be increased.

FiO2 and PEEP improve oxygenation differently

ControlPrimary effectImportant tradeoff
FiO2Raises inspired oxygen concentration rapidlyProlonged excessive oxygen exposure should be reduced when possible.
PEEPMaintains end-expiratory pressure and may recruit alveoliCan increase intrathoracic pressure and affect venous return and blood pressure.

When FiO2 is already high, PEEP is low, and recruitable hypoxemia persists, increasing PEEP may be the more targeted next step. During immediate severe hypoxemia, a rapid FiO2 increase may still be needed. Current settings, diagnosis, recruitability, pressure, and hemodynamics determine the choice.

What you are expected to know

  • Select a PaCO2 target using baseline, pH, bicarbonate, diagnosis, and air-trapping risk.
  • Explain the danger of forcing every patient to PaCO2 40 mm Hg.
  • Distinguish the effects and risks of FiO2 and PEEP.
  • Select the more appropriate oxygenation control from the current settings and patient physiology.
C5: Predict and evaluate the response to an adjustment.
Aligned competencies: 1.4.10, 1.4.11

Prediction turns a setting change into a testable plan

Before making an adjustment, state what should change. A ventilation adjustment should produce an expected direction and approximate magnitude of change in VT, VE, PaCO2, or pH. An oxygenation adjustment should produce an expected change in SpO2, PaO2, oxygen requirement, or recruitment response.

Predict a new PaCO2

Predicted PaCO2 = current PaCO2 x current VE / new VE

If current PaCO2 is 56 mm Hg at VE 6 L/min and new VE is 7.5 L/min, predicted PaCO2 is about 45 mm Hg, assuming dead space and carbon dioxide production are stable.

Reassessment should match the intervention

Change madeWhat to reassess
VT, pressure, support, or rateExhaled VT, total rate, VE, pH, PaCO2, expiratory time, patient effort
FiO2SpO2, PaO2 when indicated, oxygen target, ability to titrate down
PEEP or EPAPOxygenation, blood pressure, recruitment response, pressure, VT
NIV pressure changeVT, leak, comfort, synchrony, work, pH, PaCO2, SpO2

When the measured response differs substantially from the prediction, consider changes in dead space, CO2 production, patient effort, leaks, delivered VT, or the underlying condition. This is how the course moves from formula use to clinical reasoning.

What you are expected to know

  • Predict the direction and approximate size of a PaCO2 response to a VE change.
  • Select reassessment data that directly evaluate the intervention.
  • Recognize reasons a measured result may differ from the calculated prediction.
C6: Use alarms, positioning, and emergency actions safely.
Aligned competencies: 1.4.12, 1.4.13, 1.4.14

Interpret the alarm as a clinical signal

A high-pressure alarm means the ventilator encountered more pressure than the set limit. Causes include secretions, tube obstruction or biting, bronchospasm, coughing, reduced compliance, pneumothorax, circuit obstruction, water, and asynchrony. Raising the limit without identifying the cause removes protection.

Acute deterioration requires immediate support

Recognize deterioration→Assess airway and breathing→Disconnect if needed→Manually ventilate with oxygen→Separate circuit from patient cause→Correct problem

Manual ventilation both supports the patient and provides diagnostic information. Easy manual ventilation suggests a ventilator or circuit problem. Difficult manual ventilation suggests an artificial-airway or patient problem such as obstruction, bronchospasm, reduced compliance, or pneumothorax.

Continue to use positioning as an intervention

Head elevation supports routine airway safety and ventilation when not contraindicated. Position may also be changed for secretion drainage, unilateral disease, recruitment, skin protection, procedures, or prone ventilation in selected severe hypoxemia. Positioning supplements - but does not replace - correction of the underlying ventilator or airway problem.

Limited use of the patient-first rule

This emergency sequence is the deliberate Unit 1 situation in which immediate patient and airway assessment is the tested priority. In most other course questions, sufficient information will be provided so that you can select a specific intervention rather than defaulting to a generic “assess first” response.

What you are expected to know

  • Set a high-pressure alarm relative to stable measured pressure.
  • Identify common patient, airway, and circuit causes of a high-pressure alarm.
  • Describe how manual ventilation separates equipment problems from patient or airway problems.
  • Select routine or selective positioning based on clinical need and contraindications.
End-of-reading check: You should be able to classify ventilation and oxygenation problems, calculate the needed change, adjust settings by mode, individualize targets, predict the response, and respond safely to deterioration.
