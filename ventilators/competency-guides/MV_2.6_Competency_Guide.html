C1: Compare pressure control, inverse-ratio ventilation, and APRV.
Aligned competencies: 2.6.1, 2.6.2, 2.6.3

Pressure-targeted strategies do not guarantee tidal volume

Pressure control targets inspiratory pressure. VT varies with compliance, resistance, inspiratory time, effort, and leak. It is useful when pressure targeting supports the clinical plan, but it is not an automatic response to an isolated high peak pressure.

StrategyDefining featureMajor monitoring need
Pressure controlPressure target with time-cycled mandatory breathsExhaled VT, VE, mechanics, pressure, synchrony.
Inverse-ratio ventilationInspiratory time equals or exceeds expiratory timeAuto-PEEP, hemodynamics, sedation, gas exchange.
APRVAlternates between high and low pressure levels with brief releases; may allow spontaneous breathingRelease VT, total ventilation, spontaneous effort, pressure-time settings.

Longer inspiration is a deliberate tradeoff

Inverse-ratio ventilation and long high-pressure time in APRV can raise mean airway pressure and support recruitment, but shorten expiration and may increase air trapping or hemodynamic compromise. These approaches require protocol familiarity and close monitoring.

What you are expected to know

  • State what is targeted and what varies in pressure control.
  • Describe how inverse timing changes mean pressure and expiratory time.
  • Describe the high-pressure, low-pressure, and release concept of APRV.
  • Identify variable VT, auto-PEEP, and hemodynamics as important safety concerns.
C2: Compare PAV, NAVA, and high-frequency ventilation.
Aligned competencies: 2.6.4, 2.6.5, 2.6.6

Advanced modes may use different signals to determine assistance

StrategyControl conceptClinical implication
Proportional assist ventilation (PAV)Assistance varies in proportion to estimated patient effort and mechanicsRequires spontaneous effort and reliable mechanics estimation.
Neurally adjusted ventilatory assist (NAVA)Uses diaphragmatic electrical activity (EAdi) to trigger and proportion assistanceCan align support with neural timing when the signal and drive are intact.
High-frequency ventilation (HFV)Uses very rapid, small-volume breaths or oscillations depending on deviceSpecialty strategy with device-specific gas-exchange controls and monitoring.

The mode must match the clinical objective

PAV and NAVA are designed to improve proportionality and synchrony in spontaneously breathing patients, but neither replaces airway protection or guarantees an appropriate VT. HFV is not simply conventional ventilation at a higher rate; oxygenation and ventilation controls vary by device and require specialty expertise.

What you are expected to know

  • Match PAV with proportional assistance.
  • Match NAVA with EAdi and neural timing.
  • Describe HFV as rapid small-volume specialty ventilation.
  • Identify the patient drive, signal, and monitoring requirements that limit each strategy.
C3: Match an advanced mode to the objective and anticipate risks.
Aligned competencies: 2.6.7, 2.6.8

Begin with the problem the strategy is meant to solve

Clinical objectiveStrategy category to considerQuestion before use
Pressure-targeted mandatory supportPressure controlCan VT be monitored and retitrated as mechanics change?
Increase mean pressure through timingInverse-ratio approachCan the patient tolerate reduced expiratory time and sedation needs?
Support spontaneous breathing in a pressure-release frameworkAPRVDoes the team understand release settings and VT monitoring?
Proportional assistance with variable demandPAV or NAVAIs respiratory drive present and is the required signal reliable?
Specialty gas exchange with very small breathsHFVIs the device, protocol, and experienced team available?

Advanced does not mean automatically better

A mode should be selected because its operating characteristics address a defined problem. Monitor for excessive or inadequate VT, air trapping, hemodynamic effects, loss of the patient signal, inappropriate effort, and failure to achieve the clinical objective. Return to a safer strategy if the benefit is not demonstrated.

What you are expected to know

  • Match the defining control signal to the mode.
  • Select a mode based on a stated physiologic or synchrony objective.
  • Identify important mode-specific risks and monitoring needs.
  • Recognize when a familiar conventional strategy may be safer or sufficient.
C4: Use prone positioning, selective recruitment, and independent lung ventilation.
Aligned competencies: 2.6.9, 2.6.10, 2.6.11

Rescue strategies address different mechanisms

StrategyBest conceptual fitKey limitation
Prone positioningSevere ARDS with refractory hypoxemia after optimized protective supportRequires trained team, airway/line security, skin protection, and monitoring.
Recruitment maneuverSelected potentially recruitable lung with protocolized risk assessmentTransient benefit; hypotension, barotrauma, or overdistention may occur.
Independent lung ventilationMarkedly asymmetric lungs needing different pressure or timing strategiesComplex airway setup, synchronization, sedation, and specialty staffing.

Independent lung ventilation separates competing goals

When one lung requires low pressure because of a fistula or severe focal injury while the other requires greater PEEP, a single shared pressure strategy may be inadequate. Independent lung ventilation can support each lung differently, but is uncommon and should be managed by an experienced team.

Rescue does not replace the foundation

Before escalation, verify airway position and patency, lung-protective VT, oxygen delivery, PEEP strategy, hemodynamics, positioning, and reversible causes. Rescue therapies are layered onto a sound conventional plan.

What you are expected to know

  • State the principal indication and safety needs for prone positioning.
  • Explain why recruitment maneuvers are selective and higher risk.
  • Recognize markedly asymmetric lung mechanics as a possible indication for independent lung ventilation.
  • Confirm conventional-support optimization before rescue escalation.
C5: Apply analgesia, sedation, neuromuscular blockade, and specialty escalation safely.
Aligned competencies: 2.6.12, 2.6.13, 2.6.14

Treat pain and correct the ventilator mechanism first

Analgesia treats pain; sedation reduces anxiety, awareness, and agitation according to the drug and depth; neuromuscular blockade stops skeletal-muscle activity but does not provide analgesia, amnesia, or unconsciousness. A paralyzed patient still requires adequate analgesia and sedation.

InterventionAppropriate roleSafety requirement
AnalgesiaTreat pain that contributes to distress or ventilator intoleranceOngoing pain assessment and adverse-effect monitoring.
SedationSupport comfort, safety, procedures, or severe synchrony needsUse a defined target and reassess depth and delirium risk.
Neuromuscular blockadeSelected severe cases with persistent injurious effort after other correctable causes are addressedAdequate analgesia/sedation, paralysis monitoring, eye/skin care, DVT prevention, and time-limited reassessment.

Escalate complex rescue support within a system

Advanced modes, proning, independent lung ventilation, HFV, and sustained blockade require protocol familiarity, staffing, equipment, and multidisciplinary coordination. Recognizing the need for specialty consultation is itself a competency; the safest action may be to stabilize with conventional support while mobilizing the experienced team.

What you are expected to know

  • Differentiate analgesia, sedation, and paralysis.
  • State why neuromuscular blockade never replaces analgesia or sedation.
  • Reserve blockade for a defined indication after mechanical causes are addressed.
  • Identify advanced rescue situations requiring protocol use and specialty consultation.
End-of-reading check: You should be able to describe the operating concept, clinical objective, limitations, and safety needs of advanced modes and rescue strategies without treating them as routine escalation.
