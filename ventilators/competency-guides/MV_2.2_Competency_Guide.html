C1: Identify why effective ventilation is inadequate while protecting tidal volume.
Aligned competencies: 2.2.1, 2.2.2

Begin with the delivered breath, not the PaCO2 alone

When PaCO2 is too high or pH is falling, confirm that the measured exhaled VT is appropriate for PBW before increasing breath size. Then determine whether the problem is inadequate total ventilation, increased dead space, a leak, rebreathing, insufficient pressure assistance, air trapping, or increased CO2 production.

Protective VT remains the guardrail

A high PaCO2 does not automatically justify a large VT. When VT is already excessive, reduce it toward the PBW-based goal and use rate or another mode-specific control to restore VE when safe.

What you are expected to know

  • Confirm exhaled VT against the PBW-based target before increasing it.
  • Differentiate low VE from low VA caused by dead space.
  • Recognize leaks and inadequate pressure assistance as delivery problems.
  • Explain why increasing rate may fail when exhalation is incomplete.
C2: Calculate desired minute ventilation, respiratory rate, and predicted PaCO2.
Aligned competencies: 2.2.3, 2.2.11

Use proportional reasoning to plan and check a change

Core calculations

Desired VE = current PaCO2 x current VE / desired PaCO2
Desired RR = desired VE / selected safe VT
Predicted PaCO2 = current PaCO2 x current VE / new VE

These relationships assume CO2 production and the dead-space fraction remain reasonably stable. They provide a planning estimate, not a guarantee. Use the patient baseline and acid-base status to choose a sensible PaCO2 goal; a chronically hypercapnic patient may not need or tolerate normalization to 40 mm Hg.

A calculated rate must fit the respiratory mechanics

After calculating rate, check the resulting total cycle time and the amount of time available for expiration. A mathematically correct rate can be physiologically unsafe in severe airflow obstruction. If the calculated change is not feasible, consider other ways to improve VA, including correcting leaks, reducing apparatus dead space when appropriate, increasing pressure assistance, or addressing excessive CO2 production.

Worked practice

If PaCO2 is 54 mm Hg at VE 7.5 L/min and the desired PaCO2 is 45 mm Hg, desired VE is 9.0 L/min. With a selected VT of 0.45 L, the calculated rate is 20/min. The next question is whether 20/min permits complete exhalation in this patient.

What you are expected to know

  • Calculate desired VE and rate with correct unit conversion.
  • Estimate the PaCO2 expected after a VE change.
  • Choose a patient-specific PaCO2 target rather than automatically using 40 mm Hg.
  • State the assumptions and limitations of the proportional formulas.
C3: Adjust ventilation in volume control and pressure control.
Aligned competencies: 2.2.4, 2.2.5

In volume control, protect VT and use rate deliberately

Volume control targets the set VT, although the measured exhaled VT can be reduced by leaks or pressure limits. When VT is already appropriate, rate is usually the primary control for changing VE. Increasing VT should remain within the selected PBW-based range and pressure limits.

In pressure control, pressure is titrated to measured VT

Pressure control targets inspiratory pressure, so VT changes with compliance, resistance, inspiratory time, effort, and leak. If VT falls after compliance worsens, increasing inspiratory pressure may restore the target, but the clinician must also investigate the cause and monitor distending pressure. Raising PEEP without understanding whether inspiratory pressure is set above PEEP or as total pressure can unintentionally change the driving pressure.

ModeTargeted variablePrimary ventilation controlsRequired reassessment
Volume controlVTRate; VT within protective limitsExhaled VT, VE, pressures, expiratory flow, ABG.
Pressure controlInspiratory pressurePressure above PEEP, rate, inspiratory timeExhaled VT, VE, mechanics, timing, ABG.

Do not change mode to avoid interpreting pressure

An isolated high peak pressure is not an automatic indication for pressure control. First compare peak and plateau pressure to determine whether resistance or compliance changed.

What you are expected to know

  • Select a rate or safe VT adjustment in volume control.
  • Titrate pressure control using measured exhaled VT.
  • Explain how compliance and resistance affect VT in pressure control.
  • Reassess pressures, VT, VE, flow, and ABG after the change.
C4: Adjust pressure support and bilevel ventilation.
Aligned competencies: 2.2.6, 2.2.7

Pressure assistance depends on the inspiratory-expiratory difference

In pressure support and bilevel ventilation, the pressure difference contributes to VT and reduces work of breathing. Before increasing support, correct a loose interface, cuff leak, obstruction, or trigger problem that prevents effective delivery.

Clinical goalTypical controlExpected result
Increase inspiratory assistanceIncrease pressure support or increase IPAP while EPAP remains appropriateLarger VT and/or reduced effort if mechanics and leak permit.
Increase oxygenation supportIncrease FiO2 or EPAP/PEEP according to the mechanismHigher oxygen concentration or greater recruitment.
Improve both on bilevelIncrease EPAP and increase IPAP enough to preserve or enlarge the differenceOxygenation support and ventilatory assistance both rise.

Leaks can imitate inadequate support

A large NIV leak may reduce delivered VT, disrupt triggering and cycling, and cause auto-triggering. Restore the interface seal and reassess before concluding that pressure is insufficient. Excessive pressure can worsen leak, discomfort, gastric insufflation, and synchrony.

What you are expected to know

  • Calculate the pressure difference in bilevel ventilation.
  • Distinguish an IPAP adjustment from an EPAP or FiO2 adjustment.
  • Correct a significant leak before escalating pressure.
  • Use VT, respiratory rate, effort, synchrony, oxygenation, and ABG to evaluate the response.
C5: Reduce excessive ventilation, avoid air trapping, and verify the response.
Aligned competencies: 2.2.8, 2.2.9, 2.2.10, 2.2.12

Excessive ventilation also requires a mechanism-based change

When PaCO2 is too low and pH is alkalemic, determine whether excessive support comes from a high rate, excessive VT, excessive pressure assistance, patient overbreathing, pain, fever, anxiety, or auto-triggering. If VT is already protective, reducing rate or pressure assistance is usually preferable to making VT unnecessarily small.

Do not trade carbon dioxide correction for trapped gas

Before increasing rate, verify that expiratory flow returns to baseline. In obstruction, higher rate can increase intrinsic PEEP, raise the trigger load, reduce venous return, and make effective ventilation worse. Higher inspiratory flow in volume control, a shorter inspiratory time, a smaller safe VT, or a lower rate may provide more time to exhale.

After any change, reassessQuestions to answer
DeliveryDid exhaled VT, total rate, and VE change as intended?
Mechanics and timingDid peak/plateau pressure, expiratory flow, auto-PEEP, or synchrony worsen?
PhysiologyDid PaCO2 and pH move toward the patient-specific goal?
Patient responseDid work of breathing, comfort, mental status, and hemodynamics improve?

What you are expected to know

  • Select a reduction in rate or assistance for excessive ventilation while preserving safe VT.
  • Recognize when a rate increase is unsafe because of incomplete exhalation.
  • Name alternatives that improve VA without unsafe VT.
  • Predict the intended response and define the measurements used to verify it.
End-of-reading check: You should be able to protect VT, calculate a ventilation target, choose the correct mode-specific control, and reassess whether effective ventilation changed as intended.
