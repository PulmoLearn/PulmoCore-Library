C1: Match inspiratory flow and pressure delivery to patient demand.
Aligned competencies: 2.4.1, 2.4.2, 2.4.3, 2.4.4

The patient and ventilator must agree on how quickly inspiration is delivered

In volume control, set flow determines how rapidly the target VT is delivered. If flow is too low for patient demand, the pressure-time curve may show a scooped or concave inspiratory deflection and the patient may exhibit strong inspiratory effort. Increasing flow can shorten inspiratory time and improve comfort, but excessively rapid flow may create abrupt pressure delivery.

Rise time is the pressure-targeted counterpart

In pressure control or pressure support, rise time determines how quickly pressure reaches the target. A rise that is too slow leaves early inspiratory demand unmet; a rise that is too fast may feel abrupt and produce overshoot or discomfort. Terminology varies by ventilator, so know whether a larger displayed value means faster or slower rise.

Observed patternLikely mismatchControl to evaluate
Scooped pressure during VC inspirationInspiratory flow below patient demandIncrease flow or alter flow pattern; reassess timing.
Slow pressure development with strong early effortRise time too slowDeliver pressure more rapidly.
Pressure overshoot or abrupt deliveryFlow or rise too rapidSlow delivery while maintaining adequate support.

What you are expected to know

  • Recognize a flow-starvation pressure pattern.
  • Adjust volume-control flow and predict the effect on inspiratory time.
  • Adjust rise time in a pressure-targeted breath.
  • Avoid confusing a delivery problem with a trigger or oxygenation problem.
C2: Recognize and reduce incomplete exhalation and auto-PEEP.
Aligned competencies: 2.4.5, 2.4.6, 2.4.7

Air trapping occurs when the next breath begins before exhalation is complete

The most useful waveform clue is expiratory flow that has not returned to the zero-flow baseline before the next inspiration. An expiratory hold can quantify total PEEP when the patient is passive. Intrinsic PEEP increases end-expiratory lung volume, work of triggering, and intrathoracic pressure.

Setting or conditionHow it promotes trapping
High respiratory rateShortens the total respiratory cycle and expiratory time.
Low inspiratory flow in volume controlLengthens inspiratory time.
Large VTIncreases the volume that must be exhaled.
Long inspiratory time or pauseDirectly removes time from expiration.
High airway resistanceSlows expiratory emptying.

Create more expiratory time and reduce the expiratory burden

  • Reduce respiratory rate when clinically feasible.
  • Increase inspiratory flow in volume control to deliver VT faster.
  • Shorten inspiratory time or remove an unnecessary pause.
  • Reduce VT when above the selected safe target.
  • Treat reversible resistance such as bronchospasm or secretions.
  • Consider external PEEP carefully when intrinsic PEEP creates a trigger threshold in a spontaneously breathing patient.

What you are expected to know

  • Identify incomplete exhalation on a flow-time curve.
  • Calculate intrinsic PEEP from a valid expiratory hold.
  • Identify settings that reduce expiratory time.
  • Select changes that provide more time to exhale without abandoning ventilation goals.
C3: Differentiate ineffective triggering from auto-triggering and adjust sensitivity.
Aligned competencies: 2.4.8, 2.4.9, 2.4.14

Triggering is the start of the assisted breath

ProblemWhat it looks likeCommon causes
Ineffective triggeringPatient effort appears on pressure or flow but no machine breath followsInsensitive trigger, intrinsic PEEP, weakness, excessive assistance, poor signal.
Auto-triggeringMachine breath occurs without patient effortLeak, water, cardiac oscillation, motion, overly sensitive trigger.

Sensitivity is a balance

A trigger should require little patient effort but remain resistant to false signals. Making the trigger more sensitive may help missed efforts, but can worsen auto-triggering. In obstruction, intrinsic PEEP may be the dominant threshold load; reducing trapped gas is often more important than adjusting sensitivity alone.

Treat the cause of false breaths

On NIV, a high leak with breaths occurring without visible effort should prompt interface correction before escalating pressure or making the trigger even more sensitive.

What you are expected to know

  • Identify ineffective and automatic triggering on waveforms.
  • Generate causes for both patterns.
  • Adjust trigger sensitivity in the correct direction.
  • Correct leak, water, motion, or intrinsic PEEP when it is the underlying trigger problem.
C4: Recognize double triggering, premature cycling, and delayed cycling.
Aligned competencies: 2.4.10, 2.4.11, 2.4.12

Cycling is the transition from inspiration to expiration

AsynchronyBreath-sequence mismatchTypical consequence
Double triggeringOne prolonged patient inspiration triggers two ventilator breathsBreath stacking and potentially excessive combined VT.
Premature cyclingVentilator inspiration ends before patient neural inspiration endsContinued effort may trigger another breath or increase work.
Delayed cyclingVentilator inspiration continues after the patient begins exhalingActive exhalation against inspiratory flow and discomfort.

Correction depends on mode and mechanism

A patient whose inspiratory demand outlasts set inspiratory time may require a longer available inspiratory time, different cycling criterion, more appropriate flow delivery, or correction of insufficient support. A patient whose ventilator breath continues too long may require a shorter inspiratory time or earlier flow cycling. Continue to protect VT and pressures while correcting timing.

What you are expected to know

  • Recognize two breaths during one patient effort as double triggering.
  • Differentiate premature from delayed cycling.
  • Relate set inspiratory time or cycling criteria to neural inspiratory time.
  • Correct timing without accepting injurious breath stacking or excessive VT.
C5: Locate the mismatch and choose a targeted intervention.
Aligned competencies: 2.4.13, 2.4.15

Use the breath sequence as a diagnostic framework

Breath phasePrimary questionCommon problem
Before inspirationDid patient effort initiate a breath?Ineffective trigger or auto-trigger.
During inspirationDid flow or pressure meet demand?Flow starvation or inappropriate rise.
End inspirationDid the ventilator end at the right time?Premature or delayed cycling, double trigger.
ExpirationDid flow return to baseline?Air trapping and intrinsic PEEP.

Correct delivery before assuming agitation

Pain, anxiety, delirium, fever, and respiratory drive can contribute to asynchrony, but sedation does not correct an insensitive trigger, inadequate flow, a leak, or inappropriate cycling. First identify and correct the ventilator or airway mechanism. Treat pain and other patient causes in parallel when present.

A complete answer has three parts

Name the mismatch, identify the evidence that supports it, and select the setting or bedside action that directly changes that mechanism. Then state how VT, pressures, timing, and the patient response will be reassessed.

What you are expected to know

  • Classify an asynchrony as trigger, delivery, cycling, or expiratory.
  • Use waveform timing to defend the classification.
  • Select a targeted ventilator or interface correction.
  • Distinguish a mechanical correction from an appropriate analgesia or sedation intervention.
End-of-reading check: You should be able to locate the mismatch within the breath, distinguish flow, trigger, and cycling problems, and select a targeted correction while protecting expiratory time.
