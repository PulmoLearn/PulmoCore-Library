C1: Quantify total ventilation, effective ventilation, and dead space.
Aligned competencies: 2.1.1, 2.1.2, 2.1.3

Minute ventilation is not the same as effective ventilation

Minute ventilation (VE) is the total volume exhaled each minute. Alveolar ventilation (VA) is the portion that reaches gas-exchanging alveoli after dead-space volume is removed. Two patients can have the same VE but very different VA when their dead-space fractions differ.

Core relationships

VE = VT x total respiratory rate
VA = (VT - VD) x respiratory rate
Convert VT and VD from milliliters to liters when the answer is expressed in L/min.

MeasurementWhat it includesClinical meaning
Minute ventilationAll exhaled volume, including dead-space gasDescribes total movement of gas per minute.
Alveolar ventilationOnly the volume participating in alveolar gas exchangeMore closely relates to carbon dioxide elimination.
Physiologic dead spaceAnatomic dead space plus ventilated but poorly perfused alveoliExplains why VE may appear adequate while PaCO2 rises.

Dead-space ratio shows how much of each breath is wasted

The Bohr-Enghoff relationship commonly used in respiratory care is VD/VT = (PaCO2 - PECO2) / PaCO2. A higher fraction means more of each breath fails to eliminate carbon dioxide. When VD/VT rises, increasing total VE may be necessary just to preserve the same VA.

Interpret the trend, not just the calculation

A rising PaCO2 with unchanged VE may reflect increased dead space, increased CO2 production, rebreathing, or a new delivery problem. Use the calculated value with hemodynamics, disease progression, circuit configuration, and exhaled volumes.

What you are expected to know

  • Calculate VE and VA with correct units.
  • Calculate VD/VT from PaCO2 and mixed-expired PCO2.
  • Explain why an unchanged VE does not guarantee unchanged VA.
  • Connect rising dead space with a higher PaCO2 or greater ventilation requirement.
C2: Calculate and distinguish dynamic and static compliance.
Aligned competencies: 2.1.4, 2.1.5

Compliance describes volume change for a pressure change

Respiratory-system compliance reflects the distensibility of the lungs and chest wall. Dynamic compliance is measured while gas is flowing and therefore reflects both elastic and resistive influences. Static compliance uses plateau pressure during zero flow and more specifically reflects the elastic load.

MeasurementFormulaPressure condition
Dynamic complianceCdyn = exhaled VT / (PIP - PEEP)Measured during flow; affected by resistance and compliance.
Static complianceCstat = exhaled VT / (Pplat - PEEP)Measured during an inspiratory hold when flow is zero.

Use the correct pressure in the denominator

Peak pressure contains the pressure needed to overcome airway resistance plus the pressure needed to distend the respiratory system. Plateau pressure is obtained after flow stops, so the resistive component falls away. Subtract set PEEP or total PEEP according to the clinical question and protocol, and use exhaled VT rather than assuming the set VT was delivered.

Pattern recognition

If Cdyn falls while Cstat remains relatively stable, look for a resistive problem. If both fall and plateau pressure rises for the same VT, look for reduced lung or chest-wall compliance.

What you are expected to know

  • Calculate Cdyn and Cstat in mL/cm H2O.
  • State why PIP is used for dynamic compliance and plateau pressure for static compliance.
  • Use the relationship between the two values to separate resistance from compliance.
  • Recognize conditions that make a compliance measurement unreliable, including active effort or an invalid hold.
C3: Use peak and plateau pressure to evaluate resistance and elastic load.
Aligned competencies: 2.1.6, 2.1.9, 2.1.11

Peak and plateau pressure answer different questions

PressureRepresentsWhat a rise suggests
Peak inspiratory pressureResistive pressure + elastic pressure + PEEPResistance, compliance, flow, effort, or a combination.
Plateau pressureElastic pressure + PEEP during zero flowReduced compliance, increased VT, overdistention, or chest-wall load.
Peak-minus-plateau differenceResistive pressure at the measured flowAirway, tube, secretion, bronchospasm, or flow-related resistance.

Airway-resistance formula

Raw = (PIP - Pplat) / inspiratory flow in L/sec
Convert L/min to L/sec before calculating. The value is meaningful only when the pressure and flow measurements are valid.

Read the pattern before changing the mode

A selective increase in peak pressure with a stable plateau points toward increased resistance. A rise in both peak and plateau pressure with a similar peak-to-plateau separation points toward reduced compliance or a larger elastic load. A mixed pattern can occur when both mechanisms change.

Likely categoryExamples to evaluate
ResistanceSecretions, bronchospasm, kinked tube, biting, water in circuit, higher set flow, smaller artificial airway.
ComplianceAtelectasis, edema, pneumothorax, abdominal distention, positioning, overdistention, worsening ARDS.
Measurement/effortCoughing, active inspiration during the hold, dyssynchrony, leak, insufficient hold time.

What you are expected to know

  • Calculate Raw after converting flow to L/sec.
  • Interpret peak-only, plateau-inclusive, and mixed pressure changes.
  • Generate a focused differential for resistance and compliance problems.
  • Use the pressure pattern to choose the next assessment rather than changing modes reflexively.
C4: Obtain and interpret inspiratory and expiratory holds.
Aligned competencies: 2.1.7, 2.1.8

An inspiratory hold estimates plateau pressure

During an inspiratory hold, flow stops while the airway remains closed. Pressure equilibrates between the proximal airway and alveoli, allowing plateau pressure to approximate the elastic pressure required to hold the delivered volume. The patient should be passive or sufficiently synchronous for the value to be interpretable.

An expiratory hold measures total PEEP

During an expiratory hold, the expiratory valve closes at end expiration. If the patient is not actively breathing, airway pressure equilibrates with alveolar pressure. Intrinsic PEEP = total PEEP - set PEEP. Expiratory flow that has not returned to baseline before the next breath is an important clue that trapped gas may be present.

HoldPrimary measurementCommon limitations
Inspiratory holdPlateau pressureActive effort, leak, cough, irregular breath, inadequate hold.
Expiratory holdTotal PEEP and intrinsic PEEPSpontaneous effort, leak, unstable breathing pattern, incomplete occlusion.

A hold confirms a mechanism; it does not replace waveform review

Use flow-time and pressure-time curves to decide when a hold is needed and whether the result is plausible. A single hold value that conflicts with the rest of the data should be repeated under better conditions.

What you are expected to know

  • Describe how inspiratory and expiratory holds are performed and what each measures.
  • Calculate intrinsic PEEP from total and set PEEP.
  • Identify patient effort and leak as threats to measurement validity.
  • Use hold results with pressures, flow, and the patient presentation.
C5: Interpret mean airway pressure and integrate mechanics into a clinical explanation.
Aligned competencies: 2.1.10, 2.1.11

Mean airway pressure is the average pressure across the respiratory cycle

Mean airway pressure (Pmean or MAP) is influenced by PEEP, inspiratory pressure, inspiratory time, respiratory rate, and the shape of the inspiratory pressure or flow waveform. Increasing the amount of pressure or the time spent at higher pressure generally raises mean airway pressure and may improve recruitment and oxygenation.

ChangeExpected effect on mean airway pressureImportant tradeoff
Increase PEEPUsually increases MAPMay reduce venous return or cause overdistention.
Increase inspiratory pressureUsually increases MAPMay increase VT or distending pressure.
Prolong inspiratory timeIncreases time at inspiratory pressureShortens expiratory time and may promote trapping.
Increase rateMay increase MAP by shortening the cycleMay reduce expiratory time and alter auto-PEEP.

Build a mechanism statement

A strong interpretation links the observation to physiology: identify what changed, name the mechanical category, give the most likely causes, and state what measurement or action would confirm it. For example, do not stop at “high pressure.” Determine whether resistance, compliance, trapped gas, flow demand, effort, or measurement error best explains the pattern.

What you are expected to know

  • Identify the major determinants of mean airway pressure.
  • Predict how a timing or pressure change may affect oxygenation and expiratory time.
  • Combine pressures, holds, flow, and calculated mechanics into a mechanism statement.
  • Name a targeted next assessment for the suspected mechanism.
End-of-reading check: You should be able to calculate and interpret ventilation and mechanics, obtain valid hold measurements, and use pressure patterns to identify the likely mechanism.
