Mechanical Ventilation - 1.2 Competency Guide

How the Ventilator Delivers a Breath

Teach C3 and C4 as a connected reasoning sequence: identify the breath target, interpret pressure-volume-flow relationships, recognize timing and trigger problems, and use graphics to decide what information to assess next.

How to use this guide: The definitions and calculations below are prerequisite knowledge. The competency endpoints are prediction and interpretation. Students should be able to explain why a value or waveform changed, not simply name a term.

Clinical question
What is the ventilator controlling, what is the patient's respiratory system doing, and what should change when mechanics change?

Breath-delivery framework

Trigger

What starts inspiration?

Patient effort or machine timing can begin a breath.

Control / limit

What is targeted during inspiration?

Basic volume control targets volume/flow delivery; pressure control targets inspiratory pressure.

Cycle

What ends inspiration?

Time or a flow threshold commonly ends inspiration, depending on the breath type.

Baseline

What remains at expiration?

PEEP establishes the end-expiratory pressure baseline.

Important: patient-triggered describes how the breath begins. A patient-triggered assist-control breath can still receive the full preset mandatory target.

Volume control versus pressure control

Volume control

The selected volume/flow target is protected. Pressure becomes dependent on compliance and resistance.

Compliance down -> pressure up for the same VT
Resistance up -> PIP rises more than Pplat when compliance is unchanged

Pressure control

The selected inspiratory pressure and inspiratory time are protected. Flow and tidal volume become dependent on mechanics.

Compliance down -> delivered VT can fall
Resistance up -> flow and VT can fall

Pressure landmarks

PEEP
baseline
Pplat
static / elastic pressure
PIP
elastic + resistive pressure

Resistance pattern

PIP rises while Pplat remains relatively stable.

Resistive pressure ~= PIP - Pplat

Compliance pattern

PIP and Pplat rise together because more pressure is required to stretch the respiratory system.

Elastic driving pressure ~= Pplat - PEEP

Useful calculations

Static compliance

Cstat = exhaled VT / (Pplat - PEEP)

Lower Cstat means a stiffer respiratory system.

Airway resistance

Raw ~= (PIP - Pplat) / flow in L/s

Convert L/min to L/s by dividing by 60.

Total cycle time

TCT = 60 / respiratory rate

Ti + Te must fit inside the breath cycle.

Calculation rule: calculations are useful when they explain the patient's trend. Do not turn C3 into repeated arithmetic without interpretation.

Time constants and auto-PEEP

Long time constant

High resistance slows filling and emptying. Obstructive disease may require more expiratory time.

Time constant = resistance x compliance

Air-trapping clue

If expiratory flow has not returned to zero before the next breath begins, exhalation is incomplete and intrinsic PEEP should be assessed.

Why this matters: C3 is not "calculate compliance." The endpoint is predicting what happens to pressure, flow, tidal volume, and timing when the patient's mechanics change.

Clinical question
Is the ventilator responding to the patient at the right time, with enough flow, and with enough time for exhalation?

Triggering

Pressure trigger

The ventilator senses a patient-generated pressure drop. A less negative threshold generally requires less effort.

Flow trigger

The ventilator detects a change in circuit flow caused by patient effort.

Ineffective trigger

The patient makes an effort but no assisted breath follows. Assess sensitivity, strength, intrinsic PEEP, and circuit factors.

Auto-trigger

A ventilator breath occurs without true effort. Assess leaks, water, movement, circuit disturbance, and excessive sensitivity.

Cycling and timing

Time cycling

Inspiration ends after the selected inspiratory time. Pressure-controlled mandatory breaths are commonly time-cycled.

Flow cycling

Inspiration ends when inspiratory flow falls to a threshold. Pressure-support breaths are commonly flow-cycled.

Obstructive timing trap: a higher respiratory rate shortens total cycle time. If inspiratory time stays fixed, expiratory time decreases and air trapping can worsen.

Basic graphics

Patient trigger deflection

A small negative pressure deflection immediately before a supported breath can represent patient inspiratory effort.

Incomplete exhalation

Expiratory flow remaining below zero when the next breath starts is a basic clue for air trapping and auto-PEEP.

Flow-demand mismatch

In volume-targeted ventilation, a concave inspiratory pressure contour during strong patient effort can suggest insufficient inspiratory flow for patient demand.

Missed-trigger pattern

Repeated effort deflections without a supported breath indicate ineffective triggering.

Information gathering before correction

High PIP

Obtain or compare plateau pressure to decide whether the rise is predominantly resistive or elastic.

Missed effort

Assess trigger sensitivity, patient strength, intrinsic PEEP, and circuit factors before reflexively changing sensitivity.

Waveform abnormality

Confirm the pattern with bedside assessment, mode, settings, pressures, volumes, flow, and circuit before acting.

Why this matters: C4 is a clinical-judgment competency. The waveform should help students decide what to assess next, not become a memorized picture with one automatic answer.

Before the weekly quiz and competency exam

Students should be able to predict VC versus PC responses to mechanics, distinguish resistance from compliance using pressure data, calculate and interpret Cstat/Raw/TCT, recognize incomplete exhalation, identify ineffective versus auto-triggering, and connect basic waveform patterns to the next useful assessment.
