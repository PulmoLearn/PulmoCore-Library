C1: Distinguish weaning, liberation, SBT, and extubation.
Aligned competencies: 3.1.1, 3.1.9

The terms describe different decisions

TermMeaningClinical question
WeaningGradual reduction of ventilatory supportDoes the patient need less assistance over time?
LiberationEnding mechanical ventilatory support because it is no longer requiredCan the patient sustain spontaneous breathing?
Spontaneous breathing trialA time-limited assessment with minimal or no positive-pressure assistanceCan the patient tolerate the load of spontaneous breathing now?
ExtubationRemoval of the endotracheal tubeCan the patient maintain airway patency, protection, and clearance after tube removal?

A patient can pass an SBT and be liberated from ventilatory assistance yet remain unsafe for extubation because of impaired consciousness, weak cough, copious secretions, or upper-airway obstruction risk. Keeping these decisions separate prevents an SBT from becoming an automatic extubation order.

What you are expected to know

  • Use liberation, weaning, SBT, and extubation correctly.
  • Explain why passing an SBT does not prove airway readiness.
  • Identify airway protection and clearance as separate from ventilatory capacity.
C2: Perform a standardized daily readiness screen.
Aligned competencies: 3.1.2, 3.1.10

Readiness is a safety screen for formal spontaneous-breathing assessment

A daily screen should be structured enough to prevent missed opportunities while flexible enough to reflect patient-specific physiology. The purpose is not to prove extubation success. It is to decide whether an SBT is reasonable and safe.

DomainEvidence supporting readinessFindings that require correction or caution
Underlying causeResolved or sufficiently controlledWorsening pneumonia, bronchospasm, edema, shock, or neurologic injury.
OxygenationAcceptable saturation/PaO2 on modest, stable FiO2 and PEEPEscalating oxygen or PEEP, severe refractory hypoxemia.
HemodynamicsStable perfusion without escalating supportRising vasopressor need, ischemia, serious dysrhythmia, worsening lactate.
Ventilatory/acid-baseAcceptable pH and ventilation for the patient baselineProgressive acidemia, uncontrolled load, severe auto-PEEP.
Neurologic/driveAble to initiate an effective inspiratory effortDeep sedation, apnea, or inability to participate sufficiently.

Use a protocol, not a collection of automatic cutoffs

Thresholds can organize screening, but one borderline value should be interpreted with trend, diagnosis, reserve, and the rest of the readiness pattern.

What you are expected to know

  • Screen the five major readiness domains.
  • Use trends and patient baseline rather than demanding normal values.
  • Distinguish a reason to defer from a finding that only requires closer monitoring.
C3: Evaluate disease improvement, oxygenation, hemodynamics, and acid-base status.
Aligned competencies: 3.1.3, 3.1.4, 3.1.5, 3.1.7

The original respiratory failure must be improving

The cause does not need to be completely cured, but the patient must be able to assume the work previously carried by the ventilator. Evaluate the current load and the trajectory: resolving infection, improved bronchospasm, controlled fluid overload, stabilized neurologic disease, corrected postoperative effects, or restored perfusion.

Adequate does not mean normal

A patient with chronic hypercapnia may be ready at a PaCO2 near baseline with an acceptable pH. A patient can remain on supplemental oxygen and still be ready if oxygenation is stable on support that can be reasonably continued after extubation. Hemodynamic readiness is defined by stability, not by the complete absence of cardiovascular medication.

Interpretation errorBetter reasoning
Require PaCO2 of 40 mm HgCompare with baseline PaCO2, bicarbonate, pH, and air-trapping risk.
Reject any patient receiving a vasopressorEvaluate dose, trend, perfusion, and whether support is stable or escalating.
Require room air and zero PEEPAssess stable oxygenation on modest support and the likely post-extubation plan.
Treat fever or RR alone as failureEvaluate cause, trend, VT, effort, and overall tolerance.

What you are expected to know

  • Recognize evidence that the underlying cause is controlled.
  • Judge oxygenation and acid-base status relative to patient baseline.
  • Differentiate stable limited hemodynamic support from worsening shock.
  • Avoid unnecessary delay caused by demands for complete normalization.
C4: Evaluate neurologic status, inspiratory drive, sedation, and reversible barriers.
Aligned competencies: 3.1.6, 3.1.8, 3.1.11

The patient must be able to demonstrate spontaneous breathing

An SBT requires an effective inspiratory effort. Deep sedation, residual neuromuscular blockade, metabolic encephalopathy, severe weakness, pain, and sleep deprivation may suppress or distort performance. Coordinate a spontaneous-awakening or sedation-reduction strategy when appropriate, while continuing analgesia and safety monitoring.

BarrierHow it interferesPossible response
Excess sedationSuppresses drive, consciousness, and coughUse a protocolized reduction and reassess.
Bronchospasm or secretionsRaises resistive work and promotes trappingTreat obstruction and clear the airway.
Positive fluid balance/cardiac loadIncreases spontaneous-breathing cardiovascular stressEvaluate and treat fluid/cardiac contributors.
Electrolyte or nutrition problemWeakens respiratory musclesCorrect clinically important abnormalities.
Pain, fever, agitationIncreases drive and metabolic demandTreat the cause without masking respiratory failure.

Sedation is not a treatment for an uncorrected ventilator problem

Before attributing tachypnea or distress to anxiety, check flow, timing, auto-PEEP, airway resistance, pain, and disease progression.

What you are expected to know

  • Confirm an effective spontaneous inspiratory effort.
  • Recognize excessive sedation as a reversible barrier.
  • Coordinate sedation reduction with the readiness assessment.
  • Identify and correct common mechanical, cardiac, metabolic, and comfort barriers.
C5: Integrate readiness without relying on a single value.
Aligned competencies: 3.1.9, 3.1.10, 3.1.11

Readiness is a clinical pattern

The best decision combines trajectory, oxygenation, hemodynamics, acid-base status, inspiratory effort, mechanics, comfort, and reversible load. Calculations such as RSBI can contribute information, but the SBT is the integrated performance test. Airway protection is evaluated separately after ventilatory readiness is demonstrated.

A defensible readiness statement

State whether the cause is improving, summarize the major safety domains, identify any reversible barrier, and decide whether to proceed with an SBT, defer briefly for treatment, or escalate evaluation.

DecisionWhat supports it
Proceed to SBTMajor domains are stable and the patient can initiate breathing.
Defer and correctA reversible barrier is likely to make the trial unsafe or invalid.
Do not extubate despite SBT readinessAirway protection, patency, or secretion clearance is inadequate.

What you are expected to know

  • Create an integrated readiness statement.
  • Use RSBI and other indices as supporting data rather than absolute gates.
  • Name the specific reversible barrier when deferring.
  • Separate the SBT decision from the extubation decision.
C1: Interpret spontaneous VT and vital capacity.
Aligned competencies: 3.2.1, 3.2.2, 3.2.10

Breath size and reserve must be interpreted in context

Spontaneous VT describes the volume of an individual breath; vital capacity is the maximal volume exhaled after a maximal inspiration. Both depend on effort, coaching, pain, position, leak, and measurement technique. Expressing volume per PBW can help compare patients, but no single value replaces the observed breathing pattern.

MeasurementCalculation/techniqueInterpretation
VT per PBWExhaled VT in mL / PBW in kgCombine with rate, effort, and gas exchange.
Vital capacity per PBWMaximal exhaled volume in mL / PBW in kgReflects reserve but is effort dependent.

Technique matters

Use a secure system, explain and coach the maneuver, allow adequate effort, and repeat when the result conflicts with the patient presentation. A leak or poor effort can create a falsely low value.

What you are expected to know

  • Calculate VT and VC in mL/kg PBW.
  • Distinguish quiet-breath VT from maximal vital capacity.
  • Identify effort, leak, pain, and coaching as validity issues.
  • Use volume measurements with rate, comfort, and SBT performance.
C2: Measure and interpret MIP/NIF and MEP.
Aligned competencies: 3.2.3, 3.2.4, 3.2.10

Maximal pressures estimate respiratory-muscle strength

MeasurementDirectionClinical relationship
MIP or NIFNegative inspiratory pressureInspiratory muscle strength and ability to generate a breath.
MEPPositive expiratory pressureExpiratory muscle strength and contribution to an effective cough.

For MIP/NIF, the more negative valid pressure represents the stronger inspiratory effort. Record the best reproducible effort rather than averaging strong and weak attempts. MEP adds information about cough potential but does not directly measure secretion burden, glottic function, or mental status.

Validity depends on patient and equipment

  • Cooperation and coaching
  • A tight seal without cuff or circuit leak
  • Sufficient occlusion or maneuver time
  • Appropriate position and pain control
  • Recognition of fatigue or inconsistent effort

What you are expected to know

  • Identify the direction and meaning of MIP/NIF and MEP.
  • Choose the strongest valid inspiratory effort.
  • Connect MEP with cough strength without treating it as a complete airway assessment.
  • Recognize technical and patient factors that invalidate maximal pressures.
C3: Calculate and use RSBI appropriately.
Aligned competencies: 3.2.5, 3.2.6, 3.2.12

RSBI describes rapid shallow breathing

Formula

RSBI = spontaneous respiratory rate / VT in liters
A patient breathing 24/min with VT 0.40 L has an RSBI of 60 breaths/min/L.

A lower RSBI generally reflects a slower, deeper pattern; a higher value reflects rapid shallow breathing. Traditional thresholds remain common on board-style questions, but current guidance does not require RSBI to decide readiness for an SBT. A patient may proceed based on the clinical readiness screen, and real-time SBT performance takes priority over a favorable pretrial index.

MisuseBetter use
Delay every SBT until RSBI is measuredProceed when the patient meets readiness criteria.
Extubate solely because RSBI is favorableComplete SBT and airway assessment.
Ignore distress because RSBI was favorableStop or reassess when observed tolerance is poor.
Treat one high value as permanent failureCheck technique, trend, causes, and SBT performance.

What you are expected to know

  • Calculate RSBI with VT converted to liters.
  • Describe what a high or low value means.
  • Recognize RSBI as supporting rather than mandatory information.
  • Prioritize observed SBT tolerance over a screening index.
C4: Calculate VD/VT, shunt fraction, and A-a gradient.
Aligned competencies: 3.2.7, 3.2.8, 3.2.9

These calculations describe different gas-exchange problems

CalculationFormulaPrimary meaning
Dead-space ratioVD/VT = (PaCO2 - PECO2) / PaCO2Fraction of VT not effectively eliminating CO2.
Shunt fractionQs/Qt = (CcO2 - CaO2) / (CcO2 - CvO2)Fraction of cardiac output bypassing effective oxygenation.
Alveolar oxygen pressurePAO2 = FiO2(Patm - PH2O) - PaCO2/REstimated alveolar oxygen tension.
A-a gradientPAO2 - PaO2Difference between alveolar and arterial oxygen tension.

A high dead-space fraction increases the VE required to maintain a given VA. A high shunt fraction explains oxygenation difficulty that may respond poorly to FiO2 alone. An elevated A-a gradient indicates impaired oxygen transfer beyond hypoventilation alone; interpret it with age, FiO2, barometric pressure, and clinical setting.

Board calculation versus bedside decision

Know the formulas and what direction is favorable, but do not use one calculated value as an automatic extubation rule. The patient must still demonstrate sustainable spontaneous breathing and airway safety.

What you are expected to know

  • Calculate VD/VT, Qs/Qt, PAO2, and A-a gradient when data are provided.
  • Explain the physiologic problem represented by each calculation.
  • Connect dead space with ventilation demand and shunt/A-a gradient with oxygenation.
  • Avoid substituting a gas-exchange calculation for the SBT.
C5: Integrate trends, validity, and bedside performance.
Aligned competencies: 3.2.10, 3.2.11, 3.2.12

Measurements are most useful when they agree with the patient

A strong interpretation begins by checking whether the measurement is technically valid. Then compare it with previous values and other domains. Discordant data should prompt investigation rather than automatic pass or failure.

Discordant patternQuestion to ask
Low VC but comfortable sustained breathingWas effort maximal, coaching adequate, and the system leak-free?
Favorable RSBI but rising distressIs the patient developing fatigue, cardiac load, or worsening gas exchange?
Strong MIP but weak coughIs expiratory strength, glottic function, mental status, or secretion burden limiting?
Adequate VE but rising PaCO2Has dead space increased or is volume delivery inaccurate?

Integration sequence

Validate the measurement → interpret the physiology → compare the trend → observe the SBT → assess airway readiness.

What you are expected to know

  • Identify whether a liberation measurement is technically valid.
  • Use trends and multiple domains instead of one cutoff.
  • Resolve discordant findings with targeted reassessment.
  • Distinguish calculations retained for board preparation from current protocol decisions.
End-of-reading check: You should be able to distinguish liberation from extubation, complete a daily readiness screen, identify reversible barriers, and decide whether an SBT is appropriate.
