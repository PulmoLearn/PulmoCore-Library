C1: Identify the cause of hypoxemia and choose between FiO2 and PEEP.
Aligned competencies: 2.3.1, 2.3.2

FiO2 and PEEP solve different parts of the problem

FiO2 changes the oxygen concentration entering the alveoli. PEEP increases end-expiratory pressure and may recruit unstable or collapsed alveoli. The appropriate first adjustment depends on the current FiO2, current PEEP, severity and speed of desaturation, evidence of recruitability, and hemodynamic tolerance.

FindingMore direct initial controlReasoning
Low FiO2 with acute hypoxemiaIncrease FiO2Rapidly raises inspired oxygen concentration.
High FiO2 with low PEEP and recruitable diseaseConsider increasing PEEPAddresses derecruitment and shunt rather than oxygen concentration alone.
Hypoxemia with hypotension after a PEEP increaseReduce or reassess PEEPThe pressure may be impairing circulation or causing overdistention.
Unilateral or positional diseaseReposition and evaluate the causeDistribution of ventilation and perfusion may be the dominant problem.

Persistent hypoxemia requires a differential

  • Insufficient FiO2 or oxygen-supply failure
  • Derecruitment, atelectasis, pulmonary edema, or ARDS
  • Shunt or severe ventilation-perfusion mismatch
  • Airway obstruction, mucus plugging, or mainstem intubation
  • Pneumothorax, worsening infiltrates, or hemodynamic deterioration
  • Poor positioning or loss of a previously effective position

What you are expected to know

  • Separate oxygen concentration from alveolar recruitment.
  • Use current FiO2 and PEEP when choosing the next control.
  • Generate a focused differential for new or persistent hypoxemia.
  • Recognize conditions in which a setting change is not the primary solution.
C2: Use PEEP for recruitment while monitoring hemodynamic effects.
Aligned competencies: 2.3.3, 2.3.4

PEEP can recruit alveoli, but response is not defined by SpO2 alone

A successful PEEP change improves oxygenation or compliance without unacceptable plateau pressure, overdistention, hypotension, or reduction in cardiac output. Evaluate the entire response rather than treating any increase in saturation as proof that more PEEP is beneficial.

Potential benefitPotential harm
Maintains end-expiratory lung volumeReduces venous return and blood pressure.
Recruits unstable alveoli and reduces shuntOverdistends already open alveoli.
May improve compliance in recruitable lungMay reduce compliance when overdistention occurs.
May permit later FiO2 reductionMay raise plateau and driving pressure.

Assess before and after the change

  • SpO2, PaO2, and FiO2 requirement
  • Blood pressure, perfusion, and evidence of reduced cardiac output
  • Plateau pressure and driving pressure
  • Static compliance or VT in pressure control
  • Pressure-volume loop pattern and signs of overdistention
  • Patient comfort and synchrony

Recruitment is patient-specific

An oxygenation response to PEEP can reflect recruitment, but it can also coexist with overdistention or hemodynamic compromise. Keep the change only when the overall benefit is favorable.

What you are expected to know

  • State the intended recruitment effect of PEEP.
  • Identify hypotension and falling VT as possible adverse responses.
  • Compare oxygenation benefit with changes in compliance and plateau pressure.
  • Reverse or modify a PEEP change when harm outweighs benefit.
C3: Relate mean airway pressure and inspiratory time to oxygenation.
Aligned competencies: 2.3.5, 2.3.6, 2.3.7

Oxygenation can improve when pressure is sustained longer

Mean airway pressure rises when PEEP, inspiratory pressure, or time at inspiratory pressure increases. A higher mean pressure may improve alveolar recruitment and oxygenation, but it does not identify which component should be changed or guarantee a safe response.

AdjustmentPossible benefitImportant risk
Increase PEEPHigher end-expiratory volume and recruitmentHypotension and overdistention.
Increase inspiratory pressureHigher pressure and possibly larger VTExcess VT or distending pressure.
Prolong inspiratory timeMore time at elevated pressureLess expiratory time, air trapping, discomfort, sedation need.
Increase rateMay raise time-averaged pressureShorter expiration and auto-PEEP.

Inspiratory-to-expiratory timing is a tradeoff

Longer inspiration can improve oxygenation in selected patients by raising mean airway pressure, but it reduces time available for exhalation. This is particularly hazardous in airflow obstruction. Inverse-ratio strategies require careful monitoring and are not a routine response to hypoxemia.

What you are expected to know

  • Explain how mean airway pressure can influence oxygenation.
  • Predict which pressure and timing changes tend to increase it.
  • Identify air trapping and hemodynamic compromise as potential costs.
  • Distinguish selective timing strategies from routine oxygenation adjustments.
C4: Differentiate inadequate PEEP from overdistention.
Aligned competencies: 2.3.8

Both under-recruitment and overdistention can impair oxygenation

EvidenceInadequate PEEP / tidal recruitmentExcessive PEEP / overdistention
Pressure-volume loopLower inflection or opening pattern; repeated low-volume recruitmentUpper beaking or flattening.
ComplianceMay improve when appropriate PEEP is addedMay fall as PEEP increases.
Plateau pressureMay be acceptable despite hypoxemiaMay rise into an unsafe range.
HemodynamicsOften stable before recruitmentMay worsen with higher intrathoracic pressure.
Oxygenation responseMay improve with recruitmentMay improve slightly, remain unchanged, or worsen despite mechanical harm.

Use a bundle of evidence

A loop pattern should be interpreted with the axis scale, VT, PEEP, plateau pressure, compliance, blood pressure, and oxygenation. No single loop feature should be used in isolation. The goal is enough PEEP to support recruitment without pushing the lung onto a less compliant, overdistended portion of its pressure-volume relationship.

What you are expected to know

  • Recognize lower-loop recruitment and upper-loop beaking.
  • Use compliance and plateau pressure to support the interpretation.
  • Include hemodynamic response in the PEEP decision.
  • Select a direction of change based on the full pattern rather than oxygen saturation alone.
C5: Use positioning and selective rescue strategies when conventional support fails.
Aligned competencies: 2.3.9, 2.3.10, 2.3.11, 2.3.12

Positioning is an active oxygenation intervention

Head elevation, lateral positioning, secretion-directed positioning, and prone positioning can change ventilation distribution, perfusion matching, lung recruitment, and secretion drainage. The position should match the disease pattern, airway needs, hemodynamics, skin risk, and procedural constraints.

Prone positioning for severe ARDS

Prone positioning is considered when severe ARDS remains hypoxemic despite lung-protective ventilation and an optimized FiO2/PEEP strategy. It requires a trained team, secure airway and lines, pressure-injury prevention, and a plan for monitoring tolerance and response.

Recruitment maneuvers are selective, higher-risk interventions

Recruitment maneuvers can transiently raise airway pressure to open unstable lung units, but may cause hypotension, overdistention, or barotrauma. They should not be taught as routine escalation for every hypoxemic patient. Use protocol guidance and assess contraindications and response.

Recognizing conventional-support failure

Persistent severe hypoxemia after verifying the airway and circuit, protective VT, FiO2 delivery, PEEP strategy, positioning, and hemodynamics should prompt escalation to an experienced rescue team rather than repeated unguided setting changes.

What you are expected to know

  • Select positioning based on oxygenation and secretion goals.
  • State when prone positioning should be considered in severe ARDS.
  • Explain why recruitment maneuvers are selective rather than routine.
  • Recognize when optimized conventional support has failed and specialty escalation is needed.
End-of-reading check: You should be able to identify the oxygenation mechanism, select FiO2, PEEP, timing, or positioning appropriately, and recognize both failure and harm from escalation.
