C1: Calculate predicted body weight and select a lung-protective tidal volume.
Aligned competencies: 1.3.1, 1.3.2, 1.3.3, 1.3.4, 1.3.5

Predicted body weight represents expected lung size

Adult VT is selected from predicted body weight, not actual body weight. PBW is based on height and a sex-specific formula because lung size does not increase in proportion to adipose tissue or total body weight.

FormulaCalculation
Adult male PBW50 + 2.3(inches over 60)
Adult female PBW45.5 + 2.3(inches over 60)
Target VTPBW x prescribed mL/kg
VT rangePBW x lower target through PBW x upper target

Select the target within a lung-protective range

For many adults without a condition requiring a lower target, an initial range of 6-8 mL/kg PBW is appropriate. A stronger lung-protective approach, commonly beginning near 6 mL/kg PBW, is used in ARDS. The selected value is refined using plateau pressure, delivered VT, acid-base status, synchrony, and clinical protocol.

Worked example

A patient has PBW 68 kg. A 6 mL/kg target is 408 mL; an 8 mL/kg target is 544 mL. A practical starting setting would be chosen within that range and then evaluated using delivered volume, pressure, and patient response.

Obesity does not justify a larger VT

For a patient with obesity, continue to use height-based PBW. Hypoxemia related to reduced functional residual capacity is addressed with positioning, PEEP, FiO2, and recruitment strategy when appropriate - not by calculating VT from actual weight.

What you are expected to know

  • Calculate PBW from height using the correct formula.
  • Calculate a single VT target and a VT range.
  • Use PBW rather than actual weight in obesity.
  • Recognize why ARDS generally requires a lower VT target and closer pressure monitoring.
C2: Calculate minute ventilation and an initial respiratory rate.
Aligned competencies: 1.3.6, 1.3.7

Minute ventilation combines breath size and frequency

Minute ventilation is the volume exhaled in one minute. Use exhaled VT and total respiratory rate when evaluating what the patient actually receives.

Core formulas

VE = VT x total respiratory rate
Initial RR = desired VE / selected VT
Convert milliliters to liters before calculating L/min.

Worked calculation

If exhaled VT is 0.40 L and total rate is 16/min, VE is 6.4 L/min. If a desired VE of 8.4 L/min is needed with a selected VT of 0.42 L, the calculated rate is 20/min.

A calculated rate still requires clinical judgment

  • Use the total rate when the patient triggers extra breaths.
  • Confirm that VT is safe for PBW before solving for rate.
  • Ensure the rate permits adequate expiration, especially with airflow obstruction.
  • Consider that leaks or pressure limits may make exhaled VT differ from the intended setting.
  • Reassess pH, PaCO2, delivered VE, and expiratory time after initiation.

What you are expected to know

  • Calculate VE using correct units and the total respiratory rate.
  • Calculate an initial rate from desired VE and selected VT.
  • Explain why the calculated rate may need modification for obstruction, triggering, or incomplete exhalation.
C3: Establish initial invasive ventilator settings for the clinical problem.
Aligned competencies: 1.3.8, 1.3.9, 1.3.10, 1.3.11, 1.3.12

A complete prescription includes more than mode and VT

  • Mode and breath target
  • VT or inspiratory pressure
  • Respiratory rate
  • FiO2
  • PEEP
  • Inspiratory flow or inspiratory time
  • I:E ratio
  • Trigger sensitivity
  • Alarm limits

Volume-control initiation

Select VT from PBW, choose a rate expected to provide the desired VE, use FiO2 and PEEP appropriate for stabilization, and set flow high enough to meet demand and create suitable timing. A high FiO2 may be used during immediate stabilization but should be reduced when oxygenation permits.

Pressure-control initiation

Select inspiratory pressure, inspiratory time, rate, PEEP, and FiO2. Then measure exhaled VT. The pressure setting may be displayed above PEEP or as total inspiratory pressure, so the clinician must understand the ventilator convention. Pressure is adjusted according to the resulting VT and patient response.

PhysiologyInitial emphasis
Restrictive hypoxemic diseaseLower lung-protective VT, adequate rate, FiO2 and PEEP for oxygenation, pressure monitoring.
Severe airflow obstructionProtective VT, cautious rate, higher flow, shorter inspiration, longer expiration.
No major obstruction or restrictionProtective VT, rate based on VE needs, baseline PEEP and rapid oxygen titration.

Flow and I:E ratio in obstruction

In volume control, higher inspiratory flow delivers the same VT in less time, leaving more time for expiration. High rate, low flow, large VT, long inspiratory time, or inverse timing can reduce expiratory time and increase air-trapping risk.

What you are expected to know

  • Identify every element of a complete initial ventilator prescription.
  • Set volume control using PBW, VE needs, oxygenation needs, flow, and timing.
  • Set pressure control and adjust pressure to measured exhaled VT.
  • Differentiate initial priorities for restrictive hypoxemia and airflow obstruction.
  • Explain how flow and respiratory rate affect expiratory time.
C4: Calculate and adjust IPAP, EPAP, and pressure support.
Aligned competencies: 1.3.13, 1.3.14, 1.3.15, 1.3.16

Pressure support is the pressure difference

Core relationship

Pressure support = IPAP - EPAP
IPAP influences inspiratory assistance. EPAP supports oxygenation, recruitment, and airway patency. FiO2 changes inspired oxygen concentration.

Change settings according to the physiologic goal

GoalTypical coordinated adjustmentResult
Increase ventilationIncrease IPAP while EPAP remains appropriatePressure support increases.
Increase EPAP and preserve assistanceIncrease IPAP and EPAP by the same amountPressure support remains unchanged.
Improve both ventilation and oxygenationIncrease EPAP and increase IPAP by a larger amountEPAP and pressure support both increase.
Increase oxygen concentrationIncrease FiO2Pressure support does not change.

Use arithmetic to prevent unintended changes

If settings change from 13/5 to 16/8 cm H2O, pressure support remains 8 cm H2O. If settings change from 13/5 to 18/5, pressure support rises from 8 to 13 cm H2O. If EPAP rises without an equal IPAP increase, pressure support falls.

One physiologic goal may require two knob changes

Raising EPAP for oxygenation and raising IPAP by the same amount is a coordinated oxygenation adjustment. It preserves ventilation assistance rather than representing two unrelated changes.

What you are expected to know

  • Calculate pressure support from any IPAP/EPAP pair.
  • Increase ventilatory assistance without unintentionally changing EPAP.
  • Increase EPAP while preserving pressure support.
  • Select a coordinated change when both oxygenation and ventilation are inadequate.
C5: Establish alarms and safe positioning with initial support.
Aligned competencies: 1.3.17, 1.3.18

Alarm settings must be individualized

Alarm defaults should be replaced with limits based on the patient measured pressures, exhaled volumes, minute ventilation, respiratory rate, PEEP, and need for backup ventilation. The goal is to detect clinically important change without creating constant nuisance alarms.

AlarmWhat it helps detect
High pressureObstruction, secretions, coughing, bronchospasm, reduced compliance, circuit problems.
Low pressure or disconnectLeak, disconnection, loss of airway pressure, cuff or circuit problem.
Low VT or low VELeak, inadequate pressure, worsening mechanics, apnea, disconnection.
High VT or high VEExcess support, excessive effort, auto-triggering, inappropriate settings.
High rate or apneaDistress, triggering problems, loss of spontaneous breathing.

A high-pressure alarm is commonly set modestly above the stable peak pressure, often about 5-10 cm H2O higher according to patient variation and policy. It should not be placed below the normal peak, exactly at the peak, or so high that important change is missed.

Positioning is part of the initial support plan

When no contraindication is present, elevate the head of the bed about 30-45 degrees. Consider hemodynamics, spinal precautions, procedures, aspiration risk, skin protection, secretion management, and oxygenation needs. Prone positioning is selective rather than routine for every newly intubated patient.

What you are expected to know

  • Select the major alarms required at initiation and explain what each protects against.
  • Choose a reasonable high-pressure alarm relative to stable measured pressure.
  • Describe routine head elevation and the conditions that modify positioning.
End-of-reading check: You should be able to calculate PBW, VT, VE, rate, and pressure support; establish initial invasive and noninvasive settings; and set alarms and positioning for safe initiation.
