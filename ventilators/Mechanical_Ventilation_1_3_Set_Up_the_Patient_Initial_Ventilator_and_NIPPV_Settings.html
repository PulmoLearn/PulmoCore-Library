1Clinical orientation

Start With the Patient, Not the Settings

Two patients can both be intubated and need very different ventilator plans. First identify what is failing and what danger you are trying to avoid.

Patient A

Severe pneumonia

SpO₂ 82% despite high oxygen. Diffuse infiltrates. Lung compliance is likely reduced.

Primary setup concern: oxygenation plus lung protection.
Patient B

Neuromuscular weakness

SpO₂ 97% on room air. Clear lungs. Vital capacity and cough are deteriorating.

Primary setup concern: replace failing ventilatory pump function without adding unnecessary oxygen.

Before you touch the ventilator, answer these questions

Why is ventilation needed?
Oxygenation failure, ventilation failure, respiratory-pump failure, airway protection, or a combination?
What mechanics are likely?
Normal, obstructed/high resistance, or stiff/low compliance?
What was happening before intubation?
Oxygen requirement, blood gas, spontaneous effort, hemodynamics, and whether resuscitation occurred.
Clinical anchor: Diagnosis gives context. Physiology, severity, mechanics, oxygen requirement, and airway safety determine the initial settings.

2Patient size and lung protection

Predicted Body Weight Sets the Tidal-Volume Boundaries

Ventilator tidal volume is based on predicted body weight (PBW), which reflects height and sex—not the patient's actual scale weight.

Formal PBW

Male

PBW = 50 + 2.3 × (height in inches − 60)
Formal PBW

Female

PBW = 45.5 + 2.3 × (height in inches − 60)

Quick bedside estimate

For rapid mental math, this course also uses a practical estimate:

50 + 2 × (height in inches − 60), then +2 kg for males or −2 kg for females
Worked example · 68-inch male
Formal: 50 + 2.3(8) = 68.4 kg
Quick estimate: 50 + 2(8) + 2 = 68 kg

The shortcut is excellent for a bedside estimate. Use the formal PBW calculation when precision matters for lung-protective tidal-volume calculations, documentation, or exam questions.

Convert PBW into a tidal-volume range

Once you know PBW, calculate the volume that corresponds to the mL/kg strategy you are considering.

68-kg PBW
6 mL/kg = 408 mL
8 mL/kg = 544 mL
10 mL/kg = 680 mL
RTE test tip: When answer choices include tidal volumes, estimate or calculate PBW first. Eliminate any option that gives a VT above 10 mL/kg PBW. Then choose among the remaining options using the disease process, minute-ventilation need, pressures, and timing.
Do not confuse the ceiling with the target. A VT of 10 mL/kg is not a routine starting goal. Many patients—especially those with ARDS or reduced compliance—should receive substantially less.

3Build adequate ventilation

Choose Tidal Volume and Rate Together

A tidal volume and a respiratory rate are not separate choices. Together they determine minute ventilation.

Minute ventilation (V̇E) = tidal volume (VT) × respiratory rate (f)

For initial-setting practice, this course uses a quick starting estimate:

Estimated ideal V̇E ≈ 10% of PBW in L/min
This is a starting estimate, not a final prescription. PaCO₂, pH, metabolic demand, dead space, disease process, timing, and the patient's response determine the ventilation actually required.
Worked example · Solve for rate

PBW = 70 kg → estimated V̇E ≈ 7 L/min. If you select VT = 420 mL:

7,000 mL/min ÷ 420 mL = 16.7 breaths/min
A starting rate of about 17/min may be reasonable when mechanics are normal.
Worked example · COPD changes the answer

The same 70-kg patient has severe obstruction and is at risk for air trapping. A rate near 17/min may shorten expiration too much. If you want to keep the rate near 12/min:

7,000 mL/min ÷ 12/min = 583 mL
583 ÷ 70 = 8.3 mL/kg PBW

The larger VT remains below the 10 mL/kg PBW ceiling and may be a more sensible starting combination than using a high rate—provided pressures and patient response are acceptable.

The equation gives you possible settings. The disease tells you which combination makes sense.

4Disease-specific setup strategy

Same Ventilator. Different Patient.

The safest VT–rate combination, timing, FiO₂, and PEEP depend on the patient's physiology. Explore each patient type before moving on.

Explore all six patient types.
RTE pattern: The best answer usually protects the physiology causing the problem. In obstruction, protect expiration. In ARDS, protect the lung. In pump failure, replace ventilation. In hypoxemia, support oxygenation. In a room-air patient with normal lungs, do not invent an oxygen problem.
5Protect the time needed to exhale

Timing Is a Ventilator Setting Too

In obstruction, the patient may need more time to exhale than your first minute-ventilation calculation naturally provides.

Total cycle time
TCT = 60 ÷ respiratory rate
Expiratory time
Te = TCT − Ti
Worked COPD example
RR = 12/min → TCT = 5 sec
Ti = 0.8 sec → Te = 4.2 sec
Approximate I:E = 1:5.25

Compare this with RR 20/min and Ti 1 sec: TCT = 3 sec, Te = 2 sec, I:E ≈ 1:2. The first strategy gives a severely obstructed patient much more time to empty the lungs.

Obstructive starting strategy: A rate around 10–12/min and Ti around 0.75–1.0 sec can be a useful classroom starting pattern when air trapping is a major concern. These are not magic numbers. The expiratory flow waveform, pressures, gas exchange, and patient response determine whether the strategy is actually working.

6Initial oxygen strategy

FiO₂ Follows the Oxygenation Problem

An endotracheal tube does not create an oxygen requirement. Choose the starting FiO₂ from the clinical situation, then titrate to the patient's measured oxygenation target.

Room-air patient

Normal lungs / pump failure / elective ventilation

If the patient had normal oxygenation on room air and was not unstable around intubation, FiO₂ 0.21 may be appropriate.

Modest oxygen need

Stable but requiring supplemental oxygen

Use the known pre-intubation oxygen requirement as a guide. A starting FiO₂ around 0.30–0.40 may be reasonable in an appropriate stable patient.

Severe hypoxemia

Pneumonia, edema, ARDS

Start substantially higher as needed to establish safe oxygenation, then titrate promptly using reliable SpO₂/PaO₂ data.

Post-cardiac arrest

ROSC after resuscitation

Use FiO₂ 1.0 until SpO₂ or PaO₂ can be measured reliably, then titrate to avoid both hypoxemia and hyperoxemia.

RTE test tip: Do not confuse the FiO₂ used for preoxygenation or bag-mask ventilation with the FiO₂ the patient necessarily needs once stable on the ventilator.
COPD: Once reliable monitoring is available, avoid unnecessary oxygen and titrate to the ordered/appropriate target. On board-style questions, if two choices provide adequate oxygenation, the lower reasonable FiO₂ is often favored over excessive oxygen. A common acute target for patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure is SpO₂ around 88–92%.

7Build the oxygenation strategy

FiO₂ and PEEP Work Together

FiO₂ changes the oxygen concentration delivered. Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) helps keep recruitable alveoli open and can improve the lung's ability to use that oxygen.

FiO₂

Changes inspired oxygen

Increase when oxygen delivery is inadequate, then reduce unnecessary exposure as the patient stabilizes.

PEEP

Changes expiratory baseline pressure

Can recruit unstable alveoli and improve oxygenation, but may also affect venous return and blood pressure.

Initial pattern: A patient with normal lungs may need only modest baseline PEEP. A patient with severe pneumonia or ARDS may need PEEP to play a much larger role in the oxygenation strategy. Full ARDS PEEP/FiO₂ optimization is intentionally saved for Unit 2.

8Finish the breath-delivery setup

Set Flow, Inspiratory Time, and Trigger for the Patient

These variables are not finishing touches. They determine how comfortably and how quickly the breath is delivered.

Volume control

Flow influences inspiratory time

Higher inspiratory flow can deliver the selected VT faster, shortening Ti and creating more time for expiration. Flow must also meet patient demand.

Pressure control

Inspiratory time affects delivered VT

Pressure is targeted; flow and VT vary with mechanics, patient effort, pressure difference, and time available for inspiration.

Obstruction: If the patient needs more expiratory time, a shorter Ti is often useful. In volume control, higher inspiratory flow is one tool for shortening Ti without reducing the selected VT.

Trigger sensitivity

Choose a pressure or flow trigger sensitive enough to recognize real effort but not so sensitive that leak, water, or circuit motion causes auto-triggering. Remember from 1.2: intrinsic PEEP can increase the effort needed to trigger a breath.

9Patient safety

Alarms Are Part of the Prescription

Do not think of alarms as factory settings. The respiratory therapist sets limits that should detect a meaningful change in this patient's ventilation or circuit.

High pressureProtects against important increases in airway pressure from obstruction, secretions, bronchospasm, biting, tube/circuit problems, or reduced compliance.
Low pressure / disconnectHelps detect loss of circuit pressure from disconnection, cuff leak, or major system leak.
Low exhaled VTWarns that the patient may not be receiving or returning the expected volume.
High / low minute ventilationDetects a clinically important change in the total ventilation delivered each minute.
High / low respiratory rateCan reveal tachypnea, apnea, excessive triggering, or loss of spontaneous breathing depending on mode.
Apnea / PEEP / FiO₂ alarmsUse when applicable to detect loss of spontaneous drive, baseline pressure, or oxygen delivery.
Alarm-setting rule: Establish the patient's expected or observed baseline, then place limits close enough to detect clinically meaningful deterioration but wide enough to avoid constant nuisance alarms. Exact numeric defaults vary with device, mode, patient, and institutional policy.

11Initial noninvasive setup

Build NIPPV From the Same Physiology

Noninvasive positive-pressure ventilation (NIPPV) changes the interface, not the reasoning. Decide whether the patient needs recruitment, inspiratory assistance, a backup rate, or escalation to an artificial airway.

CPAP / EPAP

Recruitment and oxygenation

A single continuous pressure or the EPAP component of bilevel support raises expiratory baseline pressure and can improve alveolar recruitment.

IPAP above EPAP

Inspiratory assistance

The difference between IPAP and EPAP is the pressure-support component that assists inspiration.

Pressure support = IPAP − EPAP
Worked COPD example

Suppose bilevel starts at IPAP 12 / EPAP 5 cm H₂O.

Pressure support = 12 − 5 = 7 cm H₂O

If ventilation remains inadequate and the patient is tolerating NIV, increasing IPAP while holding EPAP constant increases pressure support and usually increases inspiratory assistance.

Worked pulmonary-edema example

If oxygenation/recruitment is the main problem and spontaneous ventilation is adequate, CPAP may be enough. If the patient also develops hypercapnia or ventilatory fatigue, bilevel support can add inspiratory assistance above the expiratory pressure.

NIV safety still comes first. A backup rate, higher IPAP, or a different mask does not make NIV safe when the patient cannot protect the airway, is vomiting, cannot manage secretions, or is otherwise failing noninvasive support.

12Clinical handoff to reassessment

The First Five Minutes on the Ventilator

Starting the ventilator is not the endpoint. Your initial settings create a new clinical question: Is this actually working for the patient?

1
Look at the patient. Chest rise, respiratory effort, comfort, mental status, color, and perfusion.
2
Verify the airway and system. Tube position, circuit connection, humidification, oxygen source, and active alarms.
3
Read the ventilator. Exhaled VT, V̇E, actual rate, PIP, Pplat when appropriate, PEEP, graphics, expiratory flow, and synchrony.
4
Check oxygenation and circulation. SpO₂, heart rate, blood pressure, and hemodynamic tolerance.
5
Plan objective reassessment. ABG when indicated, repeat clinical assessment, and airway/imaging verification as appropriate.
Carry forward to 1.4: Initial settings are hypotheses. The patient's response tells you whether to continue, modify, or escalate.

14Clinical reasoning framework

Build Every Initial Plan in the Same Order

1 · Know the patientWhy are they ventilated? What is failing? What mechanics and oxygen need are present?
2 · Size the breathCalculate PBW, select a safe VT range, and eliminate VT above 10 mL/kg PBW.
3 · Build ventilationEstimate V̇E, pair VT with rate, and make sure the disease can tolerate the timing.
4 · Build oxygenation + deliverySelect FiO₂, PEEP, flow/Ti, trigger, and alarms based on physiology.
5 · Verify and reassessLook at the patient, read actual ventilator values, check hemodynamics, and prepare to modify the first setting that is not working.
Carry forward: The math creates a starting plan. The disease constrains the plan. The patient response decides whether the plan stays.

Post-cardiac-arrest oxygen guidance follows current American Heart Association recommendations. ARDS examples use lung-protective PBW-based ventilation principles.
