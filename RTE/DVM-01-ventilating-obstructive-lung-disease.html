Ventilating Obstructive Lung Disease

In severe asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), narrowed airways slow exhalation. If a new breath begins before the last one ends, trapped gas raises pressure in the chest. Set and reassess the ventilator to allow exhalation while supporting oxygenation and safe ventilation.

Is there time to exhale?

Obstructive ventilation framework

Limit stacking → assess mechanics → reassess the patient

Why air traps

An obstructed patient needs longer to breathe out. If expiratory flow has not returned to zero when the next breath starts, gas can stack up. This dynamic hyperinflation creates intrinsic positive end-expiratory pressure (auto-PEEP).

Create exhalation time

Reduce unnecessary minute ventilation, often by lowering the set rate and using a modest tidal volume based on predicted body weight. A shorter inspiratory time, sometimes achieved with higher inspiratory flow in volume control, gives more time to exhale.

Measure what matters

Inspect the expiratory flow waveform and, when appropriate, measure auto-PEEP with an end-expiratory hold in a passive patient. Check plateau pressure with an inspiratory hold and distinguish it from peak pressure, which also rises with airway resistance.

Balance gas exchange and safety

Treat bronchospasm, mucus, and tube problems while adjusting support. A rising carbon dioxide level may be accepted within a clinician-directed plan if pH and patient status are acceptable. Recheck pressure, blood pressure, oxygenation, gases, and patient effort after changes.

Core rule: do not chase a normal carbon dioxide value by forcing breaths so rapidly that exhalation becomes incomplete. Reassess the whole patient after each change.
Pressure cue: high peak pressure with a much lower plateau pressure points toward increased airway resistance. A high plateau pressure calls for attention to lung volume, compliance, and trapped gas.
Mechanics and bedside decisions

Review each concept

Asthma and COPD narrow the airways and slow expiratory flow. Bronchospasm, secretions, and a blocked or kinked tube can make resistance worse; inspect the patient and circuit.
If expiratory flow is still moving outward when the next breath starts, the previous breath did not fully leave. This suggests air trapping and a need to review rate, inspiratory time, and delivered volume.
Intrinsic positive end-expiratory pressure (auto-PEEP) is pressure left by trapped gas at the end of exhalation. It can increase work of triggering, reduce venous return, and contribute to hypotension.
A lower respiratory rate makes each breathing cycle longer and may extend expiratory time. Check the resulting ventilation and blood gas response instead of choosing a rate in isolation.
In volume control, increasing set inspiratory flow may deliver the same tidal volume sooner and lengthen expiration. Peak pressure may rise with flow; assess plateau pressure and the patient too.
Use predicted body weight, not actual weight, to guide a modest tidal volume; assess plateau pressure and delivered volume. Avoid large breaths that take longer to exhale and add to hyperinflation.
Peak pressure includes resistive and elastic pressure. Plateau pressure measured during a passive inspiratory hold better reflects end-inspiratory alveolar pressure. Interpret both with waveforms and the bedside assessment.
External positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) can sometimes help an actively breathing COPD patient trigger the ventilator, but its effect varies. Assess total pressure, hyperinflation, and hemodynamics after changes; avoid an automatic setting for every patient.
New hypotension, rising pressures, and poor ventilation demand an immediate patient and circuit check. Look for severe air trapping, tube obstruction, and pneumothorax while coordinating urgent support.
Review expiratory flow, auto-PEEP if measurable, peak and plateau pressures, oxygenation, carbon dioxide and pH, blood pressure, and synchrony. Adjust the plan to the response.
Integrated RTE-style ventilation case

Flow does not reach baseline

An intubated adult with severe asthma is receiving volume-control ventilation:
  • Set respiratory rate is 24 breaths/min
  • Expiratory flow has not returned to zero before the next breath
  • Peak pressure is high; plateau pressure and blood pressure need review
  • Carbon dioxide is elevated without an immediate life-threatening pH change
