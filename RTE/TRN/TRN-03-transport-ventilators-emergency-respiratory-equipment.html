Transport Ventilators & Emergency Respiratory Equipment

Before a ventilated patient moves, check that the portable ventilator can provide the intended support and that the team can respond if it fails. Verify the circuit, alarms, oxygen connection, monitor, suction, airway supplies, and bag-mask ventilation setup. After changing devices, check the patient—not just the screen—and keep rescue equipment within reach throughout transport.

Is the rescue setup ready?

Equipment readiness framework

Assemble → test → rescue → reassess

Set up the transport ventilator

Confirm the prescribed mode, breath rate, tidal volume or pressure target, oxygen concentration, and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP). Check an intact circuit, secure artificial airway connection, working oxygen source, adequate battery, and the correct patient interface.

Test support and alarms

After switching to the transport device, assess chest rise, breath sounds, exhaled volume, oxygen saturation, and exhaled carbon dioxide when available. Check airway pressure and confirm that high-pressure, low-pressure, and disconnect alarms are active and audible.

Prepare immediate backup

Bring a self-inflating bag-mask device (BVM) that fits the patient, its oxygen tubing and reservoir when needed, suction that works, and airway supplies suited to the patient. Check connections before departure so manual ventilation is available if the ventilator fails.

Troubleshoot at the patient

With a low-pressure alarm and poor chest rise, check for a disconnection or leak, restore ventilation, and reassess. With a high-pressure alarm, assess kinks, secretions, obstruction, and the patient. If effective mechanical ventilation cannot be restored promptly, use the tested backup and call for help.

Core transport rule: a portable ventilator is ready only when its settings, gas, power, alarms, circuit, and patient response have all been checked.
Rescue detail: a bag-mask device without a working oxygen connection may not meet a high oxygen need. Confirm the entire backup setup and any needed PEEP option before moving.
Device checks & rescue response

Review each concept

Compare portable ventilator settings with the bedside care plan, including oxygen concentration and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP). Confirm the device can deliver the support the patient requires throughout movement.
Check tubing, filter or heat-moisture exchanger if used, adapters, airway connection, and securement. A loose connector can cause a low-pressure alarm and little delivered ventilation.
Confirm low-pressure, high-pressure, and disconnect alarms are active and audible. Set limits appropriate to the patient and check that an alarm leads to an immediate patient assessment.
After switching devices, look for chest movement and assess breath sounds, exhaled volume, oxygenation, and exhaled carbon dioxide when available. A matching screen setting does not prove the patient receives effective breaths.
Verify the portable gas connection, pressure or source status, and charged power supply. Confirm backup oxygen and battery planning for the expected trip; cylinder-duration calculations are covered in the preceding lesson.
Ensure suction powers on and draws appropriately with catheter or tubing ready. Secretions or a mucus plug can obstruct an artificial airway during transport, so suction must be accessible.
Select a size-appropriate self-inflating bag and mask or airway connector. Attach and test oxygen tubing and reservoir as needed; include a PEEP valve if clinically required and compatible with the rescue plan.
Bring supplies matched to the airway, such as a spare tracheostomy tube when indicated, and ensure staff know who manages accidental displacement. Equipment alone is insufficient without a responder who can use it.
Stop movement, assess the patient and chest rise, reconnect or replace faulty tubing, and restore effective ventilation. If the ventilator cannot rapidly be made safe, use the tested manual backup with oxygen and call for help.
After moving to the portable ventilator and again at the destination, verify airway position, circuit, settings, oxygenation, ventilation, and alarms. Report any event and response to the receiving team.
Integrated RTE-style equipment case

Restore breathing during transport

Ventilated adult during a hallway transfer:
  • Low-pressure and disconnect alarms sound
  • Exhaled volume falls sharply; chest rise is minimal
  • Oxygen saturation falls from 96% to 88%
  • A connector near the endotracheal tube has come apart
