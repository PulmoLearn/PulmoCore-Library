Preparing a Respiratory Patient for Transport

Moving a patient away from bedside support adds risk. Before transport, check whether the patient can tolerate the trip, confirm airway and ventilation, match the transport device to the care plan, and bring monitoring and rescue supplies. Tell the receiving team about current support and likely problems, ask them to repeat critical details, then reassess immediately after transfer.

Transport readiness framework

Stabilize → equip → communicate → reassess

Decide if movement is safe

Review the reason for transport, current oxygenation, ventilation, blood pressure, mental status, and recent changes. Treat new instability before a nonurgent trip. If urgent movement is necessary, bring the staff and equipment needed to manage the likely problem on the way.

Protect the airway and breathing

Check the airway device and secure connections. Match transport ventilator settings to the prescribed plan and confirm chest rise, oxygenation, alarms, and exhaled carbon dioxide when available. Bring suction and a manual resuscitation bag with an oxygen source for backup.

Confirm resources and monitoring

Verify a working oxygen supply with a reserve, charged power, functioning alarms, and continuous monitoring suited to severity. Check pulse oximetry, heart rhythm, blood pressure, and ventilator or capnography data when indicated. Make sure lines and tubes will remain secure during movement.

Close the communication loop

Name the transport lead, tell the sending and receiving teams about the airway, settings, recent trend, and rescue plan. The receiver repeats back high-risk information, and the sender confirms it. Recheck the patient after changing equipment and again on arrival.

Core transport rule: stabilize what you can, anticipate failure points, and verify support works before leaving the bedside.
Urgent exception: necessary emergency movement may proceed with active resuscitation, but the transport plan must include appropriate staff, monitoring, oxygen, and rescue capability.
Readiness, backup & handoff

Review each concept

Know where the patient is going, why the test or procedure is needed, travel time, destination readiness, and whether the trip is urgent. A longer or remote route may change staffing and equipment needs.
Compare current breathing, oxygenation, blood pressure, mental status, and ventilator needs with earlier values. New severe instability before a nonurgent trip calls for treatment and a revised transport decision.
Check tube position and securement, airway patency, tubing connections, intravenous access, and drains. A disconnected circuit or displaced tube during a transfer can quickly become an emergency.
Confirm the portable device can deliver the intended settings and oxygen concentration. After switching ventilators, check chest rise, exhaled volume when available, oxygen saturation, pressures, and patient comfort.
Confirm the oxygen source, reserve supply, and charged battery will cover the entire trip and possible delays. The next lesson covers cylinder-duration calculations; this lesson focuses on verifying readiness.
Have a manual resuscitation bag, suitable oxygen connection, suction, and appropriate airway supplies at hand. Check that the team can respond if the transport ventilator or oxygen supply fails.
Use continuous pulse oximetry and monitoring suited to patient risk, such as heart rhythm, blood pressure, and exhaled carbon dioxide for an artificial airway when indicated. Do not lose sight of the patient while moving equipment.
Identify who manages the airway, who watches monitors and lines, and who calls for help. High-risk patients need staff capable of managing foreseeable respiratory and circulatory deterioration.
At handoff, state the airway type, ventilator settings, oxygen support, recent desaturation, and rescue plan. The receiver repeats the key instructions; the sender confirms or corrects them. This completes the communication loop.
After switching to transport equipment and again on arrival, reassess chest rise, oxygenation, ventilation, blood pressure, tube connections, and alarms. Document events and hand off any new change to the receiving team.

Fix the risk before departure

Ventilated adult scheduled for a nonurgent computed tomography scan:
  • Oxygen saturation has fallen from 95% to 86% during transfer to the portable ventilator
  • The transport alarm shows low exhaled volume
  • A tubing connection is loose at the artificial airway
  • The transport team is waiting by the door
