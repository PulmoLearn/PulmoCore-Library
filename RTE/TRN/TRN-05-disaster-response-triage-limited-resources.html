Disaster Response, Triage & Limited Resources

When many patients need help at once, respiratory care starts with scene safety, rapid assessment, and a shared triage plan. Identify immediately treatable breathing threats, keep patients on the support they need, and coordinate scarce oxygen, equipment, and staff. Reassess: a patient's priority may change.

What needs attention first?

Disaster respiratory-care framework

Safety → rapid triage → treatment → reassessment

Start with safety and command

Follow the incident command and site response plan. Check for hazards, use the needed protective equipment, and confirm where patients and supplies are being directed. Do not enter an unsafe area without the trained response team.

Sort rapidly and revisit

Triage is a quick first assessment that sets the order for attention; it is not a final diagnosis. Use the adopted local triage system. Look for severe breathing trouble, poor air movement, altered mental status, and other signs of urgent need. Reassess after care and as the situation changes.

Perform immediate breathing actions

Within the response plan and your role, open or reposition an obstructed airway, provide effective ventilation when indicated, and request help. A patient with inadequate breathing needs prompt attention; changing an oxygen setting alone does not fix absent ventilation.

Use resources wisely

Check remaining oxygen, power, suction, and working equipment. Fix leaks and disconnected tubing. Titrate oxygen to the patient's ordered or protocol target and clinical response. Ask incident command for resupply early; never withhold necessary oxygen simply to save supply.

Core emergency rule: use the local triage and command plan, treat reversible respiratory threats quickly, and communicate changes in patient condition and supply.
Important distinction: formal crisis allocation of scarce ventilators follows an authorized plan. A single clinician does not invent allocation rules or remove needed support based only on arrival order or age.
Disaster response priorities

Review each concept

Identify hazards such as damaged electrical systems, smoke, or possible contamination. Use assigned protective equipment and enter only when cleared under the response plan.
Report to the designated lead, learn your assignment, and use the established route for requests and patient reports. A common command structure prevents duplicated work and missed patients.
Apply the locally adopted mass-casualty triage process. Make a quick first pass, identify immediate threats, then reassess because a patient's condition and priority can change.
Look for obstruction, absent or ineffective breaths, poor chest rise, and falling responsiveness. Perform permitted immediate airway actions and request the help needed for continued support.
Oxygen supplies oxygen to inhaled gas; ventilation moves air into and out of the lungs. If breaths are ineffective, assess airway and ventilation promptly. Turning up oxygen alone does not restore effective breaths.
Check remaining cylinder or system supply, patient demand, backup sources, and likely delays. Request replacement before the supply runs out, especially when several patients depend on it.
Correct leaks, close unused outlets, use working flowmeters, and adjust delivered oxygen to the ordered or protocol target. Continue necessary support and monitor the patient after any change.
Check which ventilators, manual resuscitators, suction units, batteries, and interfaces work and fit the patient. Send accurate counts and failures to the equipment lead instead of assuming a device is ready.
If demand exceeds available support after resupply efforts, notify incident command and follow the authorized allocation process. Bedside staff should provide appropriate care within that process, not make ad hoc allocation rules.
Share triage status, respiratory findings, treatment, response, current oxygen or ventilator support, remaining supply, and destination with the next team.
Integrated RTE-style emergency case

Act while resources are strained

After a large incident, several patients arrive while portable oxygen is limited:
  • One patient has weak breathing and poor chest rise after repositioning
  • A second patient is alert and speaking on prescribed oxygen
  • An oxygen connection is leaking; more cylinders have been requested
  • The respiratory therapist is working within the incident response plan
