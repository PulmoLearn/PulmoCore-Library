Neonatal/Pediatric & High-Risk Transport

Infants and children need equipment matched to their size and a plan for fast changes in breathing. A small breath makes bulky connectors and added tubing more significant. Newborns also lose heat quickly. Before and during transport, check airway support, delivered breaths, oxygenation, temperature, and backup supplies; stop and stabilize the patient if breathing becomes ineffective.

What needs extra protection?

Small-patient transport framework

Size → warmth → breathing → reassessment

Choose patient-size equipment

Prepare an airway interface, bag, suction catheter, sensor, and ventilator circuit suited to age and weight. Check that the transport device can deliver and measure the needed small breath size. Secure each connection and bring the correct backup sizes.

Limit added dead space

Tidal volume is the air delivered with one breath. Connectors or adapters between the airway and circuit add apparatus dead space: air that moves but does not reach gas-exchanging lung tissue. In an infant, even a few added milliliters can make a large share of the breath ineffective and raise carbon dioxide.

Protect temperature and oxygenation

For a newborn, use a warmed transport environment and monitor temperature, aiming to maintain 36.5–37.5 °C during stabilization. Select and adjust oxygen to the prescribed target, assess work of breathing and saturation, and confirm chest rise after device changes.

Prepare for rapid deterioration

Track heart rate, oxygenation, ventilation, and temperature during movement. In an intubated child with a pulse, exhaled carbon dioxide monitoring helps detect tube problems during transport. If breathing becomes ineffective, stop, give effective ventilation, call for help, and reassess before continuing.

Core transport rule: match the equipment to the patient and verify delivered ventilation after every change. A newborn with ineffective breathing needs support before a nonurgent trip continues.
Dead-space warning: adding a bulky adapter to a small circuit can reduce effective alveolar ventilation even when the ventilator displays the planned breath size.
Small-patient transport priorities

Review each concept

Confirm the airway interface, suction catheter, bag, mask, circuit, and monitoring probes fit the infant or child. Pack appropriate backup sizes and verify compatibility before the patient moves.
The transport ventilator must deliver and measure an appropriate tidal volume or pressure for the patient's size. After transfer, compare chest rise, exhaled volume, heart rate, oxygenation, and overall effort with the baseline.
Extra connectors and sensors near the airway add space where exhaled gas may remain. With a small tidal volume, added dead space can cause carbon dioxide rebreathing or poor effective ventilation; use compatible low-dead-space parts when indicated.
Use a warmed environment and monitor body temperature during preparation and transport. Keep a newborn's temperature in the normal range of 36.5–37.5 °C during admission and stabilization. A cold infant may need warming and reassessment before departure.
Set the ordered oxygen support and watch saturation, color, work of breathing, and heart rate. Adjust under protocol to the patient's clinical target. A display setting alone does not establish effective oxygenation.
For an intubated infant or child with a pulse, exhaled carbon dioxide helps monitor airway tube placement and ventilation during transport. Check the waveform or detector with the clinical picture; leaks and small breaths can affect measurements.
Confirm oxygen, battery life, warming power, alarms, and suction will last the trip and possible delay. Put spare supply and a working patient-size manual ventilation setup within reach.
Transport a fragile newborn or child with a team trained to manage the size-specific airway, ventilation, resuscitation, and temperature needs. Assign who will watch the airway, monitors, and equipment.
Poor chest rise, falling oxygenation, a slowing heart rate, or a lost exhaled carbon dioxide signal warrants an immediate pause. Check airway and circuit, provide effective ventilation if needed, and call for help before restarting movement.
Communicate weight, airway size and position, ventilator settings, oxygen support, temperature, recent heart rate and saturation trends, equipment changes, and any intervention during the trip.
Integrated RTE-style transport case

Stabilize before departure

Preterm newborn just placed in a transport incubator:
  • Breathing is weak with little chest rise
  • Heart rate falls to 88 beats per minute
  • Oxygen saturation is falling despite the planned oxygen setting
  • Temperature is 36.1 °C; the scan is nonurgent
