Acute, Chronic & Acute-on-Chronic Ventilatory Failure

Use the relationship between pH, PaCO₂, and HCO₃⁻ to decide whether CO₂ retention is new, chronically compensated, or an acute worsening on top of chronic hypercapnia.

2027 RTEI.D.5 Evaluate blood gas results
AcuteCO₂ rises before the kidneys have time to retain much bicarbonate.
ChronicRenal bicarbonate retention helps return pH toward normal.
Acute-on-chronicChronic compensation is present, but pH falls again with a new CO₂ rise.
Socratic pattern string

Is the CO₂ retention acute, chronic, or acute-on-chronic?

Focus on the combination of PaCO₂, pH, and bicarbonate—not the PaCO₂ alone.

Recognition framework

Read the compensation pattern

This lesson is about pattern recognition, not memorizing every compensation equation.

Acute respiratory acidosispH clearly low + PaCO₂ high + HCO₃⁻ normal or only slightly increased.
Chronic respiratory acidosisPaCO₂ high + HCO₃⁻ clearly elevated + pH near normal or only mildly acidemic.
Acute-on-chronicPaCO₂ high + HCO₃⁻ elevated + pH now significantly acidemic again.
Fast clue

If the bicarbonate is already elevated, the patient has had time to compensate. If the pH is still markedly acidemic despite that elevated bicarbonate, think acute worsening on top of chronic CO₂ retention.

Compare two patients

Same PaCO₂, different story

Patient A: pH 7.24 • PaCO₂ 68 • HCO₃⁻ 27
Patient B: pH 7.36 • PaCO₂ 68 • HCO₃⁻ 37
Acute-on-chronic clue

Look for chronic compensation plus new acidemia

ABG: pH 7.25 • PaCO₂ 82 • HCO₃⁻ 35
Use the ABG in context

Do not label chronic CO₂ retention as “normal”

Scenario: A patient with chronic hypercapnia has pH 7.37, PaCO₂ 60 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 34 mEq/L and is clinically at baseline.
