ABG Sampling, Specimen Quality & Errors

Decide whether a blood gas result represents the patient—or a collection, handling, or documentation problem that must be corrected before treatment decisions are made.

What went wrong with the sample?

Use the pattern of the result plus the collection history to identify the most likely preanalytic error.

Correct collection first

Build a specimen you can actually interpret

A precise analyzer cannot rescue a bad specimen.

Before the drawVerify two identifiers, the clinical question, bleeding risk, intended source/site, and the exact oxygen device plus flow or FiO₂ at the time of collection.
Radial puncture safetyConfirm an appropriate pulse and collateral circulation according to the taught procedure before using the radial site.
During collectionUse an approved preheparinized syringe. A true arterial sample should fill from arterial pressure rather than forceful aspiration.
After collectionApply firm pressure, check for hematoma/distal perfusion, remove air bubbles, cap, gently mix, label at the bedside, and transport promptly.
Modified Allen sequence

Clench hand → compress radial and ulnar arteries → open hand and observe blanching → release ulnar pressure while maintaining radial compression → observe color return. The source lesson teaches 5–7 seconds as adequate return and >15 seconds as a reason not to use that radial site.

Error fingerprints

Know what each preanalytic problem tends to do

Open each error once. The goal is not just to name it—the goal is to know whether the result should be trusted.

0 of 7 reviewedReview all specimen problems.
  • PaCO₂ tends to decrease
  • pH tends to increase
  • PaO₂ moves toward room-air equilibrium and may rise or fall depending on starting value
  • Expel bubbles immediately
  • Withhold interpretation if contamination is meaningful
  • Recollect when integrity is uncertain
An air bubble does not always raise PaO₂. It moves the sample toward ambient equilibrium.
  • PaO₂ decreases
  • PaCO₂ increases
  • pH decreases
  • Cells continue consuming O₂
  • Cells continue producing CO₂
Follow approved timing/transport procedure. The source lesson teaches prompt analysis and ice slurry when a delay is unavoidable.
  • Weak pulsatility or difficult/prolonged probing
  • Unexpectedly low PaO₂ compared with reliable SpO₂/prior arterial data
  • Possible higher CO₂ and lower pH than arterial blood
  • Verify the source and collection characteristics
  • Do not use a questionable venous sample for arterial oxygenation decisions
Venous and arterial blood are not interchangeable for oxygenation decisions.
  • Dilution may lower measured gases and electrolytes
  • Calculated bicarbonate may also be altered
  • Use approved balanced-heparin device
  • Use the required fill volume
  • Recollect correctly if dilution is suspected
More heparin is not “safer.” Too much liquid heparin can distort the specimen.
  • Unreliable values
  • May obstruct or damage the analyzer sample path
  • Do not force a clotted specimen through the analyzer
  • Reject/recollect according to policy
Gently mix the specimen as required after collection.
  • Flush solution may dilute values
  • Medication or venous admixture may distort results
  • Follow discard-volume and line-draw policy
  • Verify source
  • Recollect if integrity is uncertain
Unexpected values after a line draw should trigger a source-integrity check before treatment changes.
  • Two patient identifiers
  • Source/site
  • Time of draw
  • Oxygen device and flow/FiO₂
  • Recent oxygen or ventilator changes
  • A PaO₂ cannot be interpreted correctly without the support conditions present at the draw
  • A mislabeled result cannot safely be assigned to the patient
Do not call an ABG “room air” when the patient was receiving oxygen.
Arterial or venous?

Use the sample source that answers the clinical question

Scenario: A patient has an unexpectedly low oxygen value from a sample obtained after prolonged probing with weak pulsatility. Bedside SpO₂ and the prior arterial result do not fit the reported PaO₂.
Trust the result?

When the ABG and the patient do not agree

Scenario: A patient on room air has an SpO₂ of 84%, but the ABG reports PaO₂ 96 mmHg. A visible air bubble remained in the syringe during transport.
recommend another blood gas

When is recollection the diagnostic recommendation?

Scenario: A blood gas is clotted and the oxygen setting documented on the label does not match the device currently on the patient. The patient remains clinically stable.
