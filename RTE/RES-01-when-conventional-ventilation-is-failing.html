When Conventional Ventilation Is Failing

Rescue therapy begins with recognizing that safe conventional adjustments are no longer enough. The key pattern is persistent oxygenation or ventilation failure despite appropriate lung-protective support—or a situation where further conventional escalation would require unsafe pressures, volumes, oxygen exposure, or would worsen hemodynamics.

Is there still a safe conventional move left?

Failure-recognition framework

Refractory means “not responding safely to optimized conventional support”

Persistent hypoxemia

Your ARDS source warns that rapidly increasing FiO₂ and PEEP with a persistently low P/F ratio suggests progression to severe disease and the need for escalation planning.

Unsafe pressure requirement

If oxygenation or ventilation can only be improved by abandoning lung-protective VT or exceeding safe plateau-pressure strategy, conventional support is reaching its limit.

Worsening compliance

Your source lists worsening compliance as a danger sign because the lungs are becoming stiffer and harder to ventilate safely.

Shock or rising lactate

Your ARDS source specifically flags shock/rising lactate because oxygen delivery may be threatened even when SpO₂ looks acceptable.

Dyssynchrony that defeats protection

Your source notes that severe dyssynchrony can worsen pressures, oxygenation, and work of breathing; it must be corrected before declaring optimized conventional ventilation a failure.

Persistent ventilation failure

The registry includes refractory ventilation failure. If PaCO₂/acidemia worsen despite safe adjustments—or correcting them would require unsafe pressure/volume—escalation planning is appropriate.

Failure is not “the oxygen is low.”

First verify that conventional therapy is truly optimized: lung-protective VT, pressure limitation, appropriate PEEP/FiO₂, airway/circuit function, synchrony, hemodynamics, and treatment of the underlying cause.

Source boundary: the registry intentionally asks learners to recognize when conventional ventilation is no longer sufficient. The retrieved source does not provide one universal numeric “rescue threshold” for every diagnosis. This lesson therefore uses supported trends and safety limits rather than inventing a single cutoff.
Source-supported escalation sequence

Do the basics well before calling them “failed”

1
Assess severity and current oxygenation/ventilation.
Review ABG, FiO₂, PEEP, P/F trend, pressures, compliance, VT, waveforms, hemodynamics and synchrony.
2
Identify immediate threats.
Your ARDS source explicitly names refractory hypoxemia, shock, and unsafe pressures.
3
Optimize lung-protective conventional support.
Use low VT by PBW, pressure limitation, appropriate PEEP/FiO₂, and permissive hypercapnia when appropriate rather than injurious ventilation.
4
Correct reversible obstacles.
Check airway/circuit, secretions, pneumothorax, auto-PEEP, dyssynchrony, hemodynamics, and the underlying disease process.
5
Reassess the response.
If P/F ratio, oxygen requirement, plateau pressure, compliance, PaCO₂/pH, or hemodynamics continue to worsen, conventional support may be failing.
6
Escalate to the appropriate rescue pathway.
Your source lists proning, paralysis, inhaled pulmonary vasodilator, and ECMO referral as rescue options when ordered/appropriate. Later RES lessons teach these separately.
