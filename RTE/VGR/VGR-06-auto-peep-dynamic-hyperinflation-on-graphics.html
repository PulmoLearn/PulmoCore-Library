Auto-PEEP & Dynamic Hyperinflation on Graphics

The clearest graphic clue is expiratory flow that has not returned to baseline when the next inspiration begins. Pair that pattern with pressure behavior, patient effort, rate, timing, hemodynamics, and obstruction clues to recognize intrinsic PEEP and choose a safe correction.

Socratic graphics string

Did the lungs finish emptying?

Auto-PEEP graphics framework

Flow pattern → intrinsic pressure → patient effect → correction

1. Look at expiratory flow.

Your source explicitly identifies expiratory flow that remains below baseline when the next breath begins as incomplete exhalation.

2. Interpret what that means.

The lungs did not finish emptying. Gas remains trapped, end-expiratory lung volume rises, and intrinsic PEEP develops.

3. Look for the burden on the patient.

Intrinsic pressure increases the effort needed to trigger the next breath. Missed efforts or visible trigger work strengthen the interpretation.

4. Look for clinical consequences.

Your source case pairs incomplete exhalation with a high total rate, prolonged expiration, and falling blood pressure.

The waveform is the evidence—not just the diagnosis label.

For shared I.C.10 and I.D.11, learners must gather the relevant monitoring information correctly, interpret what the flow pattern means, and then choose/reassess a ventilator strategy that creates enough expiratory time and treats the cause.

Source boundary: the retrieved PulmoLearn materials do not provide a step-by-step expiratory-hold measurement procedure for intrinsic PEEP. This lesson therefore does not invent one. It teaches the source-supported graphical determination using expiratory-flow pattern, pressure burden, patient effort, timing, and response to intervention.

Your source states that auto-PEEP develops when the next breath begins before the previous breath has fully exhaled.
Gas trapping increases end-expiratory lung volume and creates intrinsic pressure.
This is why a high respiratory rate can paradoxically worsen hypercapnia in an already air-trapping patient: less expiratory time produces more trapped gas rather than more effective emptying.
Your source contributors: COPD/asthma, high RR, large VT, long Ti or low inspiratory flow, bronchospasm, and secretions.
VGR-06 does not re-teach all time constants from MVS-06. It uses these causes to explain why the graphic abnormality is present.
The source explains that trapped gas creates intrinsic end-expiratory pressure.
That pressure is clinically important even when the most obvious graphic clue is on the flow-time waveform, because the patient must first overcome intrinsic pressure before a patient-triggered breath can begin.
Repeated effort deflections without delivered breaths may represent ineffective triggering.
If expiratory flow is also failing to return to baseline, the source framework strongly supports auto-PEEP as the trigger barrier rather than simply an insensitive trigger setting.
Your source progressive case pairs incomplete exhalation with a total rate of 26/min and falling blood pressure.
The source warning says a faster rate can worsen auto-PEEP, hypotension, and CO₂ retention.
RTE move: a waveform showing air trapping plus hemodynamic deterioration demands correction—not merely a higher alarm limit or a faster rate.
Source-supported correction: increase expiratory time by reducing mandatory rate and/or shortening inspiratory time.
Treat bronchospasm and secretions when present. In volume control, increasing inspiratory flow can shorten Ti when appropriate.
Then reassess whether expiratory flow now reaches baseline, blood pressure improves, triggering is easier, and the clinical ventilation problem is improving.
