Reading Pressure, Flow & Volume Waveforms

Waveforms are a continuous record of pressure, flow, volume, time, and patient effort. Read them in the same order every time: trigger, inspiration, exhalation, pressure trend, volume return, then match the graphics to the patient.

Which waveform tells you what?

PulmoLearn reading routine

Use the same sequence every time

1. Is the patient triggering?

Look for a deflection before inspiration or evidence that effort occurred without a delivered breath.

2. Is inspiration smooth and adequate?

Look for a smooth inspiratory contour rather than scooping or distortion.

3. Is exhalation complete?

On the flow-time waveform, expiratory flow should return to baseline before the next inspiration.

4. Are pressures changing?

Trend peak pressure and, when measured, plateau pressure. A changed pressure pattern narrows the problem but does not identify the bedside cause by itself.

5. Does volume return?

Volume rises during inspiration and should return to baseline during exhalation. Incomplete return suggests lost exhaled volume/leak.

6. Do graphics match the patient?

Your source stresses that waveform recognition is only useful when paired with patient assessment and a safe next action.

Waveforms are not decorative.

Your source notes they may change before an alarm, oxygen desaturation, or a new ABG. The exam is usually testing whether you can connect the shape to physiology and choose what to do next.

Normal reference

Build the mental picture first

Pressure-time

Pressure rises during inspiration and returns to baseline after the breath. PIP is the peak during flowing inspiration; Pplat requires a no-flow inspiratory hold.

Flow-time

Inspiratory flow is above baseline and expiratory flow below baseline. Normal exhalation returns to baseline before the next breath.

Volume-time

Volume rises during inspiration and returns to baseline during exhalation when measured exhaled volume returns appropriately.

Your source defines PIP as including pressure required to overcome resistance plus pressure needed to expand lung/chest wall.
Pplat is measured during an inspiratory hold at zero flow and more closely reflects alveolar/static load.
Keep the deep differential for VGR-03: VGR-01 only teaches that a pressure change is abnormal and should be integrated with PIP/Pplat trend and bedside assessment.

Incomplete exhalation

The next inspiration begins while expiratory flow is still below baseline.
Your source identifies this pattern as incomplete exhalation and dynamic hyperinflation/auto-PEEP. VGR-06 will teach the full graphics interpretation.
The source reading routine says inspiration should be smooth and adequate.
Your source later uses inspiratory scooping as a clue that patient inspiratory demand may exceed delivered flow.
Detailed flow-starvation diagnosis is intentionally reserved for VGR-05.

Incomplete volume return

Exhaled volume remains above the baseline rather than returning fully.
Your source lists cuff leak, loose circuit connection, bronchopleural fistula, chest-tube air leak, and intentional NIV leak as possible causes.
The waveform identifies lost exhaled volume. Your source says to pair it with exhaled VT, cuff pressure, audible leak, tube depth and circuit inspection before selecting the intervention.
Your source combines pressure deflections, incomplete expiratory flow, and normal volume return to distinguish ineffective triggering from a major leak.
A normal volume return makes a large leak less likely in that source case, while incomplete expiratory flow supports air trapping.
Your source warns against naming a waveform abnormality without assessing the patient.
Examples it names for incomplete exhalation include COPD, bronchospasm, high respiratory rate, excessive VT, or short expiratory time.
Framework: waveform identifies the problem; bedside assessment identifies the cause; then choose the safest intervention.
