Triggering Problems: Ineffective Effort & Autotriggering

Triggering is the start of the breath. The ventilator must detect patient effort soon enough to assist—but not so easily that leaks, water, movement, or cardiac oscillations create breaths the patient never requested.

Who actually started the breath?

Triggering framework

Effort → detection → breath

Normal patient-triggered breath

The patient creates a pressure or flow signal that crosses the set trigger threshold, and the ventilator delivers the breath promptly.

Trigger delay

The breath eventually occurs, but the patient must work too long or too hard before the trigger is recognized. An insensitive pressure trigger can contribute because a more negative setting requires greater patient effort.

Ineffective effort

The patient makes an inspiratory effort, but no ventilator breath follows.

Autotriggering

The ventilator delivers a breath without true patient inspiratory effort.

The trigger must be sensitive—but not too sensitive.

Your PulmoLearn source states that the trigger should respond promptly to true patient effort without allowing random circuit disturbances to initiate breaths.

Visual reference

Recognize the trigger mismatch

Effective patient trigger

Small patient-trigger deflection followed promptly by a delivered breath.

Ineffective effort

Patient-effort deflections occur, but some are not followed by a ventilator breath.

Autotriggering

Repeated breaths occur without a visible patient-trigger signal. Confirm with the patient before labeling the cause.

Clinical cue: if patient effort is real and there is no auto-PEEP or other mechanical barrier, an insensitive trigger may contribute to delay or missed efforts.

Source causes: auto-PEEP, an insensitive trigger, respiratory muscle weakness, excessive sedation, or excessive support.
The graphic finding is not enough by itself. Your source's COPD case uses incomplete expiratory flow as the clue that auto-PEEP—not just trigger sensitivity—is the dominant barrier.
Your source explains that with auto-PEEP, the patient must first overcome the trapped intrinsic pressure before the ventilator can sense the effort.
In that scenario, simply making the trigger more sensitive may not fix the underlying problem.
Source-supported correction: increase expiratory time by reducing rate, shortening inspiratory time, or increasing inspiratory flow as appropriate, thereby reducing auto-PEEP and trigger burden.
Source causes: overly sensitive settings, circuit leaks, condensation, cardiac oscillations, or movement.
The first question is whether the patient actually initiated the breath. If not, inspect the trigger setting and the circuit/patient for false trigger signals.
Making a trigger easier to activate can help an insensitive-trigger problem.
But an overly sensitive trigger can produce autotriggering from leak, condensation, cardiac oscillation, or movement.
Your source's central rule is therefore prompt response to real effort without response to random disturbances.
For ineffective effort, assess auto-PEEP, trigger sensitivity, weakness, sedation, and support level.
For autotriggering, assess trigger sensitivity, circuit leak, condensation, cardiac oscillations, and movement.
RTE move: adjust the variable causing the dyssynchrony, then reassess triggering rather than changing multiple settings without a clear cause.
