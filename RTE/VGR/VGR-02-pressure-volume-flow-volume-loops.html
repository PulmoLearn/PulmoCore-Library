Pressure-Volume & Flow-Volume Loops

Loops show relationships rather than one variable over time. Pressure-volume loops are especially useful for compliance and overdistention. Flow-volume loops help reveal obstruction and lost volume. Interpret the loop, then confirm the cause at the bedside.

Loop framework

Shape → meaning → patient correlation → safest adjustment

Pressure-volume loop

Use the slope and left/right shift to judge whether the same pressure is producing more or less volume.

Flow-volume loop

Use inspiratory/expiratory contour and whether the loop closes to identify obstructive flow limitation or lost measured volume.

Compare with the patient's own prior loop

Your source explicitly says trends are often more useful than comparing a single loop with an idealized textbook shape.

Do not treat the loop name alone

Your source warns that the loop must be interpreted with plateau pressure, oxygenation, hemodynamics, breath sounds, secretions, and the disease process.

VGR-02 teaches loop recognition, not the entire mechanics differential.

Detailed pressure/resistance/secretion discrimination belongs to VGR-03, and detailed auto-PEEP graphics belong to VGR-06. Here the goal is to recognize the loop pattern and choose a safe, source-supported next step.

PV: reduced compliance

Source pattern: loop shifts right and becomes flatter → more pressure needed for the same volume → compliance worsened.

PV: overdistention

Upper flattening or “beaking” means additional pressure is producing little additional volume.

FV: airflow obstruction

Source pattern: expiratory limb scoops inward and expiration is prolonged → airflow obstruction.

FV: leak / non-closure

Your source says a leak is suggested when the flow-volume loop does not close.

Your source states that when the loop shifts right and becomes flatter, more pressure is required for the same volume, indicating worsening compliance.
When recruitment or another intervention improves alveolar participation, the source says the loop may become steeper and shift left because the same pressure now produces more volume.
Your source defines upper beaking as a point where added pressure produces little added volume because lung units are approaching their limit.
Possible source contributors include excessive VT, excessive inspiratory pressure, excessive PEEP in already-open alveoli, and heterogeneous lung disease.
Source action: reduce VT or inspiratory pressure and reassess when the clinical context supports overdistention; do not automatically increase PEEP.
Your source says improved alveolar participation after PEEP, prone positioning, secretion clearance, or recruitment may make the PV loop steeper and shift it left.
But the lesson also says to reassess SpO₂/FiO₂ requirement, plateau/driving pressure, blood pressure/cardiac output, exhaled VT, and comfort.
A prettier loop is not enough: if oxygenation improves but blood pressure falls substantially, the intervention was not fully successful.
Your source states that an inward-scooped expiratory limb with prolonged expiratory flow suggests airflow obstruction such as bronchospasm or COPD.
Bedside examination separates bronchospasm from secretion burden: diffuse wheeze with little secretion supports bronchospasm; coarse rhonchi/sawtooth with secretion return supports secretions.
The source's “right graphic for the question” table says flow-volume loop or volume-time waveform is useful for evaluating volume leak.
On the flow-volume loop, failure of the loop to close suggests lost measured volume/leak.
Confirm with exhaled VT, cuff/circuit assessment, audible leak, tube position, or other bedside data before choosing the fix.
