Analyzer QC: Blood Gas, CO-Oximetry & Gas Measurement

Blood gas, CO-oximetry, and delivered-gas results guide urgent care only when the specimen and measuring system are reliable. Check collection and handling, analyzer controls and calibration status, setup and gas supply, and whether the value fits the patient. If a required check fails, hold affected results, assess the patient, use a valid alternative, and troubleshoot before reporting.

Is the sample or system the problem?

Analyzer trust framework

Sample integrity → control status → setup → clinical fit → release

Check the specimen

Confirm patient identity, sample type, collection site and time, current oxygen or ventilator setting, and handling. Air bubbles, clots, line-fluid contamination, poor mixing, and delays can distort blood gas results. Reject or recollect a compromised specimen according to policy; do not correct it by guessing.

Check analyzer performance

Review required quality-control (QC) values, calibration and verification status, reagent or cartridge condition, alarms, and maintenance. A failed control or unverified calibration means affected results should not be released until the problem is investigated and corrected.

Know what is measured

A blood gas analyzer measures pH and gas pressures; some reported values are calculated. CO-oximetry (multiwavelength hemoximetry) measures hemoglobin fractions, including carboxyhemoglobin when available. A standard two-wavelength pulse oximeter cannot reliably rule out carbon monoxide exposure.

Verify delivered gas

If measured oxygen concentration differs from the set value, assess the patient, then check the gas source, connections, blending or device setup, analyzer sensor and its approved calibration. Use a functioning backup measurement if needed and communicate discrepancies.

Release rule: a plausible number is not proof of a valid test. Confirm specimen quality, instrument QC, and clinical context before reporting; hold affected results and use a validated alternative when required checks fail.
Clinical cue: treat the patient, not an isolated display. During a suspected carbon monoxide exposure, a reassuring ordinary pulse-oximeter saturation does not exclude high carboxyhemoglobin; use appropriate blood CO-oximetry and urgent clinical evaluation.
Specimen, device, and QC decisions

Review each concept

Verify identity, arterial or other sample type, collection time, and the oxygen or ventilator setting at sampling. A value without its collection context can be misinterpreted.
Check a blood gas specimen for air bubbles and clots before analysis. Follow collection instructions for prompt bubble removal, sealing, and gentle mixing; recollect if integrity cannot be assured.
Analyze within the validated handling window and check whether an arterial line specimen was diluted by flush solution. A delayed or contaminated sample should not be reported as a trustworthy patient value.
Controls test a known material against acceptable limits. Check the correct level and status for each measured parameter. A passed self-check does not automatically resolve a failed external QC control.
Follow device-specific calibration and verification instructions after relevant changes or failures. Review expired supplies, cartridge installation, sensor drift, gas standards, and analyzer messages.
Laboratory CO-oximetry uses multiple wavelengths to measure hemoglobin fractions. A conventional pulse oximeter can read deceptively well when carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin; obtain the appropriate test when exposure is suspected.
If an oxygen analyzer disagrees with a blender setting, assess the patient and compare with a verified analyzer, then inspect gas supplies, hose connections, blender setup, sensor and calibration. Do not trust the dial alone.
Compare with the patient's exam, trend, pulse oximetry limitations, and other data. Look for sample mix-up, contamination, incorrect support documentation, or a QC problem before acting on an implausible value.
With failed QC or unresolved sample error, hold affected results and follow escalation policy. Use a validated alternate analyzer or recollect a new sample as indicated while patient assessment and needed care continue.
Record the failed control or specimen problem, instrument and lot information where required, troubleshooting, repeat checks, affected patient results, and the final validated release decision.
Integrated RTE-style analyzer case

Resolve two independent problems

A patient has an urgent blood gas request:
  • The first syringe has a visible air bubble and its handling time is uncertain
  • A required control on the blood gas analyzer is outside its limits
  • A second validated analyzer is available
  • The patient still needs immediate clinical assessment
