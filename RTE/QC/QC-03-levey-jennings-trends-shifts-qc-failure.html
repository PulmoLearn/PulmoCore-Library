Levey-Jennings, Trends, Shifts & QC Failure

A Levey-Jennings chart plots known control results over time around a mean and standard-deviation (SD) lines. A scattered outlier may suggest random error; a sustained run on one side suggests a shift; gradual movement in one direction suggests a trend. Apply the laboratory's quality-control (QC) rules, investigate warning patterns, and hold affected patient results when a rejection rule or required limit fails.

Socratic control-chart string

What pattern is forming?

Control-chart interpretation framework

Read the mean → notice the pattern → apply the rule → protect results

Read the chart

The horizontal axis shows runs or time. The vertical axis shows the control result, with a mean and lines at set numbers of SD above and below. Compare each result with its own control level's accepted mean and limits.

Separate random and systematic clues

An isolated extreme point suggests an outlier or random problem. A cluster that abruptly moves to one side of the mean suggests a shift; a sequence that steadily climbs or falls suggests a trend. Either pattern can warn of systematic error even before a single point crosses a limit.

Apply the approved rule set

A single point beyond 2 SD is commonly treated as a warning in a multirule system, not automatically a failed run. A point beyond 3 SD or two consecutive results beyond 2 SD on the same side may meet rejection criteria when those rules are in the lab's plan. Use the local rule set and control levels.

Investigate and document

When a rejection rule or required acceptance limit fails, hold affected patient results. Check control preparation and lot, reagent or sensor changes, calibration, maintenance, and operator steps. Document the cause, corrective action, successful repeat controls, and review of affected results.

Core rule: an in-range point can be part of a concerning trend or shift. Do not erase a failed control or keep repeating it until one result passes; investigate the pattern and follow the approved QC plan.
Rule cue: not every lab uses the same control limits or multirule set. In the examples below, the lab's plan treats one result beyond 2 SD as a warning and one beyond 3 SD or two consecutive beyond 2 SD on the same side as rejection.
Patterns and safe actions

Review each concept

Results are plotted by time or run against a target mean and SD lines. Look at the sequence rather than only the most recent point, and use the correct chart for each control level.
SD shows expected spread around a stable mean. A point farther from the mean is less typical, but the lab's decision rule determines whether it is a warning or a rejection.
One result far from a previously steady cluster may be random error, such as a preparation or aspiration problem. Check its distance from limits and investigate by the approved rule.
After a stable baseline, several values grouped on one side of the mean suggest a new systematic offset. Consider a new reagent lot, calibration change, sensor, or maintenance event.
A run of results steadily increasing or decreasing points toward drift. Review the cause early even if the newest result has not crossed a rejection limit.
In a common multirule design, one point beyond 2 SD triggers further inspection, not automatic rejection. Check other control levels and previous runs against the rules your lab actually uses.
Under the example rule set, one result beyond 3 SD or two results beyond 2 SD on the same side calls for rejecting the run. Your lab's actual thresholds govern decisions.
Look at control material, lot and expiration, mixing, reagent change, calibration, maintenance, temperature, and instrument alerts. Avoid changing several factors at once without recording them.
For rejected QC, stop reporting affected patient tests and use an approved alternative if urgent care needs a result. Determine which prior results may be affected and follow the lab's review process.
Record the pattern, rule, investigation, correction, repeat controls, and the decision to resume testing. Preserve failed values for traceability rather than removing them from the chart.
