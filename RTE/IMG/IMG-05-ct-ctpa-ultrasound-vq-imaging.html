CT, CTPA, Ultrasound & V/Q Imaging

Different imaging studies answer different respiratory questions. Computed tomography (CT) shows detailed lung and chest anatomy. CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) examines pulmonary arteries with intravenous contrast. Ultrasound evaluates findings at the bedside; a ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) scan compares air movement with blood flow. Start with the clinical question and patient stability.

Which study answers the question?

Imaging-method framework

Clinical question → modality → result → next action

CT: detailed structures

A chest CT uses multiple X-ray slices to show lung tissue, airways, pleura, and nearby structures in more detail than a plain film. The clinical question determines whether a noncontrast study or a contrast-enhanced protocol is appropriate.

CTPA: pulmonary arteries

CTPA uses intravenous iodinated contrast timed to the pulmonary arteries to look for pulmonary embolism (PE). Review the patient’s clinical probability and local diagnostic pathway, as well as contrast history, kidney function, stability for transport, and image quality.

Ultrasound: bedside pleura and lung

Thoracic ultrasound can help detect and map pleural fluid and assess selected lung or pleural findings at the bedside. It can guide a pleural procedure. Interpretation depends on the operator, acoustic window, and the suspected condition.

V/Q and selected fluoroscopy

A V/Q study compares ventilation and perfusion; a segmental perfusion defect with relatively preserved ventilation supports PE in the right context. When CTPA cannot be performed, it may be an alternative. Fluoroscopy can show diaphragm motion during a targeted sniff test.

Core rule: recommend an additional imaging study when its answer would change care. Check whether the patient can safely undergo the test and whether the result addresses the suspected problem.
Result cue: a normal or nondiagnostic study does not settle every clinical question. Reassess the probability of disease, quality of the study, and any ongoing deterioration with the team.
Matching method to question

Review each concept

CT can clarify a lung lesion, complex infection, interstitial disease, or pleural finding that remains unclear on a radiograph. Match the protocol and contrast choice to the clinical question.
CTPA times iodinated contrast to the pulmonary arteries. A filling defect in a pulmonary artery can support acute PE; a study with poor contrast timing may be nondiagnostic.
Consider signs, probability, and laboratory or imaging results in the relevant clinical pathway. An imaging test should answer a live question rather than replace initial assessment.
Before CTPA, review iodinated-contrast reactions, kidney function when relevant, pregnancy considerations, and whether the patient can be transported safely. The team chooses the best feasible study.
The V/Q scan compares how air reaches lung regions with how blood reaches them. A segmental perfusion defect with relatively preserved ventilation supports vascular obstruction such as PE.
When ventilation and perfusion are both reduced in the same area, lung disease is one possible explanation. Interpret the entire scan with a recent chest radiograph and clinical probability.
V/Q imaging may be considered when CT contrast is contraindicated or the patient cannot undergo CTPA, if the scan is feasible and the results will change management.
Bedside ultrasound can confirm and locate pleural fluid and help plan a safe procedure. A supine radiograph may underestimate or obscure small fluid collections.
Ultrasound can show findings at the pleural surface, including fluid, consolidation, or signs relevant to pneumothorax. Deeper abnormalities hidden behind air may not be seen.
A targeted fluoroscopic sniff study can show the direction of diaphragm movement when weakness or paralysis is suspected. It is not a general test for PE or pleural fluid.

A PE question when CT contrast is unsuitable

A stable adult develops sudden shortness of breath after a period of immobility:
  • The care team’s clinical assessment raises concern for pulmonary embolism
  • A chest radiograph does not explain the new hypoxemia
  • The record documents a prior severe reaction to iodinated CT contrast
