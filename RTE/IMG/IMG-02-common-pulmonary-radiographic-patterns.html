Common Pulmonary Radiographic Patterns

Many conditions make a lung look white or dark on a chest radiograph. Look for volume loss, a shift in nearby structures, heart size, pleural fluid, and diaphragm position. Then compare earlier images and the patient’s symptoms. A pattern narrows the possibilities; it does not name the cause by itself.

What does the pattern suggest?

Pattern-reading framework

Opacity → volume → direction of shift → patient

Opacity with little volume loss

Consolidation fills air spaces with material such as inflammatory fluid. An air bronchogram may show dark, air-filled bronchi within a denser area. Infection is one possible cause, so match the image to fever, cough, and exam.

Opacity with volume loss

Atelectasis means part of a lung has less air and shrinks. Look for crowding of vessels or ribs, a raised diaphragm, and a fissure or mediastinum drawn toward the affected side. A central obstruction is one possible cause.

Diffuse opacity and cardiac clues

Bilateral lung opacities may reflect edema or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), among other causes. Heart enlargement, vascular congestion, and pleural fluid can favor cardiogenic edema, but a portable view and clinical findings matter.

Dark lungs and displaced structures

Hyperinflation often shows low, flatter diaphragms and increased lung volume. A shift toward an opaque side supports volume loss; a large pleural collection can push away. Check image rotation before concluding a true shift.

Core rule: compare the image pattern, image quality, prior studies, and current breathing. A sudden new opacity or shift with worsening oxygenation needs prompt bedside assessment and escalation.
Direction cue: volume loss pulls structures toward the affected lung. A large space-occupying pleural process can push them away. Rotation can imitate either appearance, so check technique first.
Recognizing the major patterns

Review each concept

An opacity is a whiter area on a radiograph. State its location and whether it is new, then look for lung volume change and nearby findings before suggesting a cause.
An ill-defined or lobar opacity with little volume loss may reflect consolidation. Air bronchograms can support air-space disease; pneumonia is possible when symptoms and history fit.
Atelectasis often combines increased density with loss of lung volume. A raised diaphragm, shifted fissure, or mediastinum pulled toward the affected side adds support.
Volume loss may pull the trachea or mediastinum toward the involved side. A large pleural fluid or air collection can push it away. First rule out patient rotation.
Bilateral central or diffuse opacities with vascular congestion, enlarged heart appearance, or pleural fluid can support cardiogenic edema. Consider the view and current cardiac findings.
Acute bilateral opacities and severe low oxygen after a known insult can fit ARDS. The image alone does not establish the diagnosis; exclude findings fully explained by fluid overload or collapse.
Low, flattened diaphragms, increased lung volume, and a relatively narrow heart outline can suggest hyperinflation. Compare with prior studies and the clinical history.
A larger cardiac silhouette may reflect heart enlargement, but a portable anteroposterior (AP) view and low inspiration can magnify its appearance. Compare the view and prior films.
A raised hemidiaphragm can occur with volume loss or other causes. Flattening can reflect hyperinflation. Note asymmetry and check the rest of the chest before assigning a cause.
Compare onset, oxygen need, breath sounds, fever, fluid status, and prior imaging. Report new severe hypoxemia or hemodynamic compromise promptly even when the image is not definitive.

New opacity and a shift

A ventilated adult develops rising oxygen needs and reduced right-sided breath sounds:
  • The new radiograph has a dense right upper-lung opacity
  • The right upper fissure and right hilum are drawn upward compared with yesterday
  • The patient is not rotated; the right upper lung has lost volume
