Pleural & Air-Leak Findings on Imaging

Air or fluid in the pleural space can compress a lung and change breathing quickly. A chest image may show a pleural line, a deep angle, a fluid layer, or air in the chest wall. Use the image with breath sounds, oxygenation, blood pressure, and the clinical history. Treat suspected tension physiology as an emergency.

What is happening in the pleural space?

Pleural image framework

Air or fluid → position → patient stability → response

Air around a lung

An upright radiograph may show a thin visceral pleural line with no lung markings beyond it: a pneumothorax. In a patient lying flat, air may collect near the front or base; a very deep, dark costophrenic angle is a possible clue.

Tension is a clinical emergency

A pneumothorax with worsening breath sounds, low oxygen, and falling blood pressure may impair venous return. Mediastinal shift can support the concern but is neither required nor specific. Alert the team and follow the emergency pathway without delaying for another image.

Fluid around a lung

An upright pleural effusion often blunts the costophrenic angle and can form a curved upper border. In a supine patient, fluid may layer behind the lung and make the whole side hazy. Ultrasound can clarify suspected fluid.

Blood or air outside the pleura

After chest injury, a pleural fluid collection with shock raises concern for hemothorax, but plain film cannot reliably distinguish blood from other fluid. Streaks of air in chest-wall soft tissues suggest subcutaneous emphysema; assess for a related air leak.

Core rule: check the patient first when breathing or circulation worsens. A pleural line, deep sulcus, fluid layer, or chest-wall air guides assessment; image appearance alone does not prove tension or blood in the pleural space.
Position cue: air and fluid shift with body position. Supine portable films can hide a small pneumothorax or effusion; ultrasound or other imaging may help when the patient is stable enough and the result would change care.
Air and fluid around the lung

Review each concept

A visible visceral pleural line with absent lung markings beyond it supports pneumothorax. Do not mistake a skin fold or large bulla for pleural air; compare the line and patient.
In a supine film, pleural air may not rise to the apex. An unusually deep and dark costophrenic angle can be a clue to a hidden pneumothorax.
Sudden low oxygen, one-sided quiet breath sounds, falling blood pressure, and poor perfusion after an air leak raise concern for tension. Escalate urgently even if a film is pending or does not show a large shift.
An upright effusion may blunt an angle and form a meniscus, a curved fluid border. In a supine patient, layering fluid may look like broad haze rather than a neat curve.
A large pleural collection may compress the nearby lung and push the mediastinum away. Compare rotation and other causes of shift; a small effusion need not produce displacement.
Pleural fluid after an injury plus falling blood pressure raises concern for blood in the chest. A plain radiograph identifies a collection but cannot alone prove its contents.
Air in the chest-wall tissue may appear as dark streaks outlining tissue planes. Check the patient for crackling under the skin and assess for an associated pleural or airway leak.
Air outlining central chest structures can suggest pneumomediastinum. It may follow an air leak or procedure; report it with symptoms and possible associated pneumothorax.
Bedside ultrasound may clarify pleural air or fluid when the radiograph is limited, especially in a supine patient. A stable patient may need further imaging; suspected tension needs immediate clinical action.
Review injury or procedure history, prior films, oxygenation, breath sounds, blood pressure, and chest-drain findings when present. Report new deterioration and reassess after intervention.

Sudden decline on a ventilator

  • Left breath sounds are much quieter than before, and chest movement is uneven
  • A recent portable film showed a new left pleural line without markings beyond it
  • Current pressure is 76/42 mm Hg; a new image is not yet available
