Systematic Chest X-Ray Review

A chest radiograph can show important changes in the lungs, pleura, heart, and support devices. Read every image in the same order. First check whose image it is and whether its position or quality limits interpretation. Then compare with earlier images and the patient’s current breathing.

What does the image add to assessment?

Chest radiograph review framework

Identity and quality → anatomy → devices → patient

Verify the study and quality

Confirm patient, date, view, and position. A bedside anteroposterior (AP) film can magnify the heart compared with a standard posteroanterior (PA) view. Check rotation, depth of inspiration, exposure, and motion before calling a change real.

Trace airway and soft tissues

Follow the trachea toward the carina. Check for displacement, then inspect the neck, chest wall, ribs, spine, and soft tissues. Remember that rotation can make the mediastinum look shifted.

Review heart, lungs, and borders

Inspect heart and mediastinal outline, hila, and both lungs from top to base. Compare right and left, looking for new opacity, unusual lucency, and loss of expected borders. Consider technique and prior films.

Finish pleura, diaphragm, and devices

Look at pleural edges and costophrenic angles for air or fluid. Check diaphragm height and contour. Trace each visible tube and line to its tip and assess the patient if its position is concerning.

Core rule: use a fixed scan order and compare the image with prior studies and the patient. Escalate an urgent finding or deterioration even when the portable image is limited.
Quality cue: low inspiration can crowd normal lung markings and mimic basilar opacity. Rotation can alter the apparent trachea and heart position. A portable AP view can make the heart look larger.
A repeatable image-reading sequence

Review each concept

Check identifiers, date, time, and prior image. A current clinical change should be compared with the most relevant earlier study, not an unrelated film.
A portable AP image can make the heart look larger. A PA upright image differs from a supine portable one, so avoid claiming new heart enlargement from view alone.
Unequal distance from each clavicle to the spine suggests rotation. Low inspiration crowds lower lung markings. Exposure and motion may hide or imitate findings.
Inspect the trachea, carina, and main bronchi. Consider a true shift only after checking rotation and signs in the lungs or pleura that fit the direction.
Scan clavicles, ribs, shoulders, spine, and visible soft tissue. An unexpected fracture, subcutaneous air, or artifact can change the clinical reading.
Compare heart and mediastinal outlines with prior studies and account for the AP or PA view. An enlarged appearance on a portable AP film does not alone prove heart enlargement.
Sweep each lung from apices to bases, comparing sides. Mark any new dense area, unusual dark region, or altered lung marking and connect it to symptoms.
Check for a visible pleural line with missing peripheral lung markings or blunted costophrenic angles. Match the finding with breathing, oxygenation, and stability.
Inspect both hemidiaphragms, costophrenic angles, and the area behind the heart. Low lung volume can change diaphragm position and the appearance of bases.
Follow the full visible path and tip of each airway or vascular device. A possible misplaced endotracheal tube with new one-sided breath findings requires immediate clinical assessment and team notification.
