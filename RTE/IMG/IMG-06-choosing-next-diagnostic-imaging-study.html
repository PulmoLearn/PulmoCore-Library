Choosing the Next Diagnostic Imaging Study

Choose imaging for a specific unanswered question, not just because a patient is short of breath. Consider what earlier studies showed, how fast the patient is changing, and whether transport or contrast is safe. If a patient has signs of a life-threatening problem, start the emergency response instead of waiting for another image.

Next-study decision framework

Urgency → question → feasible study → meaningful result

First: will imaging delay urgent care?

Assess oxygenation, work of breathing, blood pressure, and perfusion. A patient with sudden unilateral breath-sound loss and shock may need an emergency response for suspected tension pneumothorax; do not wait for transport or a repeat radiograph.

Second: name the suspected target

A chest radiograph can show broad lung changes and devices. Computed tomography (CT) gives more anatomic detail. CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) examines pulmonary arteries when pulmonary embolism (PE) is suspected through an appropriate clinical pathway.

Third: match bedside options

Thoracic ultrasound helps clarify pleural fluid and guide aspiration, and it can assist in selected pneumothorax assessments. It is useful when transport is difficult but has operator and field-of-view limits.

Fourth: weigh limits and alternatives

A ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) scan may help assess PE when CTPA cannot be done and the scan is feasible. Review contrast reactions, kidney function when relevant, pregnancy considerations, transport risk, and prior-image quality before making a recommendation.

Core rule: recommend imaging when its expected result will answer a question and change care. A nondiagnostic first image, unstable patient, or known test limitation changes which study is next—or whether imaging must wait.
Question cue: PE in pulmonary arteries → consider CTPA or a feasible alternative; uncertain pleural fluid before drainage → ultrasound; persistent unexplained focal change → consider targeted CT. Suspected tension with shock → emergency action now.
From presentation to recommendation

Review each concept

New shock, severe low oxygen, or rapidly failing ventilation calls for bedside assessment and escalation. A test should not hold up time-critical support or treatment.
Read the most recent image, report, quality limits, and clinical trend. Repeating a film may answer a new line-position question but will not directly confirm every suspected vascular disease.
If clinical assessment makes PE likely and imaging is needed, discuss a pulmonary artery study rather than an ordinary noncontrast chest CT. The team follows the relevant probability and diagnostic pathway.
A history of severe iodinated-contrast reaction or other contraindication affects use of CTPA. Discuss the risk and a feasible alternative such as V/Q imaging with the ordering team.
Ultrasound can show whether fluid is present and where it can be sampled. This is useful when a portable film is uncertain or a pleural procedure is being planned.
A small pneumothorax may be missed on a supine film. Bedside ultrasound or further imaging can clarify stable uncertainty; suspected tension physiology with shock needs action without waiting.
A concerning opacity or recurrent localized finding may require a targeted chest CT to assess anatomy. The specific protocol depends on the suspected cause and radiology guidance.
If the radiograph and symptoms leave diffuse lung disease unclear, a selected CT protocol may add detail. Do not treat a generic CT order as interchangeable with a pulmonary angiogram.
Before CT or V/Q scanning, check whether oxygen delivery, monitoring, staff, and hemodynamic support can be maintained away from the bedside.
State what the next study should confirm or exclude and how a positive, negative, or nondiagnostic result will alter the next clinical decision.

A pleural question before a procedure

A stable adult has increasing breathlessness and a new hazy right base on a supine film:
  • The report cannot distinguish pleural fluid from nearby lung opacity
  • Breath sounds are reduced at the right base; blood pressure is stable
  • The team is considering a pleural fluid sample
