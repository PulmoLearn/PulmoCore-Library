Extubation & Immediate Post-Extubation Assessment

The work is not finished when the tube comes out. Immediately reassess breathing pattern, oxygenation/ventilation, upper-airway sounds, secretion clearance, work of breathing, mental status, and hemodynamics so early deterioration is recognized before it becomes a rescue airway emergency.

What does the first change after tube removal mean?

Immediate assessment framework

Airway → breathing → gas exchange → clearance → trajectory

Airway: listen for stridor and assess retractions or other signs of upper-airway compromise.
Breathing: watch RR, depth, pattern, accessory-muscle use, air movement, and fatigue.
Gas exchange: reassess SpO₂/oxygenation and ventilation when indicated; a normal SpO₂ does not exclude hypercapnia or fatigue.
Secretion clearance: determine whether the patient can cough and clear secretions without the ETT.
Mental status/hemodynamics: new confusion, somnolence, hypotension, or instability can signal worsening respiratory failure.
Trend: improvement is not completion; continued abnormalities require ongoing reassessment and escalation when needed.
Post-extubation stridor localizes the problem to the upper airway.
Your PulmoLearn breakout guide specifically links inspiratory stridor and retractions after extubation with upper-airway obstruction/laryngeal edema and says this can worsen rapidly.
Immediate reassessment domains

Review each topic

Reassess respiratory rate and depth, accessory-muscle use, chest movement, air entry, fatigue, and whether breathing is becoming easier or more labored after extubation.
Reassess SpO₂/oxygenation and ventilation targets that match the original problem. If concern exists for hypoventilation, rising CO₂, or acidemia, evaluate ventilation rather than relying on SpO₂ alone.
Inspiratory stridor with retractions after extubation localizes the problem to the upper airway and may represent laryngeal edema. Your source says upper-airway compromise can worsen rapidly and requires immediate airway-focused evaluation and preparation for advanced support.
Assess cough effectiveness, secretion burden, oral secretion management, and whether the patient can maintain airway clearance without the artificial airway.
New agitation, confusion, or somnolence can accompany hypoxemia, hypercapnia, fatigue, or airway compromise and should be interpreted with the whole respiratory picture.
Reassess heart rate, blood pressure, and perfusion. Deterioration with worsening respiratory distress raises urgency and may signal failed liberation.
After extubation, the first priority is maintaining a patent airway. Stridor, retractions, inability to protect the airway, or copious secretions require immediate airway-focused assessment and escalation.
Successful liberation means the patient continues to sustain adequate spontaneous breathing after the tube is removed. Worsening oxygenation/ventilation, fatigue, airway compromise, or secretion failure means the post-extubation support plan must change.
Integrated RTE-style case

Recognize the dominant immediate threat

10 minutes after extubation:
  • Inspiratory stridor
  • Suprasternal retractions
  • Increasing work of breathing
  • SpO₂ falling despite oxygen
  • Patient is becoming anxious
