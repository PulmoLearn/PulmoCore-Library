Extubation Readiness: Airway, Cough & Secretions

Passing an SBT does not automatically mean the endotracheal tube should come out. Before extubation, evaluate whether the patient can maintain a patent upper airway, cough effectively, manage secretions, remain alert enough to protect the airway, and tolerate removal of the artificial airway without an unacceptable edema/obstruction risk.

Extubation-readiness framework

SBT success + airway readiness = safer extubation decision

1. Confirm SBT tolerance.

The shared I.D.12 mapping matters: extubation readiness starts after the patient has shown they can sustain spontaneous breathing well enough to move forward.

2. Evaluate airway patency.

Consider upper-airway obstruction/edema risk and whether removal of the tube could expose an airway that cannot stay open.

3. Evaluate cough.

The patient must be able to generate an effective cough or otherwise manage secretions once the artificial airway is gone.

4. Evaluate secretion burden.

Frequent suctioning needs, copious secretions, or ineffective clearance increase extubation risk even if ventilation is adequate.

5. Evaluate mental status and protection.

Ask whether the patient can remain alert enough to manage the airway, respond appropriately, and protect against aspiration.

6. Integrate the whole picture.

Airway readiness is not a single test. A favorable SBT does not override an unsafe airway, poor cough, heavy secretions, or inadequate protection.

Passing the SBT answers “Can they breathe?” Extubation readiness also asks “Can they maintain and protect the airway?”
Source boundary: the registry requires airway edema risk and extubation readiness, and the PulmoLearn lesson registry names the cuff-leak check as part of the extubation procedure set. The retrieved sources do not provide a universal cuff-leak volume/percentage cutoff or a complete steroid/timing protocol, so this lesson does not invent one.
Extubation-readiness domains

Review each topic

A tolerated SBT supports moving to extubation-readiness assessment. It does not prove the upper airway is safe or the patient can clear secretions after tube removal.
The registry explicitly requires assessment of airway patency before extubation. Consider whether upper-airway obstruction, swelling, anatomy, or another airway issue could become clinically important once the tube is removed.
The existing PulmoLearn liberation procedure content includes a cuff-leak check and post-extubation stridor as extubation topics. Use the cuff-leak result as one clue about upper-airway patency/edema risk, not as an absolute stand-alone guarantee. The retrieved sources do not provide a universal numeric cutoff.
A patient may ventilate adequately yet still fail after extubation if they cannot generate an effective cough. Observe whether the cough is strong enough to mobilize and clear secretions without the endotracheal tube.
Evaluate amount, thickness, frequency of required suctioning, and whether the patient can clear secretions independently. A high secretion burden combined with weak cough raises extubation risk.
The registry specifically includes mental status and the ability to protect the airway. Worsening somnolence, inability to manage oral secretions, or unreliable protective behavior weighs against extubation.
A patient may have acceptable SpO₂ and still be unsafe to extubate because of airway-obstruction risk, poor cough, heavy secretions, or inadequate protection.
If SBT tolerance and airway readiness are favorable, proceed toward extubation using the appropriate protocol. If airway readiness is unfavorable, correct reversible problems and reassess rather than removing the tube simply because breathing mechanics look good.
