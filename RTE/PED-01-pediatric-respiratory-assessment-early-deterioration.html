Pediatric Respiratory Assessment & Early Deterioration

Children often compensate for respiratory illness before they fail. Assess appearance and behavior before touching the child, then connect age-appropriate respiratory rate, work of breathing, air movement, oxygenation, feeding or speech, mental status, and fatigue. The most dangerous change may be a tired child who looks quieter—not better.

Assessment framework

Observe → listen → measure → trend → escalate

Observe before disturbing

Note position, color, interaction, tone, cry or speech, respiratory pattern, retractions, nasal flaring, head bobbing, and ability to feed or play.

Listen and assess air movement

Identify stridor, grunting, wheeze, crackles, or diminished breath sounds. Loud noise is not the only danger; a quieter chest with worsening effort may mean less airflow.

Measure in context

Count a full respiratory rate when irregular, compare with the age-appropriate range and baseline, and correlate heart rate, SpO₂ signal, temperature, perfusion, and mental status.

Trend compensation

Tachypnea and increased effort may be early compensation. Slowing or irregular respirations, poor effort, decreased interaction, cyanosis, bradycardia, or exhaustion are late and urgent findings.

Core pediatric rule: less visible struggle does not always mean improvement. If a child with prolonged distress becomes quiet, less responsive, bradypneic, or has poorer air movement, suspect fatigue and impending respiratory failure.
Age matters: a respiratory rate must be interpreted against age, state, fever, pain, and trend. Do not label tachypnea from one adult cutoff or from a count taken while a child is crying.
Clinical assessment & early warning

Review each concept

Watch whether the child tracks the caregiver, makes eye contact, moves normally, has an age-appropriate cry or speech, and can be consoled. Irritability may be early hypoxemia; lethargy, poor tone, or decreased responsiveness are more ominous.
Observe the chest or abdomen while the child is calm when possible. Count a full minute when the pattern is irregular. Compare with the age-appropriate range, temperature, activity, and prior rate. A rising rate may signal compensation; a falling rate with worsening status may signal fatigue.
Nasal flaring, head bobbing, tracheal tugging, accessory-muscle use, and suprasternal, intercostal, or subcostal retractions indicate increased effort. More extensive or deeper retractions generally mean more severe distress.
Stridor suggests upper-airway narrowing. Wheeze suggests lower-airway obstruction. Grunting attempts to maintain end-expiratory pressure. Crackles may accompany parenchymal or fluid processes. Diminished or absent sounds can mean poor airflow and severe disease.
Ask what the child can do now compared with baseline. Pausing during feeds, sweating with feeds, fewer wet diapers, inability to speak in normal phrases, a weak cry, or inability to play because of breathlessness are meaningful deterioration cues.
Central cyanosis is late and urgent. Pulse oximetry adds objective trend data, but validate the signal, pulse-rate match, probe position, motion, and perfusion. Supplemental oxygen may preserve SpO₂ while ventilation and fatigue worsen.
Common early findings include tachypnea, tachycardia, nasal flaring, retractions, grunting, abnormal breath sounds, anxiety or irritability, and reduced feeding or activity. Recognize the pattern before oxygenation or mental status deteriorates.
Late findings include altered mental status, exhaustion, poor tone, bradypnea or irregular respirations, weak effort, decreased air movement, cyanosis despite support, bradycardia, and apnea. These require immediate escalation and support of airway, oxygenation, and ventilation.
Ask onset and progression, fever, cough, choking or sudden onset, noisy breathing, apnea or color change, feeding/fluid intake, urine output, sleep, activity, prior episodes, medications and response, prematurity, chronic heart/lung/neuromuscular disease, and known baseline.
After positioning, suction, oxygen, medication, or respiratory support, repeat the same measures: appearance, rate, effort, air movement, SpO₂, heart rate, feeding/speech, and mental status. Improvement must be demonstrated across the patient—not assumed from one number.
