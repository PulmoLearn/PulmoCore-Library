Differentiate the major gas-exchange mechanisms by what is wrong, what the clinical clues look like, and how the hypoxemia responds to supplemental oxygen.

Socratic gas-exchange string

Which mechanism best fits?

Use ventilation, perfusion, and oxygen response—not disease names alone.

Core framework

Ventilation and perfusion must meet

Normal gas exchange depends on matching air reaching alveoli with blood reaching those alveoli.

Normal V/QVentilation and perfusion are appropriately matched. Neither low-V/Q nor high-V/Q regions dominate.
Low V/QVentilation is reduced relative to perfusion. Blood leaves those units under-oxygenated. Supplemental oxygen usually improves the problem because some ventilation is still present.
High V/QVentilation is present but perfusion is reduced. This behaves like increased wasted ventilation / dead-space physiology rather than a pure shunt.
ShuntBlood reaches arterial circulation without contacting ventilated alveoli. Large shunts respond poorly to increased FiO₂ because the shunted blood never sees the added oxygen.
Diffusion limitationReduced surface area, increased membrane thickness, or shortened capillary transit time limits equilibration. It often appears first with exercise and generally improves with supplemental oxygen.
A-a gradient clueAn elevated A-a gradient points toward impaired transfer such as V/Q mismatch, diffusion limitation, or shunt rather than isolated hypoventilation or low inspired oxygen.
Fast discriminator

Improves well with oxygen? Think V/Q mismatch or diffusion. Improves poorly despite high FiO₂? Think large shunt.

The poorly ventilated units still receive some gas, so increasing inspired oxygen can raise alveolar oxygen in those units.
  • Ventilation maintained
  • Perfusion reduced
  • Ventilation is being “wasted” because too little blood is available for gas exchange
As perfusion approaches zero, the unit behaves more like alveolar dead space. GX-03 will go deeper into dead-space physiology.
  • Blood bypasses ventilated alveoli or perfuses nonventilated units
  • Shunted blood never sees added alveolar oxygen
  • Elevated A-a gradient
  • Large shunt = limited response to high FiO₂
Refractory hypoxemia despite substantial oxygen should make you think beyond simple low V/Q.
  • Surface area ↓
  • Membrane thickness ↑
  • Transit time shortened
  • May appear first during exercise
  • Usually improves with supplemental oxygen
Faster capillary transit leaves less time for oxygen to equilibrate across an impaired membrane.
  • Low inspired oxygen
  • Isolated alveolar hypoventilation
  • V/Q mismatch
  • Diffusion limitation
  • Shunt
The A-a gradient organizes the problem; oxygen response helps distinguish the mechanism further.

Refractory hypoxemia

When high FiO₂ does not fix the problem

Scenario: A patient remains markedly hypoxemic despite a large increase in FiO₂. Imaging and clinical findings suggest alveoli are perfused but not effectively ventilated.
Exercise clue

When oxygen transfer fails first during exertion

Scenario: Resting oxygenation is near acceptable, but hypoxemia develops with exercise in a patient with reduced gas-exchange surface area. Supplemental oxygen improves the exercise desaturation.
Integrated RTE-style mechanism check

Put A-a gradient and oxygen response together

At sea level on room air:
  • PaCO₂ = 40 mmHg
  • Estimated PAO₂ ≈ 100 mmHg
  • PaO₂ = 55 mmHg
  • A-a gradient ≈ 45 mmHg
  • PaO₂ rises markedly after supplemental oxygen
