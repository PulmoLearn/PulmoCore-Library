Pediatric Asthma & Severe Exacerbation

An asthma exacerbation combines lower-airway muscle tightening, inflammation, and mucus. Severity is judged by the whole child: speech, work of breathing, air entry, oxygenation, alertness, and response to treatment. A fading wheeze can be dangerous when airflow is worsening. Rapid treatment and repeated reassessment determine whether to continue, space, or escalate respiratory care.

Care-plan framework

Assess → treat → reassess → escalate

Grade severity

Assess speech, position, respiratory rate, accessory-muscle use, air entry, wheeze, oxygen saturation, heart rate, alertness, and peak expiratory flow when the child can perform it reliably.

Start treatment

Give a short-acting beta agonist promptly. Add ipratropium for a severe exacerbation, give systemic corticosteroids early, and provide oxygen to meet the prescribed target.

Measure the response

After each treatment cycle, repeat the same severity findings. Improvement in speech, retractions, air entry, oxygen need, and peak flow is more useful than wheeze intensity alone.

Escalate by physiology

For a poor response, consider continuous bronchodilator therapy, intravenous magnesium sulfate, and critical-care support. Prepare for ventilatory support when exhaustion or inadequate ventilation develops.

Core severe-asthma rule: a normal or rising carbon dioxide level in a tachypneic child can signal fatigue and failing ventilation, especially when air entry or alertness worsens.
Do not wait for silence: a quiet chest, weak effort, confusion, cyanosis, or decreasing responsiveness requires immediate escalation. Avoid sedatives and do not delay treatment for nonessential testing.
Severity, treatment & escalation

Review each concept

Bronchospasm narrows the airways quickly, while inflammation and mucus add obstruction. The result is prolonged exhalation, wheeze, air trapping, increased work of breathing, and uneven ventilation. Severe narrowing can reduce wheeze because too little air is moving.
Compare speech, position, retractions, respiratory rate, heart rate, air entry, oxygen saturation, alertness, and peak expiratory flow when reliable. Inability to speak, poor air entry, exhaustion, or altered responsiveness signals life-threatening disease.
A short-acting beta agonist such as albuterol is the first inhaled bronchodilator for an acute exacerbation. Give repeated doses or continuous therapy according to severity, then reassess airflow, effort, oxygenation, and adverse effects.
Ipratropium adds bronchodilation through a different receptor pathway. Combine it with a short-acting beta agonist during the initial treatment of a severe exacerbation rather than using it as the only bronchodilator.
Systemic corticosteroids treat airway inflammation but do not work as quickly as inhaled bronchodilators. Give them early in a moderate or severe exacerbation so their effect begins while repeated bronchodilator treatment continues.
Provide supplemental oxygen for hypoxemia and titrate to the prescribed range. A normal saturation does not rule out severe obstruction, and oxygen does not correct bronchospasm or failing ventilation.
Repeat the same objective findings after treatment. If speech, air entry, retractions, oxygen need, or peak flow improves, therapy may be spaced. Little improvement or renewed distress supports continued or escalated treatment.
Severe symptoms that persist despite initial therapy may require continuous albuterol, intravenous magnesium sulfate, and intensive monitoring. Escalate location and support based on the child's response rather than waiting for complete decompensation.
A quiet chest, weakening effort, drowsiness, cyanosis, or worsening air entry is dangerous. A rising arterial carbon dioxide level can show that the child can no longer maintain the high ventilation expected during severe distress.
Call critical-care and airway experts early when ventilation is failing. Noninvasive support may help selected cooperative children, but worsening exhaustion, mental status, or gas exchange requires preparation for controlled intubation and mechanical ventilation.
Integrated RTE-style asthma case

Recognize treatment failure

Ten-year-old with a severe asthma exacerbation:
  • Continuous albuterol, ipratropium, oxygen, and systemic corticosteroid given
  • Retractions are now weaker, but air entry is markedly reduced
  • The child can no longer speak and is becoming drowsy
  • Arterial carbon dioxide rose from 32 to 48 mm Hg
  • Oxygen saturation remains 90% despite supplemental oxygen
