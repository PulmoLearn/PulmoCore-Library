Croup, Epiglottitis & Upper-Airway Emergencies

Stridor signals narrowing of the upper airway. The sound, onset, associated symptoms, and response to treatment help distinguish viral croup from epiglottitis, bacterial tracheitis, or an inhaled foreign body. Care must match the threat: treat airway swelling, limit agitation, watch for failing airflow, and secure expert airway support early when obstruction may progress.

Upper-airway framework

Recognize → grade → stabilize → protect

Recognize the pattern

Note whether symptoms began gradually or suddenly. Compare barking cough and hoarseness with drooling, painful swallowing, muffled voice, toxic appearance, thick secretions, or a choking event.

Grade the obstruction

Stridor only when upset is less severe than stridor at rest. Also assess retractions, air entry, color, alertness, oxygenation, fatigue, and the child's ability to speak or drink.

Limit agitation

Let the child remain upright with a caregiver when possible. Use the least upsetting oxygen method the child tolerates and avoid unnecessary procedures that can worsen obstruction.

Protect the airway

For suspected epiglottitis, severe obstruction, or deterioration, activate experienced airway and anesthesia support. Prepare for airway management in a controlled setting with rescue equipment ready.

Core upper-airway rule: a child who becomes quieter while air entry, alertness, or effort worsens may be losing airflow—not improving.
Suspected epiglottitis: do not force the child supine or inspect the throat with a tongue blade. Keep the child calm and arrange controlled airway evaluation.
Recognition, treatment & airway protection

Review each concept

Croup usually follows a viral prodrome and causes a barking cough, hoarse voice, and inspiratory stridor. Symptoms often worsen at night. Fever is commonly low grade, and the child is usually not toxic appearing.
Stridor only with crying or activity suggests milder disease. Stridor at rest, marked retractions, agitation, poor air entry, cyanosis, or decreasing alertness signals more severe obstruction and a need for urgent treatment or escalation.
A single dose of dexamethasone reduces airway inflammation and lowers return visits across croup severity levels. Choose an appropriate route based on the child's condition and ability to tolerate medication.
Nebulized epinephrine rapidly improves moderate or severe croup, including stridor at rest. Because the benefit is temporary, continue observation and reassess for recurrent stridor, retractions, poor air entry, or fatigue.
Humidified mist has not shown reliable benefit for croup. Avoid forcing an upset child into procedures or positions unless urgently required. Calm observation often gives a clearer picture of baseline stridor and effort.
Abrupt high fever, severe sore throat, drooling, painful swallowing, muffled voice, tripod positioning, and toxic appearance suggest epiglottitis. A barking cough is usually absent, and deterioration can be rapid.
Keep a child with suspected epiglottitis calm, upright, and with a caregiver when possible. Do not use a tongue blade for a bedside throat examination. Activate expert teams for controlled airway management.
High fever, toxic appearance, stridor, cough, and thick airway secretions may indicate bacterial tracheitis. Poor response to standard croup treatment increases concern. Airway clearance, antibiotics, and airway support may be needed.
A sudden choking episode followed by stridor, cough, voice change, or asymmetric breath sounds suggests foreign-body aspiration. The absence of fever or a viral prodrome supports this possibility and warrants urgent airway evaluation.
Worsening air entry, exhaustion, cyanosis, altered mental status, weak stridor, or decreasing effort can precede arrest. Escalate immediately; a quieter child may have critically reduced airflow rather than clinical improvement.
