Pediatric Mechanical Ventilation & Dead-Space Considerations

Small pediatric tidal volumes make every milliliter important. Gas compressed in the circuit may never reach the child, while adapters, sensors, and heat-moisture exchangers can add apparatus dead space that must be rebreathed. Safe ventilation requires weight-scaled targets, an appropriately sized airway and circuit, confirmation of exhaled tidal volume, pressure monitoring, and reassessment of gas exchange after each change.

Pediatric ventilation framework

Scale → account → verify → adjust

Scale to body size

Set and report tidal volume in milliliters per kilogram using the appropriate body-weight method. Physiologic volumes are commonly near 6-8 mL/kg, with lower volumes used when needed to stay within protective pressure limits.

Account for the circuit

Some ventilator volume compresses the breathing circuit instead of entering the lung. Verify circuit-compliance compensation and compare the set volume with the exhaled volume measured as close to the patient as practical.

Limit added dead space

Every connector between the endotracheal tube and circuit adds rebreathed volume. Use pediatric components, remove unnecessary adapters, and balance the value of a proximal sensor against the dead space it adds.

Protect the lung and airway

Monitor exhaled volume, peak and plateau pressures, positive end-expiratory pressure, waveforms, oxygenation, carbon dioxide, synchrony, hemodynamics, and endotracheal-tube patency after initiation and adjustment.

Core pediatric ventilation rule: the set tidal volume is not automatically the volume reaching the child. Verify actual exhaled volume after circuit compression, leaks, and apparatus effects.
Small airway, large effect: resistance rises sharply as endotracheal-tube diameter decreases. Check for kinking, secretions, biting, water, or tube obstruction before simply increasing pressure.
Volume, pressure, circuit & airway

Review each concept

Document tidal volume (VT) in mL/kg rather than as an isolated number. For pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome, 6-8 mL/kg is a common physiologic range; use a lower VT when needed to stay within protective plateau and driving-pressure limits.
Volume control targets a set VT while airway pressure changes with resistance and compliance. Pressure control limits inspiratory pressure while delivered VT changes. Neither mode removes the need to monitor exhaled VT, pressures, and gas exchange.
Pressure expands a compliant circuit and compresses gas within it. In a small child, that lost volume can be a large part of the set VT. Perform the required circuit test and verify that compliance compensation is active and appropriate.
Apparatus dead space is gas between the airway and the fresh-gas circuit that is rebreathed without gas exchange. Extra elbows, adapters, sensors, and heat-moisture exchangers can increase carbon dioxide, especially when VT is small.
A proximal flow sensor can improve measurement of exhaled VT near the endotracheal tube, but it also adds dead space and resistance. Use an appropriately sized sensor and remove unnecessary components from the airway connection.
A narrow endotracheal tube creates greater resistance than a larger tube, and a small amount of mucus or a kink can increase it further. Rising peak pressure with unchanged plateau pressure suggests an airway-resistance problem.
Fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) primarily support oxygenation. Adjust them with the oxygen target, lung recruitment, pressure limits, compliance, and hemodynamic response in mind.
Minute ventilation depends on VT and respiratory rate. When carbon dioxide rises, first verify delivered VT, dead space, airway patency, leaks, and synchrony. Then adjust rate or VT without exceeding lung-protective pressure goals.
Obstructive disease and a narrow airway can slow exhalation. If expiratory flow does not return to baseline before the next breath, reduce air trapping by allowing more expiratory time and reassessing intrinsic PEEP.
After any change, reassess the child, endotracheal tube, circuit, humidification, ventilator, waveforms, exhaled VT, pressures, oxygenation, carbon dioxide, synchrony, and hemodynamics. Correct equipment problems before assuming the disease worsened.
Integrated RTE-style ventilation case

Find the missing tidal volume

Six-kilogram infant on volume-controlled ventilation:
  • Set VT is 42 mL, or 7 mL/kg
  • Ventilator exhaled VT repeatedly reads 25 mL
  • Carbon dioxide is rising despite the ordered rate
  • Long compliant circuit was changed just before deterioration
  • Circuit-compliance compensation is not active
