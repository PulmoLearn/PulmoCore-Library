Foreign Body Aspiration & Pediatric Airway Obstruction

Aspiration may cause complete airway obstruction within seconds or leave a child with persistent cough, focal wheeze, or decreased breath sounds. The choking event may be unwitnessed, and a normal examination or chest radiograph does not exclude a retained object. Care depends on whether the child can move air, where the object is located, and whether emergency airway action or bronchoscopy is required.

Airway and procedure framework

Recognize → respond → remove → reassess

Decide if air is moving

An effective cough, speech, or crying indicates some airflow. A silent cough, inability to speak or cry, cyanosis, decreasing responsiveness, or little air movement indicates severe obstruction requiring immediate action.

Use age-specific choking care

For severe obstruction, alternate five back blows with five chest thrusts for an infant, or five back blows with five abdominal thrusts for a child. Begin cardiopulmonary resuscitation if unresponsive.

Plan definitive removal

Persistent focal findings or a convincing choking history require specialty evaluation even with normal imaging. Rigid bronchoscopy usually provides airway control and definitive removal of an aspirated object.

Support the procedure

Prepare oxygenation, ventilation, suction, monitoring, and emergency-airway equipment. During and after bronchoscopy, watch for hypoxemia, bronchospasm, bleeding, laryngospasm, pneumothorax, or recurrent obstruction.

Core aspiration rule: a normal chest examination or radiograph does not rule out an inhaled foreign body when the history or focal findings remain concerning.
Avoid blind removal: never perform a blind finger sweep. When opening the airway during cardiopulmonary resuscitation, remove an object only if it is clearly visible.
Recognition, emergency action & bronchoscopy

Review each concept

Sudden choking, coughing, gagging, or breathing difficulty during eating or play strongly suggests aspiration. The event may be brief or unwitnessed, and symptoms can temporarily improve even while the object remains in the airway.
An object near the larynx or trachea may cause stridor, voice change, or severe central obstruction. A bronchial object more often causes focal wheeze, asymmetric chest movement, unilateral decreased breath sounds, or localized air trapping.
If the child can cough forcefully, speak, or cry, encourage coughing and observe closely. Do not interfere with an effective cough or reach blindly into the mouth. Be ready to act if airflow becomes inadequate.
For a responsive infant with severe foreign-body airway obstruction, alternate five back blows with five chest thrusts. Continue until the object is expelled or the infant becomes unresponsive. Do not use abdominal thrusts in an infant.
For a responsive child with severe foreign-body airway obstruction, alternate five back blows with five abdominal thrusts. Continue until the object is expelled or the child becomes unresponsive.
Activate emergency response and begin cardiopulmonary resuscitation with chest compressions. Each time the airway is opened for breaths, remove a visible object. Do not perform a blind finger sweep.
Chest imaging may show unilateral air trapping, atelectasis, or consolidation, but many inhaled objects are not radiopaque and a chest radiograph may appear normal. A convincing history or persistent focal findings still requires specialty evaluation.
Rigid bronchoscopy is generally preferred for definitive pediatric foreign-body removal because it supports ventilation and accepts larger retrieval tools. Flexible bronchoscopy may help inspect distal airways or evaluate uncertain cases, depending on expertise and the clinical plan.
Verify the procedural plan and required equipment. Prepare oxygen, ventilation, suction, retrieval tools, specimen containers, and emergency-airway supplies. Monitor heart rate, rhythm, oxygen saturation, ventilation, and the child's clinical response throughout.
Repeat breath sounds, chest movement, respiratory effort, oxygenation, and ventilation. Watch for stridor, laryngospasm, bronchospasm, bleeding, fever, pneumothorax, or persistent focal findings that could indicate a fragment or complication.
