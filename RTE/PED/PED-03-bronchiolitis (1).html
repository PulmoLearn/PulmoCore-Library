Bronchiolitis

Bronchiolitis is an acute viral illness of the small airways, most often affecting infants and children younger than two years. Edema, mucus, and cellular debris narrow the bronchioles, producing tachypnea, retractions, crackles or wheeze, feeding difficulty, and sometimes apnea. Treatment is primarily supportive: maintain oxygenation, hydration, nasal patency, and ventilation while avoiding low-value routine therapies.

Support, avoid, or escalate?

Care-plan framework

Assess → support → reassess → escalate

Assess severity

Evaluate respiratory rate, retractions, air movement, apnea, oxygen saturation, hydration, feeding, mental status, and fatigue. Consider prematurity, young age, and cardiopulmonary or neuromuscular disease.

Support basics

Keep the airway positioned, clear obstructing nasal secretions when they impair breathing or feeding, provide oxygen for persistent hypoxemia, and protect hydration and nutrition.

Avoid routine low-value care

Typical bronchiolitis does not routinely require albuterol, epinephrine, systemic corticosteroids, antibiotics, chest physiotherapy, laboratory testing, or chest radiography.

Escalate by response

If standard oxygen and basic support do not reduce hypoxemia or work of breathing, consider heated high-flow nasal cannula. Escalate further for apnea, exhaustion, worsening gas exchange, or inadequate ventilation.

Core bronchiolitis rule: treat the infant's physiology, not the noise. Reassess work of breathing, oxygen need, hydration, feeding, apnea, and fatigue after each intervention.
Oxygen threshold: persistent oxygen saturation below about 90% commonly supports supplemental oxygen in otherwise typical bronchiolitis. Follow the ordered target and clinical context, especially for high-risk children.
Recognition, supportive care & escalation

Review each concept

Bronchiolitis usually begins with rhinorrhea, congestion, or cough and progresses to tachypnea, retractions, crackles, wheeze, feeding difficulty, or apnea. The diagnosis is primarily clinical in a child younger than two years.
Look for apnea, persistent hypoxemia, severe retractions, poor air movement, exhaustion, dehydration, or altered responsiveness. Risk increases with very young age, prematurity, chronic lung disease, significant heart disease, immunodeficiency, or neuromuscular weakness.
Gentle superficial suction can help when nasal secretions interfere with breathing, sleep, or feeding. Suction before feeding when needed. Avoid repeated deep suction as routine therapy because it can cause trauma, swelling, distress, and transient hypoxemia.
Start low-flow oxygen when saturation remains below the prescribed threshold after signal verification and basic airway care. Titrate to the ordered target rather than chasing brief dips or the highest possible saturation.
Heated high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) is reasonable rescue support when low-flow oxygen does not correct hypoxemia or work of breathing remains significant. Reassess respiratory rate, retractions, heart rate, oxygen need, air movement, and comfort soon after initiation.
Worsening apnea, fatigue, mental status, gas exchange, oxygen need, or work of breathing despite HFNC requires prompt escalation. Do not let an acceptable saturation on high oxygen conceal inadequate ventilation or exhaustion.
Tachypnea and nasal obstruction can make feeding unsafe or ineffective. Use smaller frequent feeds when appropriate. If oral intake is inadequate or aspiration risk is high, consider nasogastric or intravenous fluids according to clinical status and local practice.
Albuterol, nebulized epinephrine, and systemic corticosteroids do not routinely improve typical bronchiolitis outcomes. Do not continue a therapy without a clearly documented objective response or another diagnosis that supports its use.
Typical bronchiolitis usually does not require chest radiography, viral testing, blood tests, or antibiotics. Consider additional testing when the course is atypical, severe, focal, prolonged, or suggests another diagnosis or complication.
After suction, oxygen, fluids, or HFNC, repeat respiratory rate, effort, air movement, saturation, heart rate, feeding, hydration, apnea, and mental status. Continue, wean, or escalate support based on objective response.
