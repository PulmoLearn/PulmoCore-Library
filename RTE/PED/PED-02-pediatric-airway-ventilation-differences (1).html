Pediatric Airway & Ventilation Differences

A pediatric airway is not a smaller adult airway. A narrow lumen creates high resistance, a proportionally larger tongue and compliant airway increase obstruction risk, small tidal volumes make added dead space important, and higher oxygen consumption shortens the time available to correct apnea or failed ventilation. Airway care must anticipate these differences before deterioration occurs.

What makes the pediatric airway different?

Airway framework

Position → open → oxygenate → ventilate → verify

Position by age

An infant's large occiput can flex the neck when supine. Use neutral positioning and, when needed, a small shoulder roll rather than placing padding behind the head. Older children generally use a sniffing position unless trauma is suspected.

Expect rapid desaturation

Children have higher oxygen consumption and lower functional residual capacity than adults. Preoxygenate when possible, prepare equipment before attempts, and restore ventilation promptly.

Ventilate gently

Use an age-appropriate mask, maintain a seal, open the airway, and deliver only enough pressure and volume for visible chest rise. Excess pressure can distend the stomach and increase aspiration risk.

Verify continuously

After advanced-airway placement, use exhaled CO₂, chest rise, bilateral breath sounds, improving heart rate/oxygenation, tube-depth marking, and imaging when indicated. Recheck after movement or deterioration.

Core pediatric rule: small changes have large effects. Edema, secretions, tube obstruction, added dead space, or modest tube movement can sharply increase resistance or reduce effective ventilation.
Formula boundary: age-based tube formulas estimate a starting size or depth; they do not replace clinical fit, leak assessment when appropriate, depth markings, exhaled CO₂, breath sounds, physiologic response, and imaging.
Anatomy, ventilation & artificial-airway safety

Review each concept

Resistance rises dramatically as airway radius decreases. Because a child's airway is already narrow, a small amount of edema, mucus, blood, or a foreign body can cause a proportionally large obstruction. Do not wait for complete obstruction before escalating.
The infant's large occiput can push the neck into flexion and obstruct the airway when supine. Aim for a neutral position. A small roll beneath the shoulders may help; padding behind the head can worsen flexion. Use jaw thrust with spinal-motion restriction when trauma is suspected.
The tongue occupies more space relative to the mouth, and soft tissues are easily displaced. Loss of tone can obstruct the pharynx. Open the airway, suction visible obstructing material, and use an appropriately sized airway adjunct when indicated.
Children consume more oxygen per kilogram and have less oxygen reserve than adults. Desaturation can occur quickly during apnea, suction, airway manipulation, or repeated intubation attempts. Preoxygenate, organize equipment, limit attempt duration, and resume effective ventilation between attempts.
Choose a mask that covers the nose and mouth without compressing the eyes or extending below the chin. Use proper positioning, an effective seal, and controlled breaths that produce visible chest rise. If ineffective, correct position and seal, suction obstruction, add an adjunct, and consider two-person technique.
Extra connectors, adapters, filters, or sensors can occupy a meaningful portion of each small breath. Minimize unnecessary apparatus dead space, choose pediatric components, and suspect added dead space when CO₂ rises despite apparently adequate delivered volume.
Prepare the calculated tube plus one size smaller and larger. A common estimate for children older than about 1 year is uncuffed internal diameter = age/4 + 4 mm; cuffed = age/4 + 3.5 mm. Use length-based systems or institutional references when preferred, and confirm clinical fit rather than relying on the formula alone.
Age- or tube-based depth estimates are starting points only. Confirm with exhaled CO₂, bilateral chest rise and breath sounds, physiologic improvement, depth marking, and radiograph when indicated. Neck movement can move the tube; reassess after transport, repositioning, procedures, or deterioration.
Modern cuffed tubes can provide a reliable seal when properly sized. Use the minimum cuff pressure needed for an effective seal and monitor cuff pressure according to policy. Excess pressure can injure mucosa; a persistent large leak may make ventilation unreliable.
Secure the tube, document size and depth, maintain humidification, assess patency and secretions, minimize circuit torque, and keep alarms active. Sudden deterioration should trigger rapid assessment for displacement, obstruction, pneumothorax, and equipment failure before changing ventilator settings blindly.

Sudden deterioration after repositioning

  • SpO₂ falls rapidly and heart rate begins to decrease
