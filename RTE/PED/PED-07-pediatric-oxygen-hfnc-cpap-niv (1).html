Pediatric Oxygen, HFNC, CPAP & NIV

Respiratory support must match the problem. Supplemental oxygen treats hypoxemia. Heated high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) adds high, humidified flow that can reduce work of breathing. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) supports oxygenation and recruitment, while noninvasive ventilation (NIV) adds inspiratory assistance when ventilation is inadequate. The safest choice depends on age, size, physiology, tolerance, and response.

Support, avoid, or escalate?

Support-selection framework

Identify → select → set up → reassess

Define the problem

Hypoxemia is an oxygenation problem. Rising carbon dioxide, shallow breathing, apnea, or low minute ventilation indicates a ventilation problem. Work of breathing and fatigue show how much support the child needs.

Choose the least support that works

Use low-flow oxygen for mild oxygen need, HFNC for greater flow and work-of-breathing support, CPAP for distending pressure and recruitment, and bilevel NIV when inspiratory assistance is needed.

Build a safe system

Match the interface to the child's size, verify humidification and circuit patency, connect oxygen correctly, confirm prescribed settings, and test alarms. HFNC prongs must not completely block the nares.

Reassess the child and device

Track respiratory rate, effort, air entry, heart rate, oxygen saturation, mental status, carbon dioxide when indicated, synchrony, leak, skin pressure, and whether support requirements are rising or falling.

Core support rule: oxygen saturation can look acceptable while ventilation fails. A child with worsening fatigue, mental status, air movement, or carbon dioxide needs escalation even when oxygenation is maintained.
Do not let noninvasive support delay intubation: apnea, inability to protect the airway, severe instability, or continued deterioration despite support requires immediate expert airway evaluation.
Oxygenation, ventilation & device safety

Review each concept

Low oxygen saturation or arterial oxygen reflects an oxygenation problem. Rising carbon dioxide, low tidal volume, apnea, or inadequate minute ventilation reflects a ventilation problem. Many children have both, so assess the entire pattern.
A standard nasal cannula or mask can treat mild hypoxemia when work of breathing and ventilation remain adequate. Titrate oxygen to the prescribed target and reassess instead of automatically choosing a more complex device.
HFNC delivers heated, humidified gas at flows that can meet inspiratory demand. It provides a more stable oxygen concentration, reduces rebreathing of upper-airway gas, may create mild distending pressure, and can decrease work of breathing.
Choose a cannula sized for the child that leaves room for gas to escape around the prongs. Verify the heated humidifier, water supply, temperature, circuit connections, flow, oxygen concentration, and ordered limits before application.
Flow is selected mainly according to size, inspiratory demand, and work of breathing. Fraction of inspired oxygen is adjusted mainly to correct hypoxemia. Change one deliberately, then reassess the intended response.
CPAP provides one continuous pressure throughout the breathing cycle. It can recruit unstable alveoli, improve functional residual capacity, splint an obstructed airway, and improve oxygenation in a child who is breathing spontaneously.
Bilevel NIV provides inspiratory positive airway pressure and expiratory positive airway pressure. The pressure difference assists tidal volume and carbon dioxide removal, while expiratory pressure supports recruitment and oxygenation.
A poorly fitted mask can cause a large unintended leak, failed triggering, low tidal volume, skin injury, or eye irritation. Reposition or resize the interface, verify straps and leak, protect pressure areas, and keep exhalation ports open.
When support suddenly changes, assess the child first, then trace the system from interface to gas source. Check for disconnection, kink, obstruction, water in the circuit, empty humidifier, incorrect setting, failed oxygen supply, or disabled alarm.
Improvement means lower respiratory and heart rates, less effort, better comfort, stable gas exchange, and decreasing oxygen or pressure needs. Worsening apnea, fatigue, mental status, carbon dioxide, hypoxemia, or support need signals failure and requires escalation.

Troubleshoot before increasing support

Infant receiving prescribed HFNC:
  • Work of breathing and oxygen saturation worsen suddenly
  • Device displays a low-flow alarm
  • Condensed water is visible in a dependent circuit loop
  • Nasal prongs remain correctly positioned
  • Oxygen source and power remain connected
