Sleep-Disordered Breathing Fundamentals

Classify the failed mechanism before worrying about the treatment label. Obstructive apnea means airflow stops while respiratory effort continues. Central apnea means both airflow and effort stop. Mixed apnea contains both phases. Hypopnea is partial airflow reduction with a physiologic consequence, while sleep-related hypoventilation is a sustained ventilation problem rather than a series of discrete obstructive events.

Socratic event string

What failed: the airway, the drive, or sustained ventilation?

Classification framework

Use airflow and effort first

Obstructive apnea

Airflow is absent while respiratory effort continues because central drive persists but the upper airway is occluded. Thoracoabdominal movement may become paradoxical or out of phase.

Central apnea

Both airflow and respiratory effort are absent because central ventilatory output temporarily stops. No inspiratory pressure gradient is generated.

Mixed apnea

The same event contains both central and obstructive physiology—commonly absent effort first, followed by returning effort while airflow remains absent.

Hypopnea / hypoventilation

Hypopnea is a partial airflow reduction associated with desaturation or arousal. Sleep-related hypoventilation is sustained inadequate ventilation with rising CO₂ rather than simply repeated discrete apneas.

Airflow tells you whether gas is moving. Effort tells you whether the nervous system and respiratory muscles are trying to breathe.

This is the defining distinction in your AHRC sleep material.

Core sleep physiology

Review each concept

Your PulmoLearn sleep lesson defines apnea as a near-complete pause in airflow lasting at least 10 seconds during adult sleep testing. SLP-01 uses the duration only as the basic event definition; SLP-02 will own detailed study scoring and interpretation.
Hypopnea is a partial reduction in airflow associated with oxygen desaturation or arousal. It can still cause clinically meaningful sleep fragmentation and gas-exchange burden even though airflow does not completely stop.
Sleep reduces upper-airway dilator tone. In a susceptible airway, pharyngeal collapse can stop airflow while respiratory drive continues. Persistent or paradoxical thoracoabdominal effort therefore supports obstruction rather than central loss of drive.
Central apnea occurs when ventilatory output temporarily ceases, so both airflow and respiratory effort disappear. Your notes also explain that when PaCO₂ falls below the sleeping apneic threshold, central output may pause; unstable feedback can create periodic waxing and waning ventilation.
A mixed event may start with absent airflow and absent effort, then effort returns while airflow remains absent. Classifying only the first or last few seconds would mislabel the event. Follow airflow and effort from beginning to end.
When the diaphragm contracts against an occluded upper airway, the chest and abdomen may move out of phase. Your source stresses that paradoxical motion is evidence of inspiratory effort—not evidence of effective ventilation.
Your faculty guide teaches that wakefulness adds behavioral drive and supports upper-airway dilator activity. During sleep, behavioral drive falls, chemical control becomes more dominant, ventilatory responses may be reduced, and airway dilator tone decreases.
Your source notes that reduced accessory-muscle contribution during REM can expose limited ventilatory reserve. A patient who ventilates adequately while awake may develop REM-related hypoventilation and deeper desaturation, especially when respiratory-muscle strength or lung reserve is limited.
Your student notes distinguish recurrent discrete apnea/hypopnea from prolonged desaturation and rising CO₂ without frequent discrete airflow events. The latter should trigger consideration of sustained hypoventilation, neuromuscular weakness, obesity hypoventilation, CNS depression, or other ventilatory-pump/control problems.
AHI is the average number of apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep. Your faculty guide explicitly warns that AHI does not independently describe event duration, desaturation depth, time below threshold, hypercapnia, arousal intensity, or daytime ventilatory failure. SLP-02 will practice full sleep-study interpretation.
Recommend sleep testing when symptoms or overnight patterns require differentiation among obstructive, central, mixed, hypopnea-dominant, or sustained hypoventilation physiology. The study is useful because it can integrate airflow, effort, oxygenation, CO₂, arousal, stage, and position rather than relying on symptoms alone.
Integrated RTE-style case

Do not let a low AHI hide sustained ventilatory failure

Overnight study:
  • AHI = 4 events/hour
  • Average SpO₂ = 87%
  • Transcutaneous CO₂ progressively rises during REM
  • No frequent discrete obstructive events are present
  • Respiratory effort remains weak but present
