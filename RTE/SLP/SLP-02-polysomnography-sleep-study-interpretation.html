What does the report actually say?

Systematic interpretation framework

Use the report in layers

AHI first—but not AHI only

Your course defines AHI as total apneas + hypopneas divided by total hours of sleep. Existing course categories are normal 0–5, mild 5–15, moderate 15–30, and severe >30 events/hour.

Then oxygen burden

Review baseline/mean SpO₂, nadir, and the amount of time spent below important oxygen thresholds. AHI frequency does not independently describe how deep or prolonged the desaturation burden is.

Then event mechanism

Use airflow and respiratory effort to determine whether events are obstructive, central, mixed, or partial hypopneas. Do not assign mechanism from desaturation alone.

Then distribution/context

Check REM versus NREM, supine versus non-supine, heart rate/rhythm, snoring, CO₂ trends, and whether a home study versus full PSG could capture the needed signals.

Read the whole report before making a treatment recommendation.

Your current PulmoLearn lesson explicitly uses a nine-point review to prevent over-reliance on a single value.

Nine-point sleep-report review

Review each data domain

AHI = total apneas + hypopneas ÷ total hours of sleep. Your course uses: normal 0–5, mild 5–15, moderate 15–30, severe >30 events/hour. AHI measures event frequency—not oxygen burden, CO₂ retention, event duration, or event mechanism.
Your current report-reading framework says to review baseline/mean SpO₂, oxygen nadir, percent of time below 90%, and percent of time below 88%. The key question is not just “did SpO₂ fall?” but “how deep and how prolonged was the oxygen burden?”
ODI counts oxygen desaturation events per hour. Your course asks whether ODI roughly tracks AHI or whether oxygen desaturations appear more frequent than the scored apnea/hypopnea events, which may signal additional gas-exchange burden or a need to review the study more carefully.
Two patients can have the same AHI but different event composition. Your current source cautions against using AHI alone and directs the learner to consider event type, duration, desaturation, arousal, and recovery rather than assuming equal physiologic burden from all events.
Use airflow and effort together. Obstructive events retain effort; central events lose both airflow and effort; mixed events contain both phases. The current source update specifically avoids an automatic device assignment from a central-event count and instead calls for cause-specific evaluation.
Full PSG can identify sleep stages because it includes EEG and related channels. Check whether respiratory events cluster in REM or occur across sleep stages. Home Type 3 studies do not measure EEG and therefore cannot confirm sleep stage or true sleep time.
Your source teaches that supine sleep can worsen upper-airway collapse. Compare total AHI with supine and non-supine AHI, and note how much time was actually spent in each position. A positional pattern is most persuasive when non-supine AHI approaches normal and oxygenation is adequate outside supine sleep.
Review bradycardia/tachycardia patterns around respiratory events and any arrhythmia findings. The home study may record heart rate, while full PSG can include ECG for more complete cardiac rhythm monitoring.
Snoring supports upper-airway narrowing but does not by itself define OSA severity. Note whether snoring clusters in supine sleep, occurs broadly, or is discordant with the scored event burden.
A progressively rising transcutaneous CO₂ trend with prolonged oxygenation abnormality and few discrete events supports sustained hypoventilation rather than frequent apnea. Integrate CO₂ with airflow, effort, SpO₂, stage, and position.
Your current PulmoLearn source describes a home/Type 3 study as measuring airflow, SpO₂, heart rate, chest/abdomen effort, position, and snoring but not EEG sleep staging, EMG, or leg movements. Full PSG adds EEG, EOG, EMG, broader respiratory/cardiac monitoring, detailed event classification, and attended PAP titration capability.
