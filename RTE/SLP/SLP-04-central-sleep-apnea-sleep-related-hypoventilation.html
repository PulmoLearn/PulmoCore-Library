Central Sleep Apnea & Sleep-Related Hypoventilation

Do not confuse an open airway with adequate ventilation. Central apnea is a failure or instability of breath initiation: airflow and respiratory effort stop together. Sleep-related hypoventilation is different—it is sustained inadequate alveolar ventilation with rising CO₂ and may occur from obesity hypoventilation, neuromuscular weakness, CNS depression, or limited respiratory reserve.

Socratic mechanism string

Is the problem airway collapse, absent drive, or inadequate ventilation?

Central-vs-hypoventilation framework

Follow effort, CO₂, and the overnight pattern

Central apnea

Airflow stops because respiratory effort stops. The primary problem is absent or unstable neural drive—not upper-airway occlusion.

Periodic breathing

Crescendo–decrescendo ventilation with central pauses suggests unstable feedback control, altered chemosensitivity, prolonged circulation time, or another central mechanism.

Sleep-related hypoventilation

Airflow/effort may continue, but ventilation is inadequate. Transcutaneous or end-tidal CO₂ trends rise over time, often with sustained rather than cyclic oxygenation impairment.

Backup ventilation

A timed breath can support ventilation when spontaneous central effort is absent or unreliable. This addresses failure to initiate a breath rather than simply splinting an airway open.

Key distinction: CPAP stabilizes a collapsible airway. Pressure support assists tidal ventilation. A backup rate supplies a breath when spontaneous initiation fails.
ASV concept: your source describes adaptive servo-ventilation (ASV) as variable pressure support used for certain central or treatment-emergent patterns, but also warns that heart-failure context and ejection fraction matter because ASV may be contraindicated in reduced-EF heart failure. This lesson therefore teaches ASV as a nuanced specialist concept—not a universal “central apnea = ASV” rule.
Mechanisms & monitoring

Review each topic

Your AHRC sleep material defines central apnea by loss of both airflow and respiratory effort. The airway may remain patent, but no inspiratory pressure gradient is generated because central ventilatory output temporarily stops.
Your faculty guide teaches that when PaCO₂ falls below the sleeping apneic threshold, central output may cease temporarily. If the corrective response overshoots or is delayed, ventilation can wax and wane instead of remaining stable.
Repeated central pauses separated by waxing-and-waning ventilation support unstable central ventilatory control rather than fixed upper-airway obstruction. Your exam-aligned case specifically uses absent respiratory effort plus crescendo–decrescendo ventilation as the discriminator.
Your course distinguishes obesity hypoventilation from isolated OSA by the presence of awake daytime hypercapnia in an obese patient after other causes are excluded. Increased mechanical load, reduced ventilatory drive, and sleep-related worsening may coexist with obstructive events.
Reduced respiratory-muscle force may produce shallow ventilation and CO₂ retention, especially during REM when accessory-muscle contribution falls. Airflow and effort may remain present but insufficient—so this is hypoventilation, not necessarily central apnea.
Opioids or other CNS depressants may reduce respiratory rate, ventilatory drive, and response to CO₂. The overnight pattern may show central events, sustained hypoventilation, or both depending on the mechanism.
Your student notes explicitly warn that supplemental oxygen may improve saturation while leaving obstruction or hypoventilation unresolved and may mask a rising CO₂ burden. This is why overnight CO₂ monitoring matters in hypoventilation physiology.
A backup rate provides a timed breath when the patient fails to trigger one spontaneously. Your source contrasts this with CPAP, which cannot trigger a breath and primarily maintains airway patency.
Pressure support is the difference between inspiratory and expiratory pressure. Your faculty guide states that it can increase tidal volume and improve alveolar ventilation when shallow breathing or hypoventilation is the problem.
Your PulmoLearn source describes ASV as providing variable pressure support to normalize unstable breathing patterns in selected central or treatment-emergent cases. It is not an automatic choice for every central apnea pattern, and reduced-ejection-fraction heart failure is specifically flagged as a context where ASV may be contraindicated.
Gather the oxygen and CO₂ data needed to separate intermittent events from sustained failure: SpO₂ trend, oxygen nadir/time low, transcutaneous or end-tidal CO₂ trend, airflow, effort, sleep stage when available, respiratory rate, and whether ventilatory support changes the pattern coherently.
Integrated RTE-style case

Central apnea or sustained hypoventilation?

Overnight study in a patient with neuromuscular weakness:
  • Airflow remains present
  • Thoracoabdominal effort remains present but shallow
  • Respiratory rate falls during REM
  • Transcutaneous CO₂ steadily rises
  • SpO₂ slowly declines without repetitive discrete apnea cycles
