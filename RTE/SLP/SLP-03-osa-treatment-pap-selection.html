OSA Treatment & PAP Selection

Match positive airway pressure to the failed physiology. CPAP splints the obstructed upper airway open with one continuous pressure. APAP changes pressure within a prescribed range as obstructive needs vary. Bilevel provides separate inspiratory and expiratory pressures and may be considered when pressure intolerance, hypoventilation, hypercapnia, or additional ventilatory support needs are present.

Which support best matches the physiology?

PAP selection framework

Start with the mechanism, then choose the support

CPAP for upper-airway collapse

Continuous positive airway pressure acts as a pneumatic splint that keeps the pharyngeal airway open through the respiratory cycle. Your PulmoLearn sleep source identifies obstructive sleep apnea as the primary target for CPAP therapy.

APAP when pressure needs vary

Auto-adjusting positive airway pressure changes pressure within a prescribed range based on detected events. Your source describes it as common outpatient therapy when pressure needs vary by sleep stage or body position.

Bilevel when one pressure is not the whole problem

Bilevel positive airway pressure uses a higher inspiratory pressure (IPAP) and lower expiratory pressure (EPAP). The pressure difference supports ventilation and may be useful when pressure intolerance, hypoventilation, hypercapnia, or greater ventilatory support needs are present.

Do not confuse obstruction with absent drive

CPAP can splint an obstructed airway but does not create a breath when respiratory effort is absent. Central apnea and sustained hypoventilation require a different evaluation and may require backup-rate or ventilatory support concepts covered in SLP-04.

Board logic: persistent obstructive events → think airway splinting pressure. Ventilatory failure/hypercapnia → think support of ventilation, not simply more oxygen.
Scope boundary: SLP-03 teaches PAP selection and what to assess during sleep titration. SLP-05 will own detailed troubleshooting for leak, dryness, rainout, aerophagia, skin injury, interface fit, adherence, and pressure-tolerance barriers.
Treatment decisions

Review each concept

CPAP provides continuous positive airway pressure through the entire respiratory cycle. In obstructive sleep apnea, the goal is to prevent recurrent upper-airway collapse. During titration, assess whether obstructive apneas/hypopneas, snoring, arousals, and desaturation burden improve at the selected pressure.
APAP automatically adjusts pressure within a prescribed range in response to detected obstructive events and changing pressure needs. Your source specifically notes usefulness when pressure requirements vary by sleep stage or body position.
On bilevel therapy, EPAP is the expiratory pressure used to keep the upper airway open at end exhalation. Your PulmoLearn glossary notes that obstructive events often improve as EPAP is increased. Always interpret changes through event response, oxygenation, leak, comfort, and provider/lab protocol.
The pressure difference between IPAP and EPAP is pressure support. Your source links pressure support to assistance with ventilation and notes that it may improve hypoventilation or hypercapnia when ventilatory support is needed.
Your PulmoLearn source says bilevel may be considered when pressure intolerance, hypoventilation, hypercapnia, or ventilatory-support needs are present, depending on patient physiology and provider order. Bilevel is therefore not simply “stronger CPAP”; it changes inspiratory versus expiratory pressure and can support ventilation.
Your source explicitly states that supplemental oxygen may improve saturation when ordered, but it does not splint the upper airway open, prevent obstructive events, or correct the repeated arousal mechanism by itself.
Review airflow, respiratory effort, SpO₂, event type, event frequency, sleep stage/position when available, snoring, arousals, pressure level, mask/interface status, leak, comfort, and whether events improve as pressure changes.
An effective obstructive-airway response should reduce obstructive apneas/hypopneas and snoring while improving oxygenation and sleep continuity without creating an intolerable setup. Persistent residual AHI may reflect leak, inadequate settings, central events, or the need for reassessment rather than automatic pressure escalation.
The interface must allow the prescribed positive pressure to reach the airway. Select and fit the mask or nasal interface so that therapy can be delivered with acceptable seal and comfort. Major leak can reduce effective pressure and make the titration appear unsuccessful.
If events are central—with absent respiratory effort—do not assume that increasing CPAP pressure will solve them. Your source distinguishes upper-airway obstruction from absent drive and notes that management changes when backup ventilation or another support strategy is needed. SLP-04 owns that decision pathway.
Review residual AHI, obstructive versus central event composition, oxygen desaturation burden, leak, pressure tolerance, persistent hypoxemia, symptoms, and signs of hypoventilation. Treatment succeeds when the underlying sleep-breathing problem improves and the patient can actually use the therapy.

Choose airway splinting versus ventilatory support

Sleep-lab titration:
  • Events are predominantly obstructive
  • Respiratory effort continues during events
  • Snoring and obstruction worsen supine and in REM
  • No sustained hypercapnia is present
  • The patient tolerates positive pressure
