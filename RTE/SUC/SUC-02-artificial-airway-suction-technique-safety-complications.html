Artificial-Airway Suction Technique, Safety & Complications

Choose a catheter that does not excessively block the airway, verify the suction system, preoxygenate, keep each pass brief, preserve oxygenation and PEEP when needed, and stop when the patient develops a significant adverse response.

Safe artificial-airway suction sequence

Size → setup → oxygenate → insert → suction briefly → recover → reassess

Catheter sizeYour LindseyJones RTE example says the catheter outer diameter should not exceed about one-half the ETT internal diameter. Convert millimeters to French by multiplying by 3.
EquipmentThe 2027 NBRC explicitly lists suction regulator, canister, tubing and catheter as suctioning equipment.
PreoxygenationYour lab competency uses 100% oxygen for 30–60 seconds before/after passes.
Insertion depthThe competency advances to the predetermined depth; its deeper method inserts until resistance then withdraws 2 cm before suctioning. High-risk patients use a shallower approach to the end of the artificial airway.
DurationThe lab competency keeps continuous suction periods brief—15 seconds or less.
RecoveryIf repeated suctioning is needed, allow recovery and maintain oxygenation; the competency also says to maintain PEEP during suctioning when ordered.
Pressure source boundary: the SUC-02 RTE framework requires safe suction pressure, but the retrieved PulmoLearn/LindseyJones source passages available for this build did not provide a numeric pressure range. This lesson therefore teaches verifying the regulator and using the prescribed/facility-approved suction pressure rather than inventing an unsupported number.

3.5 mm × 3 = 10.5 Fr, so the source selects the available 10 Fr catheter.
RTE logic: too large a catheter blocks more of the airway and can worsen loss of ventilation/oxygenation during suction.
NBRC specifically names regulator, canister, tubing and catheter.
Before suctioning, the system must actually generate suction and all connections/canister setup must be functional.
Shared II.A.12 angle: if suction is absent, troubleshoot the setup before inserting the catheter.
Open suction: the lab competency uses a sterile dominant hand for catheter handling.
Closed suction: the Ballard/closed system remains attached to the tube adapter and the catheter advances through a protective sleeve.
Source-specific advantage during intolerance: the competency includes switching suction method and maintaining PEEP when ordered if the patient is not tolerating suctioning.
The lab competency administers 100% oxygen for 30–60 seconds after each suction pass and includes ensuring 100% oxygen is being delivered if the patient is not tolerating suction.
The same competency’s sequence is built around giving recovery time between passes when needed.
Source deep technique: insert to resistance, then withdraw 2 cm before suctioning.
For a patient at high risk for suction-related complications, the competency uses a shallow technique only to the end of the artificial airway.
Continuous suction period: ≤15 seconds in the lab competency.
The SUC-02 RTE review explicitly requires recognition of hypoxemia, arrhythmia, trauma and other complications.
The lab competency monitors cardiac rhythm before/during/after and reassesses cardiopulmonary status and unexpected outcomes.
Shared III.E.1: a severe adverse response means stop/terminate the suction attempt, restore oxygenation/support, reassess, and change the care plan rather than pushing through.
The lab competency requires the catheter to be fully withdrawn from the artificial airway before cleaning the closed suction catheter.
Saline is placed in the side port while suction is applied until the catheter clears, with the explicit warning that solution should not go down the airway.
