CBC & Oxygen-Carrying Capacity

A complete blood count (CBC) measures red blood cells, hemoglobin, hematocrit, white blood cells, and platelets. Hemoglobin carries most blood oxygen: a normal pulse-oximeter saturation can coexist with low total oxygen content when hemoglobin is low. Read the count beside symptoms, trends, and planned respiratory procedures.

Can a normal saturation hide a problem?

CBC interpretation framework

Read the value → compare the trend → connect the patient → choose the next step

Oxygen-carrying capacity

Hemoglobin (Hb) is the oxygen-binding protein in red blood cells (RBCs). A low Hb means less oxygen can be carried even if the fraction of Hb that is oxygenated—pulse-oximeter saturation (SpO₂)—is normal. Evaluate the patient, not SpO₂ alone.

Red-cell context

Hematocrit (Hct) is the percentage of blood volume occupied by red cells. RBC count, Hb, and Hct often move together, but hydration and other conditions can change their interpretation. Compare with the patient's earlier results and current symptoms.

White-cell context

A white blood cell (WBC) count and its differential—the proportions or numbers of WBC types—can support an infection or inflammation assessment. A high count or neutrophil increase alone does not prove bacterial pneumonia; a low count does not rule infection out.

Bleeding and testing

Platelets help stop bleeding. Low platelets, active bleeding, and antithrombotic medication can affect the safety of invasive sampling or procedures. Review the full picture and local policy with the team before proceeding; clarify surprising values with an appropriate repeat specimen.

Core rule: SpO₂ describes the percentage of available hemoglobin carrying oxygen, not how much hemoglobin is present. Anemia can lower oxygen content without lowering SpO₂.
Clinical cue: interpret CBC results with history, examination, specimen quality, and trends. A CBC is a clue—not a stand-alone diagnosis or an automatic treatment order.
CBC components and safe decisions

Review each concept

A CBC includes RBC count, Hb, Hct, WBC count, and platelet count. A CBC with differential also breaks the WBC count into cell types. Each value answers a different clinical question.
Most oxygen in blood is bound to Hb. Low Hb reduces potential oxygen content. Check the magnitude and direction of change, symptoms, and possible causes rather than treating a normal SpO₂ as proof of adequate oxygen delivery.
SpO₂ estimates the proportion of Hb carrying oxygen; it does not measure how much Hb exists. An anemic patient can have SpO₂ of 98% yet carry less oxygen per volume of blood than a patient with normal Hb.
Hct represents the portion of blood made of red cells, while the RBC count reports their number. Low results may fit anemia, but fluid shifts or sample problems may change the numbers; interpret alongside Hb and the prior CBC.
A new fall in Hb from the patient's prior value may warrant prompt assessment for bleeding or another cause. Compare collection times and the clinical condition; do not dismiss a major change because SpO₂ remains normal.
High WBC may occur with infection, inflammation, or other causes. Low WBC also has many causes and does not exclude serious infection. Combine it with fever, examination, imaging, and clinical course.
The differential distinguishes types such as neutrophils and eosinophils. Neutrophilia can support infection in context; eosinophilia may be associated with allergic or eosinophilic disease. Neither pattern alone establishes a respiratory diagnosis.
Platelets contribute to clotting. When they are low, consider bleeding signs, medication use, the procedure, and local precautions; discuss risks with the team rather than applying a single number to every procedure.
If Hb unexpectedly drops and the result conflicts with earlier values or the clinical picture, recommend confirming it with a properly collected repeat CBC while evaluating the patient for bleeding. Do not let retesting delay urgent assessment.
Communicate a meaningful new Hb drop, symptoms, relevant WBC and platelet findings, and the trend. Specify what is known, what needs confirmation, and any immediate respiratory-care or procedure safety concern.
Integrated RTE-style laboratory case

A reassuring number, an unsafe assumption

A hospitalized patient becomes weak and short of breath:
  • SpO₂ is 97% on the same oxygen setting as yesterday
  • Today's Hb is 7.1 g/dL, down from 11.8 g/dL yesterday
  • The patient is newly tachycardic and appears pale
  • A blood gas sample has been requested
