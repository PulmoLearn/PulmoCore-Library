Dyshemoglobins & Specialty Blood Tests

Carbon monoxide can form carboxyhemoglobin (COHb); certain drugs or chemicals can increase methemoglobin (MetHb). Both interfere with normal oxygen transport, and a standard two-wavelength pulse oximeter cannot reliably identify them. Blood co-oximetry measures hemoglobin species. Other specialty tests, such as immunoglobulin E (IgE), may support a respiratory workup but must be interpreted with the patient.

Can standard SpO₂ be trusted here?

Specialty blood-test framework

Recognize a misleading number → select the right assay → interpret the fraction

Recognize the mismatch

SpO₂ is the estimate from a standard pulse oximeter; it may appear reassuring in carbon monoxide (CO) exposure or be unreliable with high MetHb. Arterial oxygen pressure (PaO₂) measures dissolved oxygen and may also fail to reveal impaired hemoglobin carriage. Look for exposure history and symptoms.

Choose blood co-oximetry

A multiple-wavelength laboratory co-oximeter can directly report COHb and MetHb fractions. For suspected CO poisoning, either venous or arterial blood can be used for COHb measurement. Obtain an arterial sample if blood gases are needed for a separate clinical reason.

Collect and interpret safely

Follow local blood-gas specimen identification and handling rules: document source, collection time, current oxygen treatment, and suspected exposure; remove air bubbles and avoid delays if a gas is also ordered. Oxygen given before sampling can lower a measured COHb, so timing matters.

Keep specialty markers in context

Total IgE may be elevated with allergic disease but is not a stand-alone asthma diagnosis or proof of a specific trigger. Allergen-specific IgE addresses sensitization to a particular allergen and still needs a compatible history. Choose a test that answers the clinical question.

Core rule: standard pulse oximetry and a calculated saturation from blood-gas PaO₂ do not replace directly measured co-oximetry when COHb or MetHb is suspected.
Response cue: suspected toxic exposure needs prompt assessment and care while testing is arranged; do not wait for a co-oximetry number before alerting the team. The measured fraction is not, by itself, a complete severity assessment.
Dyshemoglobins and targeted testing

Review each concept

Headache, confusion, nausea, or collapse after exposure to a generator or combustion source can suggest CO poisoning. A normal-looking standard SpO₂ does not exclude it; report the history promptly and request blood COHb by co-oximetry.
COHb is the fraction of hemoglobin bound to CO. Timing, supplemental oxygen already given, and smoking history can change its level. Assess symptoms and exposure alongside the result; one fraction is not a complete severity score.
MetHb is a form of hemoglobin that does not transport oxygen normally. Suspect it when cyanosis and a pulse-oximeter reading do not fit a relatively preserved PaO₂, especially after exposure to an oxidizing drug or chemical.
A measured MetHb fraction from co-oximetry can explain a saturation mismatch. Pair it with clinical status, exposure, and any other dyshemoglobin fractions; a routine blood gas without co-oximetry may report a calculated, misleading saturation.
For COHb, a properly collected venous or arterial blood specimen can be tested by laboratory co-oximetry. If the team also needs pH, PaCO₂, or PaO₂, collect an arterial gas for those measurements under local procedure.
Identify arterial versus venous source, sample time, oxygen therapy, and any exposure. If blood gas values are ordered, remove air bubbles, mix and transport promptly by local protocol; poor handling can distort gas measurements.
PaO₂ is dissolved oxygen pressure; a conventional saturation calculation assumes normal hemoglobin behavior. With suspected COHb or MetHb, read the measured fractions on co-oximetry rather than trusting a calculated saturation.
IgE is an immune protein involved in allergic responses. An elevated total IgE can support an allergy evaluation but does not confirm asthma, identify a specific trigger, or establish that a symptom is allergic.
Allergen-specific IgE can help assess sensitization to a suspected trigger when history supports it. A positive result needs clinical correlation; it does not alone prove that exposure caused an individual respiratory event.
When symptoms and exposure suggest CO despite a normal SpO₂, recommend measured blood COHb by co-oximetry. When cyanosis and PaO₂ are discordant after a relevant drug exposure, request measured MetHb. Specify the question the assay will answer.
