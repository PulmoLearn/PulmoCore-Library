Glucose, Lactate & Metabolic Stress

Blood glucose can fall dangerously low or rise during illness. Lactate can rise with poor tissue perfusion and other stressors; it does not reveal its cause by itself. When acids build up, faster or deeper breathing may compensate. Connect laboratory results with perfusion, mental status, acid–base data, and the patient's breathing before deciding on the next test.

Metabolic assessment framework

Check glucose → assess perfusion → read acid–base data → seek the cause

Glucose and the brain

Low glucose (hypoglycemia) can quickly cause sweating, confusion, seizures, or loss of consciousness. Check a bedside glucose promptly in an altered patient while assessing and responding to the patient; do not assume a respiratory cause from breathing alone.

High glucose and ketones

High glucose (hyperglycemia) during illness is a clue, not a diagnosis of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). DKA involves excess ketones and metabolic acidosis; request ketone and acid–base evaluation when the patient’s history and findings raise concern.

Lactate and perfusion

Elevated lactate may accompany shock or poor tissue perfusion, but other processes can raise it too. Interpret it with blood pressure, circulation, infection clues, medications, and the trend. A single lactate value cannot identify the source.

Breathing as compensation

Metabolic acidosis may trigger deeper or faster breathing to lower carbon dioxide (CO₂). The breathing pattern is a response to the acid problem, not necessarily a primary lung disorder. Support oxygenation and ventilation while evaluating the cause.

Core rule: deep, rapid breathing may be a response to metabolic acidosis. Do not suppress compensatory breathing without understanding the underlying problem and the patient's ventilatory needs.
Diagnostic cue: for suspected DKA, pair glucose with blood ketones (preferably beta-hydroxybutyrate) and pH/bicarbonate. For possible shock, interpret lactate in clinical context and follow the trend; do not treat it as a stand-alone diagnosis.
Metabolic findings and safe actions

Review each concept

A high glucose may occur with diabetes or physiologic stress. A low glucose may cause rapid neurologic change. Compare the value with symptoms, recent food or medication, illness, and prior results.
Sudden sweating, confusion, seizure, or decreased responsiveness calls for prompt bedside glucose assessment and immediate team action. Do not let a normal oxygen saturation delay evaluation.
High glucose can rise during serious illness. Look for dehydration, symptoms, medicines, and trends; it does not establish DKA without appropriate ketone and acid–base evidence.
DKA combines hyperglycemia or known diabetes with elevated ketones and metabolic acidosis. Some patients, including those taking certain diabetes medicines, can develop ketoacidosis with less impressive glucose elevation; evaluate the whole picture.
Deep, rapid breathing can help lower CO₂ during metabolic acidosis. Identify the potential acid source and assess whether the patient can sustain compensation; do not mistake compensation for a purely respiratory disorder.
Low serum bicarbonate suggests an acid–base disturbance but does not by itself identify the cause. A blood gas can clarify pH and ventilation when clinically indicated; pair it with chemistry, ketones, lactate, and examination.
Elevated lactate can reflect impaired perfusion, severe infection, or other metabolic stress. Review blood pressure, mental status, skin perfusion, medications, and serial levels; it does not specify a pathogen or prove sepsis.
Poor tissue perfusion can raise lactate and contribute to metabolic acidosis and faster breathing. Promptly communicate hypotension or other perfusion signs and work with the team to identify and address the cause.
In an ill patient with high glucose, deep breathing, and low bicarbonate, recommend blood ketones and pH assessment to evaluate suspected DKA. Do not rely on glucose alone, and do not delay urgent assessment while testing proceeds.
Relay glucose, lactate, acid–base results, vital signs, breathing pattern, perfusion findings, and their timing. Say which diagnosis remains uncertain and which test would clarify it.
