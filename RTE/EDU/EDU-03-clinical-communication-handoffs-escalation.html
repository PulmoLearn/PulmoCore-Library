Clinical Communication, Handoffs & Escalation

When a patient's breathing changes, communicate the concern clearly and early while taking immediate actions within your role. Give the team the current findings, trend, treatments, response, and what you need next. Confirm urgent instructions, identify who owns the next step, and hand off a plan for reassessment.

Clinical communication framework

Recognize → report → check back → hand off → debrief

Act and escalate

Assess airway, breathing, and response to current support. Begin immediate care within your role and call the appropriate team member or emergency response according to local policy. Do not delay a safety action to finish a formal report.

Make a focused report

Situation–Background–Assessment–Recommendation or Request (SBAR) organizes an urgent call. Identify the patient and problem, give relevant baseline and recent changes, state what you found and did, and ask for a specific decision or bedside response.

Close the loop

In a check-back, the receiver repeats the instruction and the sender confirms or corrects it. For example: “Please bring the airway cart.” “I am bringing the airway cart now.” “Correct.” Say who is completing each action.

Transfer and learn

A handoff includes illness severity, current support, response, pending tasks, who owns them, and what changes require escalation. After an adverse event, a brief, blame-free team debrief identifies what worked, what did not, and one improvement.

Escalation rule: a report is not complete merely because it was sent. Confirm that the right person received it, the action is assigned, and the patient is reassessed.
Goals-of-care cue: when life-support withdrawal is planned, confirm the documented plan and team roles with the authorized team, use clear and respectful language with the patient or family, and coordinate comfort-focused respiratory care. Do not independently change life support or promise an outcome.
Communication decisions

Review each concept

Compare current breathing, work of breathing, oxygenation, mental status, and support with the earlier baseline. A new dangerous change calls for assessment, immediate appropriate actions, and prompt escalation.
SBAR means Situation, Background, Assessment, and Recommendation or Request. Begin with the patient's identity and current danger; add only the background needed to make a decision and ask for a clear next step.
Describe relevant numbers and observations, time of change, current respiratory support, actions taken, and response. Avoid vague reports such as “looks bad” when you can say what changed.
If deterioration is severe or the first contact cannot respond promptly, follow the local chain of escalation or activate the emergency response. Continue care within scope while the team arrives.
Give an instruction to a named person. Have that person repeat it; confirm or correct the message. A silent nod, a posted note, or an unacknowledged page does not confirm the task is underway.
State illness severity, active support, recent trend, response, pending tests or procedures, who owns each task, and what change should trigger a call. Allow the receiving clinician to ask questions.
For a provider-performed airway procedure, clarify equipment and oxygen needs, assign team roles with a check-back, and communicate the postprocedure reassessment and handoff.
Once the patient is safe and immediate work is complete, ask what went well, where communication or equipment caused delay, and what should change. Focus on learning rather than blame.
During planned withdrawal of life support, confirm the authorized plan and responsibilities with the team. Explain respiratory actions in plain language, respect patient and family preferences, and communicate distress or comfort needs promptly.
Record the change, time and recipient of the report, urgent actions, response, assigned follow-up, and reassessment according to policy. Tell the incoming clinician about unfinished tasks and confirm they accept responsibility.
Integrated RTE-style communication case

Escalate, then hand off

While preparing a provider-led airway procedure:
  • A patient has rising work of breathing and is becoming less alert
  • The respiratory therapist calls for immediate help and begins appropriate support
  • A teammate says, “I'll get the airway equipment” without confirming the location
  • Another therapist will take over when the procedure team arrives
