Core humidity framework

Humidity and aerosol are not the same thing

Your existing competency guide makes this the first distinction: humidity is vapor; aerosol is liquid droplets.

HumidityInvisible water vapor dissolved in the gas. Humidifiers add or recycle vapor.
AerosolActual liquid droplets or particles suspended in gas. Nebulizers generate droplets.
Absolute humidity (AH)Actual mass of water vapor in a volume of gas, measured in mg/L.
Relative humidity (RH)How full the gas is compared with its water-vapor capacity at that temperature, expressed as a percentage.
Body-condition benchmark

Fully conditioned gas at body temperature contains 44 mg/L AH at 37°C and 100% RH.

Board-prep formulas

Turn humidity values into clinical meaning

These formulas come directly from your existing PulmoLearn humidity competency guide.

Relative Humidity (%) = Absolute Humidity ÷ Water-vapor capacity at that temperature × 100
Body Humidity (%) = Absolute Humidity ÷ 44 mg/L × 100
Humidity Deficit = 44 mg/L − Delivered Absolute Humidity

Airway conditioning & ISB

Know what shifts the conditioning burden deeper

0 of 5 reviewedReview all airway-conditioning concepts.
  • Point where inspired gas reaches body temperature and full saturation
  • Course benchmark: 37°C, 100% RH, 44 mg/L AH
  • Approximately 5 cm below the carina
If full conditioning occurs deeper than normal, more lower-airway mucosa must supply heat and water to the inspired gas.
  • Mouth breathing
  • Cold/dry inspired gas
  • Artificial airway
  • High minute ventilation
  • Lower airways must provide more heat and moisture
  • Drying burden increases
Artificial airways and high ventilatory demand move the problem distal—exactly where airway drying becomes more dangerous.
  • Nasal passages
  • Pharynx
  • Larynx
  • Much of normal upper-airway heat/moisture exchange
  • Dry gas can reach the trachea directly
  • The tracheobronchial tree must replace the missing conditioning
Humidification is not optional comfort care for an artificial airway; it supports secretion mobility, mucosal integrity, and airway patency.
  • Thicker secretions
  • Reduced ciliary function
  • Mucosal irritation
  • Secretion retention
  • Airway obstruction risk
  • Secretion thickness
  • Airway patency
  • Need for suction
  • Current humidification method
  • Minute ventilation / gas flow demand
When secretions become thick and difficult to clear in an artificial-airway patient, think inadequate airway conditioning until proven otherwise.
  • Water-holding capacity increases
  • RH falls
  • Water-holding capacity decreases
  • RH rises
  • If saturated gas cools enough, water condenses
High RH does not necessarily mean high actual water content. Always connect RH to the gas temperature and absolute humidity.
