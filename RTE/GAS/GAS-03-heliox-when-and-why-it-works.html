Socratic selection string

Who is most likely to benefit from heliox?

Match the gas to the physiology—not simply to a diagnosis label.

Core physiology

Lower density reduces the resistance penalty of turbulent flow

Heliox does not open the airway. It makes gas movement through a narrowed airway easier while definitive treatment addresses the cause.

Why helium helpsHelium is much less dense than nitrogen. Replacing nitrogen with helium lowers the density of the inspired mixture, which can reduce turbulent resistance and work of breathing.
Where it helps mostSelected upper-airway obstruction or severe obstructive conditions where narrowed airways and turbulent flow are major contributors to respiratory effort.
What it does not fixHeliox does not remove a foreign body, reverse airway edema, provide bronchodilation by itself, or secure a failing airway.
What limits itIf the patient needs a very high oxygen concentration, the helium fraction must fall. As helium falls, the density advantage and expected benefit decrease.
Exam move

Heliox is a bridge, not definitive therapy. Use it when the patient can receive enough helium to create a density benefit while the underlying problem is treated.

Clinical use & limitations

Recognize the pattern that makes heliox useful—or not useful

  • Croup
  • Upper-airway obstruction
  • Severe obstructive airway disease when turbulent flow is contributing to high work of breathing
  • Marked work of breathing
  • Obstructive/noisy airflow pattern
  • Oxygen requirement low enough to preserve a useful helium fraction
Choose heliox because low gas density can improve flow mechanics—not because every obstructive diagnosis automatically needs it.
  • 80% helium / 20% oxygen
  • Largest helium fraction of the two source mixtures
  • Source flow-correction factor ×1.8 on an oxygen-calibrated flowmeter
  • 70% helium / 30% oxygen
  • More oxygen but less helium density advantage
  • Source flow-correction factor ×1.6 on an oxygen-calibrated flowmeter
Increasing the oxygen fraction may be necessary for oxygenation, but every increase leaves less helium available to provide the low-density effect.
  • 80/20: indicated flow × 1.8
  • 70/30: indicated flow × 1.6
  • Heliox is less dense than oxygen
  • It passes through the same flowmeter/orifice faster than the oxygen-calibrated reading suggests
Apply these factors only to the oxygen-calibrated flowmeter setup taught in the source lesson. Compatible heliox-specific devices may handle calibration differently.
  • Use a compatible delivery system
  • Verify the oxygen being delivered
  • Provide adequate total flow
  • Monitor work of breathing and gas exchange
  • Definitive airway treatment
  • Bronchodilator or edema treatment when indicated
  • Escalation planning if obstruction worsens
Starting heliox should never pause treatment of the cause of the airway narrowing.
  • Increasing oxygen requirement
  • Increasing fatigue
  • Rising CO₂
  • Altered mental status
  • Worsening obstruction or inability to maintain the airway
  • The patient may have moved beyond heliox’s useful role
  • More definitive ventilatory or airway support may be required
A patient who requires so much oxygen that little helium remains may no longer receive enough density benefit for heliox to be the right bridge.
