Apnea, Low Minute Ventilation & Backup Ventilation

When spontaneous ventilation falls or stops, determine whether the patient stopped breathing, the ventilator failed to detect the effort, delivered volume fell, or the circuit/device is losing support. Backup ventilation protects the patient while the cause is identified.

Primary: II.A.9 Mechanical ventilatorsShared: III.C.3d Ventilator alarms
Apnea alarmNo spontaneous breath detected within the set interval.
Low minute ventilationExhaled ventilation has fallen below the expected level.
Patient causesSedation, fatigue, weakness, loss of effort.
System causesTrigger failure, leak/disconnection, lost volume, circuit/device problem.
Socratic alarm string

Did the patient stop breathing—or did the ventilator stop seeing the breath?

Apnea / low-ventilation framework

Patient effort → trigger → delivered breath → exhaled ventilation

1. Is the patient making spontaneous effort?

This RTE review evaluating patient effort and sedation when apnea or low-minute-ventilation alarms occur.

2. Is the effort being detected?

Trigger problems can make a breathing patient appear apneic to the ventilator. Review trigger behavior rather than assuming true apnea immediately.

3. Is adequate volume being delivered and returned?

Low minute ventilation can reflect fewer breaths, lower VT, a leak/disconnection, or another circuit problem that reduces exhaled volume.

4. Is backup ventilation available and functioning?

Your prior PulmoLearn source describes a backup rate as protection against gaps or fatigue in spontaneous effort.

Backup ventilation is support—not the diagnosis.

When apnea or low ventilation occurs, verify that backup support is functioning, then identify why spontaneous ventilation fell or why the ventilator failed to detect it.

Source boundary: the retrieved PulmoLearn source supports the concept of a backup rate and this RTE review explicitly requires apnea, sedation, trigger, circuit and backup-ventilation troubleshooting. It does not provide a universal invasive-ventilator apnea interval or one numeric backup-rate target for all patients, so this lesson does not invent one.

Primary II.A.9

An apnea alarm sounds. What information is most important first?

Trigger problem

The patient is visibly trying to inhale, but several efforts do not produce ventilator breaths

Sedation

Apnea alarms begin shortly after sedation is increased and spontaneous effort has markedly decreased

Low minute ventilation

Exhaled minute ventilation falls while respiratory rate is unchanged

Backup ventilation

A spontaneously breathing patient has intermittent pauses and fatigue

Circuit issue

Patient effort continues, but exhaled VT and minute ventilation suddenly fall after the circuit is moved

Integrated RTE-style apnea case

Do not confuse absent detected breaths with absent patient effort

Spontaneously breathing ventilated patient:
  • Apnea and low-minute-ventilation alarms recur
  • Visible inspiratory effort is present
  • Several efforts do not trigger ventilator breaths
  • Expiratory flow does not return to baseline before the next breath
  • Backup breaths are being delivered
