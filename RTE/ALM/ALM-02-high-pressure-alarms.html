High-Pressure Alarms

A high-pressure alarm means the ventilator encountered increased opposition to gas flow or lung inflation. First assess the patient. Then use peak and plateau pressure, the circuit, airway findings, and bedside exam to separate resistance from reduced compliance.

Primary: II.A.9 Mechanical ventilatorsShared: III.C.3d Ventilator alarms

Socratic alarm string

Why did pressure rise?

Fast high-pressure framework

Patient first → PIP/Pplat → bedside cause

Resistance problem

PIP rises while Pplat stays stable. Source causes: secretions, bronchospasm, tube obstruction, biting, kinked tubing, or water.

Compliance problem

PIP and Pplat both rise. Source causes include ARDS, pulmonary edema, atelectasis, pneumothorax, abdominal distention, and severe patient-ventilator asynchrony.

Patient/circuit problem

Look for coughing/fighting the ventilator, biting, kinks, pooled water, tube obstruction, coarse rhonchi, wheeze, chest asymmetry, and acute deterioration.

Do not raise the limit first

Your faculty guide explicitly calls this a misconception. Raising the high-pressure limit can mask the problem instead of correcting it.

Numbers narrow the problem; bedside findings identify the cause.

Your source says to confirm with breath sounds, chest movement, suction-catheter passage, waveforms, hemodynamics, and manual-ventilation resistance when needed.
