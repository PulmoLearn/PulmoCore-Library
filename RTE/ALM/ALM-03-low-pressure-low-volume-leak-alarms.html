Low-Pressure, Low-Volume & Leak Alarms

When expected pressure cannot be generated or exhaled volume suddenly falls, think lost gas first: disconnection, cuff leak, circuit leak, tube displacement, or another source of volume loss. Assess the patient, then trace the airway and circuit from ventilator to patient.

Where did the pressure or volume go?

Fast low-pressure framework

Patient → airway → cuff → circuit → delivered/exhaled volume

1. Assess the patient.

If the patient is unstable or not being effectively ventilated, use the ALM-01 patient-first response and provide backup manual ventilation.

2. Trace every connection.

Your source says to inspect every connection from ventilator to patient when pressure or exhaled volume suddenly falls.

3. Check the artificial airway.

Verify tube depth/marking, cuff pressure, pilot balloon, tube position, and whether accidental displacement is possible.

4. Compare delivered versus exhaled volume.

A large gap supports leak or lost volume; a near-zero return with low pressure raises concern for major disconnection or tube displacement.

Low pressure can be deceptively quiet.

Your source warns that the ventilator may be delivering gas into the room instead of into the patient. Do not mistake a quiet alarm for a low-risk problem.
