Ventilator Troubleshooting Framework: Patient First

An alarm is a clue. The patient assessment determines urgency and the next action. If the patient is unstable or ventilation is ineffective, support the patient first—then troubleshoot the airway, circuit, ventilator, and source.

What comes first when the ventilator alarms?

Patient-first sequence

Stabilize first, then isolate the problem

1. Is the patient unstable?

Your source lists severe distress, cyanosis, altered mental status, hypotension, absent chest rise, or rapidly falling SpO₂.

2. Is ventilation occurring?

Observe chest movement, exhaled volume, breath sounds, capnography, color, and respiratory effort.

3. Is the airway/circuit intact?

Check the tube, cuff, connections, humidifier, filters, water traps, and tubing.

4. What changed?

Compare current pressures, volumes, waveforms, breath sounds, and hemodynamics with the prior trend.

If unstable or ventilation is ineffective: disconnect and manually ventilate with 100% oxygen.

Your source explicitly places manual ventilation before detailed ventilator troubleshooting when immediate support is needed.

Do not troubleshoot the machine while the patient waits. Your faculty guide names this as a common misconception and states that manual ventilation and patient support come first.

A stable patient with a brief cough may only need observation and reassessment.
An unstable patient with absent chest rise requires immediate support before a detailed search for the cause.
Primary II.A.9 angle: recognizing a device/alarm problem is not enough; the safest troubleshooting action depends on whether the patient is being effectively ventilated.
Your source says: if the patient is unstable or ventilation is not effective, disconnect from the ventilator and manually ventilate with 100% oxygen.
This both supports the patient and helps separate patient/airway problems from ventilator/circuit problems.
Shared III.E.1 angle: when a severe complication or device failure threatens the patient, stop relying on the failing setup and provide effective backup support.
Check the artificial airway itself: position, cuff, patency, biting/kinking, secretions, and whether a suction catheter can pass when appropriate.
The neonatal source provides the same patient-first logic: low pressure plus almost no exhaled VT and a missing ETT marking should be treated as accidental extubation with immediate manual ventilation while the airway is re-established.
Inspect loose/disconnected connections, kinked tubing, water in dependent limbs, humidifier connections, filters, and water traps.
A circuit problem may create either high resistance or lost pressure/volume depending on the fault; ALM-02 and ALM-03 will separate those alarm-specific differentials.
Your source includes low FiO₂/gas-supply alarms from empty cylinder, pipeline failure, disconnected hose, blender problems, or sensor problems.
When the patient is stable enough to troubleshoot, verify the ventilator setup, power/gas source, settings, measured values, and whether the alarm corresponds to the bedside condition.
High pressure: the ventilator required more pressure than allowed.
Low pressure: expected pressure was not reached.
Low exhaled VT / low minute ventilation: less volume returned than expected.
Apnea: no spontaneous breath occurred within the set interval.

The specific alarm differentials are taught in ALM-02 through ALM-05. ALM-01 teaches the common response framework first.

After correcting the suspected problem, reassess chest rise, SpO₂, breath sounds, exhaled VT, pressures, waveforms, hemodynamics, and the alarm condition.
If the patient does not respond as expected, return to the patient-first sequence and escalate the respiratory care plan/emergency response rather than assuming the problem is solved.
Primary II.A.9

A ventilator alarm sounds. What is the first clinical action?

Unstable patient

SpO₂ is falling rapidly, there is no chest rise, and effective ventilation is not occurring

Shared III.C.3d: ventilator alarm

A high-pressure alarm occurs, but the patient is stable and coughing briefly

Airway problem

Low pressure + almost no exhaled VT + ETT marking is no longer visible

Shared III.E.1: severe complication

After repositioning, high pressure occurs with SpO₂ 78%, BP 76/40, and absent right breath sounds

Circuit/device troubleshooting

The patient is stable and ventilating; the alarm began after tubing was moved

Reassessment

A loose circuit connection is corrected and the alarm stops
