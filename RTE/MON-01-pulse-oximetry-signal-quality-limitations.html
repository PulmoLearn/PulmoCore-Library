Pulse Oximetry: Signal Quality & Limitations

An SpO₂ value is only useful if the monitor is tracking a believable arterial pulsatile signal. Before acting on an unexpected number, assess the patient and verify the pleth waveform, pulse agreement, perfusion, probe placement, motion, light interference, and dyshemoglobin limitations.

Can you trust the SpO₂?

Trust-the-number framework

Assess the patient and verify the signal at the same time

1. Patient first

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, work of breathing, color, and mental status. Do not blindly accept or dismiss an unexpected saturation.

2. Check pulse agreement

Compare the monitor pulse with a palpated or independently measured pulse. A mismatch reduces confidence that the oximeter is tracking true arterial pulsations.

3. Check pleth and perfusion

Poor perfusion, shock, hypotension, hypothermia, vasoconstriction, and motion can weaken or distort the pulsatile signal.

4. Check the probe and interference

Verify sensor size, alignment, contact, site, motion, ambient light, nail coverings, venous pulsation, and other patient/device factors.

SpO₂ is an estimate of functional arterial oxygen saturation—not PaO₂, PaCO₂, hemoglobin concentration, or oxygen content.
Signal quality & limitations

Review each topic

A stable pleth waveform supports confidence that the device is detecting a pulsatile signal. A suddenly poor or erratic waveform—especially when the patient has not changed—should prompt probe/site/perfusion troubleshooting before the displayed SpO₂ is treated as unquestionably physiologic.
Your PulmoLearn monitor-detective activity specifically teaches comparing the monitor pulse rate with the patient’s palpated pulse. If they do not agree, confidence in the saturation value falls because the monitor may be tracking artifact instead of the true arterial pulse.
Shock, hypotension, hypothermia, vasoconstriction, and other low-perfusion states can weaken the peripheral signal. Consider warming/selecting a better-perfused site and reassessing while continuing to evaluate the patient.
Patient movement can distort pulse detection. Reduce motion when possible, reposition or replace the sensor, and reassess whether the pleth and pulse agreement improve.
Confirm correct sensor size, position, alignment, contact, and site. Bright ambient light, nail polish/artificial nails, some dyes, and poor optical contact may interfere with light transmission or interpretation.
Your faculty guide teaches that carbon monoxide exposure can produce a falsely reassuring standard SpO₂ because carboxyhemoglobin may be interpreted as oxyhemoglobin. Methemoglobinemia can make standard pulse oximetry unreliable and often pull the displayed value toward the mid-80% range. Use co-oximetry when the hemoglobin species are the clinical question.
A severely anemic patient can have normal SpO₂ and PaO₂ but low arterial oxygen content because fewer hemoglobin molecules are available to carry oxygen. Pulse oximetry does not measure hemoglobin concentration or oxygen content.
Your breakout guide emphasizes that direction and rate of change are more informative than one isolated reading. Interpret SpO₂ alongside respiratory rate, work of breathing, mental status, oxygen device, perfusion, and response to treatment.
Your existing review content describes overnight pulse oximetry as continuous saturation/pulse recording used to identify desaturation patterns and screen for sleep-related breathing problems. An apparent severe desaturation without internally consistent signal or physiologic changes may be artifact; verify signal quality before interpreting the event.
If the clinical question cannot be resolved with reliable pulse oximetry alone, choose the test that answers it: ABG for arterial gas tensions/acid-base status, co-oximetry for dyshemoglobin species, or additional sleep/noninvasive monitoring when the problem is nocturnal or episodic.
When the reading is questionable, assess the patient and device simultaneously: verify oxygen-delivery setup, probe placement, pulse agreement, perfusion, motion, light interference, cable/device function, and an alternate site/device if necessary.
Integrated RTE-style monitoring case

Do not chase artifact—and do not dismiss true deterioration

New monitor alarm:
  • SpO₂ suddenly falls from 95% to 82%
  • Patient is awake, speaking normally, and not visibly distressed
  • Pleth waveform is erratic
  • Monitor pulse reads 138/min
  • Palpated pulse is 82/min
  • Hand is cold and the patient is moving the finger
