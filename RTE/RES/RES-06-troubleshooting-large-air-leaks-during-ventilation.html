Troubleshooting Large Air Leaks During Ventilation

A large leak can come from the ventilator circuit, the artificial airway, tube displacement, or the thorax. The RTE task is not merely to recognize lost volume—it is to identify the most likely leak pathway and choose the safest corrective action while preserving ventilation.

Large-leak troubleshooting framework

Patient → circuit → cuff/tube → thorax

1. Assess urgency first.

Your source says sudden loss of exhaled volume in an intubated patient is more urgent than a small stable leak. Ask whether the patient is unstable, pressure also fell, an audible leak is present, or tube depth changed.

2. Inspect the circuit.

Check all connections and components for disconnection or loose fit before assuming the problem is inside the chest.

3. Inspect cuff and tube position.

Pair exhaled VT and the waveform with cuff pressure, audible leak, tube depth, and airway inspection before choosing an intervention.

4. Consider intrathoracic leak.

If cuff/circuit are intact but exhaled volume remains low, correlate with chest-tube bubbling, pneumothorax findings, or other evidence of BPF/chest-tube air leak.

The volume-time waveform tells you a leak exists—not where it is.

Your source explicitly says a failure of volume to return to baseline can occur with cuff leak, loose circuit connection, bronchopleural fistula, or chest-tube air leak.

Leak-source discriminators

0 of 8 reviewedReview all eight topics.
During inspiration volume rises; during exhalation it should return to baseline.
If it remains above baseline, some delivered volume was not measured returning through the expiratory limb.
A sudden leak after tubing manipulation strongly supports a loose/disconnected circuit component.
Correct the connection and verify recovery of pressure, exhaled VT, chest rise and oxygenation.
A large audible oral/tracheal leak with low exhaled VT should prompt cuff manometry, pilot-balloon assessment, and airway-position verification.
Do not simply increase inspiratory pressure to compensate before correcting the leak source.
Changed tube depth, loss of chest rise, near-zero exhaled VT, or missing external depth marking raises concern for displacement/accidental extubation.
The safest response is to support ventilation and re-establish/verify the airway rather than turning up ventilator pressure.
If circuit and cuff are intact but exhaled VT remains low, persistent chest-drain bubbling supports an ongoing intrathoracic leak such as BPF.
Use a pressure-limiting strategy and reassess chest-tube function rather than increasing pressure until returned VT matches delivered VT.
ALM-01’s patient-first rule still applies: if ventilation is ineffective or the patient is unstable, provide immediate backup/manual ventilation while the leak source is identified.
A ventilator leak is a device problem only until the patient is not being ventilated—then it is a patient emergency.
A major external leak often lowers achieved pressure as well as exhaled volume.
An intrathoracic leak can also reduce returned volume, so pressure behavior must be interpreted with cuff, circuit, tube position, chest tube and patient findings.
After correcting the suspected source, reassess airway pressure, delivered/exhaled VT, waveform return to baseline, chest rise, breath sounds, SpO₂, hemodynamics, and chest-drain behavior when present.
If the leak persists, continue through the differential rather than assuming the first abnormality was the only cause.

Circuit leak

Cuff leak

Exhaled VT drops and a loud leak is heard around the ETT

Tube displacement

Near-zero exhaled VT + no chest rise + previous tube-depth marking no longer visible

Intrathoracic leak

Circuit and cuff are intact, but large VT loss persists with continuous chest-tube bubbling

Unstable leak

Patient is cyanotic with absent chest rise and a large leak alarm

Reassessment

A loose circuit connection is corrected

Integrated RTE-style large-leak case

Use the clues to locate the leak

Ventilated patient with chest tube:
  • Delivered VT 500 mL; exhaled VT 220 mL
  • Volume-time waveform does not return to baseline
  • No audible oral leak
  • Cuff pressure and tube depth are unchanged
  • All circuit connections are intact
  • Continuous chest-tube bubbling is present
  • SpO₂ is acceptable; PaCO₂ is mildly elevated
