Emergency Planning for Home Respiratory Equipment

A power outage or device failure is a poor time to invent a backup plan. Patients and caregivers should know which equipment needs electricity, where backup oxygen or ventilation is kept, who to call, and what breathing changes need emergency services. Build the plan around the prescribed support, practice it, and check that it works for the whole household.

What happens when support fails?

Home emergency planning framework

Failure → patient check → backup → help

Plan for lost power

List concentrators, ventilators, positive airway pressure devices, suction, and other equipment that require electricity. Know battery run time and the patient's backup support. An oxygen concentrator may need a non-electric oxygen source; a ventilator-dependent patient needs the prescribed power and manual ventilation plan.

Plan for equipment or airway failure

Label and locate backup oxygen, tubing, suction, batteries, manual resuscitation equipment, and spare airway supplies as appropriate. When a device fails, check the patient's breathing first, use the trained backup, and call the supplier or care team while arranging urgent help if support cannot be maintained.

Set clear escalation triggers

Teach the patient's usual breathing pattern and warning changes. Severe breathing difficulty, blue or gray color, new unresponsiveness, an airway emergency, or inability to provide needed support calls for emergency services. Routine supply questions go to the supplier or care team.

Practice the whole plan

Keep a written plan with the address, equipment details, treatment orders, supplier number, care team, emergency contacts, and a destination if evacuation is needed. Ask the patient and each caregiver to demonstrate switching to backup and to describe whom to call. Revise the plan when support or caregivers change.

Core home emergency rule: check the patient, start the trained backup if support is interrupted, and seek emergency help when breathing is unsafe or cannot be restored.
Power safety: registering medical equipment with the utility is useful preparation, but the home still needs a working backup. A portable generator must run outdoors, at least 20 feet from doors, windows, and vents.
Home emergency checkpoints

Review each concept

Check what happens if electricity stops for the oxygen concentrator, ventilator, suction, positive airway pressure unit, and any monitor. Document power needs and backup options for each essential device.
A prescribed home concentrator depends on power. Confirm that the ordered non-electric backup oxygen is present, usable, and sufficient for the plan; teach the correct patient-specific switch procedure.
If a patient depends on a ventilator, verify battery, backup ventilator or manual ventilation equipment as prescribed. A trained caregiver should demonstrate the response to device failure and know when to call emergency services.
For a tracheostomy, check the patient-specific emergency kit, suction, and spare airway equipment. Practice the trained response to blockage or displacement. Call emergency services if breathing cannot be restored.
Ask the care team which changes require a routine call, an urgent call, or emergency services. Severe breathing trouble, new unresponsiveness, blue or gray color, and failure of essential support require emergency action.
Post supplier, care team, emergency contacts, and the home address near the equipment. Make sure caregivers can find the details even if phone service, lighting, or the usual caregiver is unavailable.
Ask the utility about medical-equipment registration and tell local responders about critical needs when appropriate. This step does not replace batteries, oxygen, or an evacuation option.
If the household uses a portable generator, run it outdoors at least 20 feet from doors, windows, and vents to prevent carbon monoxide poisoning. Check compatibility and safe connections with qualified providers before relying on it.
Identify a destination that can support the therapy, transport for the patient and equipment, extra supplies, and a copy of treatment orders. Set a departure trigger before backup supply is exhausted.
Ask each patient or caregiver to find the backup, show the switch or emergency step, and explain when to call for help. Correct errors, repeat the drill, and document who can carry out the plan.
Integrated RTE-style home-care case

Power loss with a breathing change

A patient on prescribed oxygen from a home concentrator loses power:
  • The patient becomes very short of breath and looks blue around the lips
  • A prescribed backup cylinder is available and the caregiver was trained to switch
  • The power company has been notified about the medical device
  • The caregiver asks whether to wait for power to return
