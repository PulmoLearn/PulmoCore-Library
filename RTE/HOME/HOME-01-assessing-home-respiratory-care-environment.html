Assessing the Home Respiratory-Care Environment

A home treatment plan must work in the room where the patient actually lives. Walk through equipment placement, power, oxygen and fire safety, walking paths, storage, emergency access, and the help available from caregivers. Ask the patient or caregiver to show how the plan will work; correct hazards before relying on the setup.

Home safety assessment

Environment → equipment → people → emergency plan

Walk the actual path

See where the patient sleeps, sits, bathes, and leaves the home. Check that tubing and cords do not block walking or wheelchair routes. Place the device so the patient can use it as prescribed without pulling on tubing or climbing around obstacles.

Check oxygen and fire risk

Ask whether anyone smokes or uses open flames in the home. Keep oxygen away from flames and heat, follow the equipment supplier's placement instructions, and secure and store cylinders according to the supplier's directions. Oxygen makes a fire burn faster.

Confirm power and supply

Identify a safe outlet, where the device will sit, whether its alarms can be heard, and what happens during a power outage. Check the working backup, oxygen supply, replacement schedule, and the supplier's contact number before the equipment is needed.

Verify the human plan

Ask who can help, when they are available, and whether the patient can set up or troubleshoot the device. Use teach-back: after showing a step, have the patient or caregiver explain and demonstrate it in their own words. Practice when to call the supplier, care team, or emergency services.

Core home rule: a safe plan requires suitable space, a working device and backup, and someone who can recognize a problem and respond.
Teach-back check: a patient saying “I understand” does not show that they can locate the backup source, prevent a tubing trip, or describe whom to call.
Home environment checkpoints

Review each concept

Watch the patient move between normal activity areas. Route tubing and cords to reduce trips while allowing the prescribed therapy to reach the patient. Consider stairs, walkers, wheelchairs, and bathroom access.
Identify the planned outlet and confirm the device can be used safely there. Ask whether outages occur, where the backup is stored, and whether the patient can operate it. A power-dependent device needs a workable outage plan.
Ask directly about smoking by anyone in the home, candles, stoves, fireplaces, and other heat sources. Oxygen supports burning; follow the supplier's safety instructions and keep it away from flames and heat.
Check that oxygen equipment is secured, ventilated, and stored as the supplier directs, away from heat and impact. Verify that the patient can reach the correct supply without moving heavy equipment unsafely.
Confirm the address, building or apartment entry, phone access, emergency contact, and how help can reach the patient. Teach what symptoms or equipment failures require emergency services.
Find out who is present during treatment or overnight, what each person can do, and whether they can hear alarms and operate backup equipment. Plan for times the usual helper is absent.
Check device setup, oxygen supply, tubing, replacement items, delivery schedule, and the supplier's phone number. Know whom to call for routine service and for urgent device failure.
Show one task at a time, use the actual equipment, and invite questions. Ask the patient or caregiver to explain the plan in their own words instead of asking only if they understand.
Have the person show how to position the device, trace the tubing, identify the backup, and describe the response to an alarm or power failure. Correct and repeat any missed steps.
Record hazards found, corrections completed, remaining barriers, patient or caregiver demonstration, and who will resolve any outstanding problem. Arrange follow-up when the setup changes.
Integrated RTE-style home-care case

Verify the plan in the room

A patient will use a home oxygen concentrator:
  • The power cord crosses the only route to the bathroom
  • Family members sometimes smoke indoors
  • The patient cannot locate the backup cylinder or supplier's number
  • The patient says, “I understand how to use it”
