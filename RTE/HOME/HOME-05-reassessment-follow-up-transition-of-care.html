Reassessment, Follow-Up & Transition of Care

Respiratory needs can change after discharge. Compare the original oxygen and equipment orders with the current patient, treatment use, device function, and measurements at rest and during activity. Report gaps and changes to the prescribing team, confirm the updated plan with the supplier, and have the patient or caregiver explain what to do next.

Home follow-up framework

Baseline → current findings → plan → handoff

Read the prior record

Review why oxygen or equipment was ordered, the discharge date, prior oxygen measurements, ordered flow or settings for rest, activity, and sleep, and the planned follow-up. Ask what the patient actually received and uses at home. This patient-history step gives current findings context.

Assess the patient and device

Ask about symptoms, activity, sleep, treatment use, and side effects. Measure oxygenation in the situations the care plan calls for, such as rest and walking; interpret the results with the full clinical picture. Check fit, tubing, power, and alarms before assuming symptoms mean the disease worsened.

Coordinate a change

Some people need less oxygen as they recover from a short illness; others need more support. Send the clinician the baseline, current findings, and any device problem. Obtain a new order or direction before changing prescribed flow or stopping therapy, and make sure the supplier receives the updated plan.

Close the loop

Give a clear handoff: current support, trend, patient concerns, actions taken, pending orders, and the next check. Ask the patient or caregiver to explain when and how to use treatment, whom to contact, and which symptoms require urgent help.

Core follow-up rule: compare today's findings with the documented baseline, check whether equipment works, and coordinate prescribed changes before teaching the new plan.
History cue: a single normal reading while oxygen is running does not prove that oxygen is no longer needed. Review the original reason for therapy and the order for rest, activity, and sleep.
Follow-up checkpoints

Review each concept

Compare the discharge diagnosis, oxygen measurements, and original reason for therapy with the patient's current report. Note whether oxygen was ordered after an acute illness and when reassessment was planned.
Check the prescribed flow or device settings at rest, with activity, and during sleep. Confirm the actual equipment delivered and what the patient uses. Resolve differences between the discharge order and home setup with the team.
Ask what the patient can do now compared with discharge, whether shortness of breath or fatigue has changed, and whether new sleep or activity problems appeared. Escalate concerning symptoms promptly.
Check oxygen saturation and the patient's condition at rest and, when ordered or safe, during activity. A reading on oxygen answers a different question from a supervised reassessment of the ongoing oxygen need.
Ask when therapy is used and what gets in the way. Mask discomfort, noise, dry air, tubing length, cost, or confusion can explain missed treatment. Treat the barrier rather than labeling the patient as unmotivated.
Inspect cannula or mask fit, tubing kinks or leaks, power, supply, and alarm history. Contact the supplier for a faulty device. A poor setup may cause symptoms that look like a change in medical need.
Report the baseline, current symptoms and measurements, and equipment findings to the prescribing team. Follow the clinician's direction for reassessment or a new prescription; communicate approved changes to the supplier and patient.
Share current oxygen or device support, the trend since discharge, specific barriers, what you checked, any urgent finding, the pending decision, and the person responsible for follow-up.
Have the patient or caregiver explain the current order, when to use the device, what changes are pending, and whom to call for symptoms or equipment failure. Repeat teaching if the plan cannot be described accurately.
Confirm when and where the patient will be reviewed, which measurements are needed, and how the results will reach the prescriber. Document open tasks so a recommended change does not get lost between teams.
Integrated RTE-style follow-up case

Compare before changing therapy

Two weeks after discharge with oxygen for an acute lung illness:
  • The discharge order says 2 L/min at rest and 3 L/min with activity
  • The patient feels better and has a resting saturation of 96% while on 2 L/min
  • No walking reassessment or new order is documented
  • The tubing is kinked where it passes behind a chair
