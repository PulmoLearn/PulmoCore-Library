Home Ventilation & Tracheostomy Care

Home ventilation can depend on a tracheostomy, a tube placed through an opening in the neck into the airway. Caregivers must be able to check the patient, respond to alarms, manage secretions, maintain humidification, and use the prescribed backup plan. The respiratory therapist verifies these skills by watching a demonstration, including an airway emergency drill.

Home airway and ventilation framework

Patient → airway → circuit → backup → teach-back

Start with the patient

When an alarm sounds, look at breathing, color, chest movement, and responsiveness. Then inspect the tracheostomy tube and ventilator circuit. A normal-looking display does not prove that the patient is getting effective breaths.

Maintain a clear airway

Caregivers learn the prescribed suction steps, signs of retained secretions, and routine tracheostomy care. Humidification helps prevent thick, dry secretions. They must know the trained response if the tube becomes blocked or comes out.

Keep working backups

Check the ordered ventilator settings, alarms, power plan, portable suction, manual ventilation equipment, and patient-size spare tracheostomy tubes. Practice the care-plan steps for a failed device or airway emergency, including when to call emergency services.

Verify who can help

Identify every caregiver's role and availability. For a child dependent on invasive home ventilation, an awake, attentive, trained caregiver should be present. Have caregivers show normal care and emergency actions with the actual equipment; repeat training when a step is missed.

Core safety rule: respond to the patient's breathing first, then troubleshoot equipment while preparing the trained backup and calling for help when needed.
Airway warning: a blocked or displaced tracheostomy can rapidly stop ventilation. Follow the individualized emergency plan and call emergency services for unresolved breathing difficulty.
Caregiver competency checkpoints

Review each concept

When an alarm sounds, check breathing effort, chest rise, color, responsiveness, and the patient's usual monitoring values. Treat visible breathing trouble as urgent even if the device screen appears normal.
Have the caregiver identify prescribed ventilator settings, the power source, tubing route, and secure airway connection. They should recognize a disconnected circuit without changing settings independently.
Demonstrate what common alarm messages mean for this particular device. Check the patient and likely cause before silencing an alarm. Repeat the drill with a simulated disconnection or blocked airway.
Review the patient's prescribed suction technique, catheter size, equipment, and signs that suction is needed. Observe the caregiver perform a safe return demonstration; check that portable suction and its backup power work.
Check the prescribed humidifier or other moisture plan, water supply, setup, and cleaning. Thick secretions, repeated plugging, or a dry airway warrant reassessment of the patient and the setup.
Have the caregiver show hand hygiene, cleaning or changing parts as ordered, secure tube ties, skin checks, and supply storage. The exact steps depend on the tube and individualized care plan.
Keep a spare tracheostomy tube of the same size and a smaller size, plus the necessary replacement supplies, with a child using a tracheostomy as directed. Check that caregivers can find them quickly and know their own trained role.
Check the working manual resuscitation setup and any backup ventilator or battery. Have caregivers demonstrate the patient-specific backup ventilation sequence and call for emergency help when the plan requires it.
Identify the trained caregiver for each period. A child on chronic invasive home ventilation needs an awake, attentive, trained caregiver nearby at all times, with relief coverage planned.
Observe normal setup, alarm response, suction, tracheostomy care, and the emergency drill. Correct mistakes, repeat the demonstration, document remaining gaps, and plan more training before the caregiver assumes independent responsibility.
Integrated RTE-style home-care case

Respond to the patient and alarm

A child with a tracheostomy uses invasive ventilation at home:
  • A high-pressure alarm sounds and chest rise is poor
  • The caregiver sees thick secretions and the child looks distressed
  • The trained caregiver has suction and backup equipment nearby
  • Another family member offers to silence the alarm
