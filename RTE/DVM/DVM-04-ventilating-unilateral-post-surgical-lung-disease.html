Ventilating Unilateral & Post-Surgical Lung Disease

When one lung is absent or one side is much more affected than the other, the respiratory system is asymmetric. Ventilator settings, positioning, imaging, pressure/volume response, postoperative lung expansion, and hemodynamics must be interpreted as one clinical picture.

How does losing or impairing one lung change the strategy?

Asymmetric-lung framework

Protect the functioning lung and reassess the whole patient

1. Identify what changed anatomically.

After lobectomy or pneumonectomy there is real volume loss. Your PulmoLearn imaging source teaches that this can pull mediastinal/tracheal structures toward the operated side rather than pushing them away.

2. Reduce excessive delivered volume after pneumonectomy.

Your RTE review source uses a board-style pneumonectomy rule: reduce VT substantially because only one lung remains, rather than using the same full-lung volume.

3. Use PEEP and oxygen thoughtfully.

The pneumonectomy example in your review source uses modest PEEP and high initial FiO₂ because the patient arrived on 100% oxygen, followed by reassessment.

4. Positioning is part of oxygenation support.

The registry explicitly requires positioning for unilateral disease. The retrieved sources support ordered positioning with response monitoring, but do not give one universal side-specific rule for every postoperative condition.

5. Anticipate postoperative atelectasis.

Your postoperative source links splinting, shallow breathing, diminished bases, low lung volumes and new oxygen need with postoperative atelectasis and recommends upright positioning, oxygen as needed and lung-expansion coaching.

6. Reassess for complications.

Sudden unilateral breath-sound loss, severe desaturation, hypotension, or unexpected pressure change should trigger evaluation for complications such as pneumothorax, tube malposition, obstruction or pleural problems.

One-lung or uneven-lung disease is not just “normal ventilation with a different diagnosis.”

The remaining/functioning lung can be overdistended if full bilateral-lung volumes are delivered. Use smaller protective delivery, monitor pressure and exhaled volume, support oxygenation, and reassess after every major change.

Source boundary: the registry requires positioning, but the retrieved PulmoLearn/RTE source set does not specify one universal “good lung down” or post-pneumonectomy side-position rule. This lesson therefore teaches ordered, physiology-guided positioning with response monitoring rather than inventing an unsupported universal rule.

When one lung is markedly less compliant or absent, ventilation is not distributed symmetrically.
Interpret exhaled VT, pressure, breath sounds, imaging and oxygenation together rather than assuming both lungs receive equal ventilation.
Your RTE review case states that after a whole-lung pneumonectomy, VT should be reduced to about half of the usual full-lung volume, or somewhat higher, rather than using the previous bilateral-lung volume.
That same review case selects 275 mL in a 64-kg patient rather than 500–650 mL.
Teaching use: treat this as a board-style pneumonectomy rule from the supplied source, not a universal bedside prescription. Reassess pressures, exhaled volume, ABG and the remaining lung.
In the supplied pneumonectomy question, the patient arrives being bagged with FiO₂ 1.0; the selected initial ventilator answer continues FiO₂ 1.0 and uses PEEP 5 cmH₂O.
The explanation states that PEEP in the 4–6 cmH₂O range is appropriate in that specific review example.
After stabilization, titrate oxygen and PEEP based on oxygenation, mechanics, surgical status and hemodynamics rather than leaving initial rescue settings unchanged automatically.
The DVM-04 registry explicitly includes positioning and ventilation of unevenly affected lungs.
Your air-leak/unilateral-disease PulmoLearn source says to implement ordered positioning in selected unilateral disease patterns and monitor response.
Reassess SpO₂, breath sounds, pressures, exhaled VT and hemodynamics after repositioning. Postoperative surgical restrictions and pleural/chest-tube considerations can alter which position is appropriate.
Your chest-imaging lesson teaches: atelectasis, lobectomy/pneumonectomy and fibrosis can reduce volume and pull the mediastinum/trachea toward the affected side.
In contrast, large pleural effusion, tension pneumothorax or a large mass may create pressure/space and push structures away.
Do not use tracheal shift alone. Pair it with lung density, volume loss/gain, peripheral markings, breath sounds and hemodynamics.
Your postoperative case describes incision splinting, shallow breathing, RR 24/min, SpO₂ 89% on room air, diminished bases and bibasilar low-volume opacities.
The source-supported first RT response is to assess severity, support oxygenation as needed, position upright and begin lung-expansion coaching.
On an invasively ventilated postoperative patient, use the same logic to evaluate low lung volume/atelectasis while also checking tube position, secretions, pressures and surgical complications.
Sudden unilateral breath-sound loss, severe desaturation and hypotension require emergency evaluation for pneumothorax or another acute obstruction.
Unexpected low pressure/low volume can indicate disconnection or tube displacement; unexpected high pressure can indicate obstruction, reduced compliance or pneumothorax.
After changing VT, PEEP, FiO₂ or position, reassess SpO₂, ABG/CO₂ trend, PIP/Pplat, exhaled VT, breath sounds, hemodynamics and imaging/clinical asymmetry.
If oxygenation improves but pressure or hemodynamics worsen, the adjustment is not fully successful.
