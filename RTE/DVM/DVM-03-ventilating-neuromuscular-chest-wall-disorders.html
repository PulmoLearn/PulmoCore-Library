Neuromuscular/chest-wall framework

Support the pump, then protect the airway and secretion pathway

1. Recognize ventilatory pump failure.

Your source classifies neuromuscular disease, spinal/chest-wall limitation and obesity-related hypoventilation as causes of hypoventilation rather than primary alveolar gas-exchange disease.

2. Do not trust SpO₂ alone.

Your myasthenia source specifically notes that normal SpO₂ does not exclude early ventilatory failure. Trend CO₂, respiratory mechanics, fatigue and airway protection.

3. Escalate before complete collapse.

Your PulmoLearn activity flags VC below about 10–15 mL/kg, spontaneous VT below 5 mL/kg, MIP/NIF magnitude below about 20 cmH₂O, or MEP below about 40 cmH₂O as neuromuscular red flags that must be interpreted with trend and airway assessment.

4. Protect the airway.

Bulbar weakness, dysphagia, aspiration risk, inability to manage secretions, or rapidly rising CO₂ can make invasive ventilation necessary even before severe hypoxemia develops.

5. Pair ventilation with secretion removal.

A weak cough means mobilized secretions may not leave the airway. Assisted cough, mechanical insufflation-exsufflation, and suction may be needed.

6. Plan liberation around recovery.

Acceptable gas exchange on the ventilator does not prove the patient can breathe, cough, protect the airway, and clear secretions independently.

Neuromuscular failure can look deceptively “quiet.”

A falling respiratory rate may mean fatigue—not improvement. Support ventilation and airway protection based on trend, mechanics, cough/bulbar status and CO₂, not distress appearance alone.

Neuromuscular weakness can produce Type 2 ventilatory failure because the bellows cannot move enough air even when the alveolar-capillary membrane is initially preserved.
The ventilator therefore primarily replaces inadequate muscle work and minute ventilation rather than treating a primary shunt problem.
Your GBS and myasthenia sources emphasize serial VC/FVC, NIF/MIP, cough strength, secretion burden, bulbar symptoms and CO₂ trends.
A rapid downward trend can be more concerning than one isolated value that has not yet crossed a threshold.
Scope: RST-03 will teach measurement technique and interpretation in depth. DVM-03 uses those findings to choose/escalate ventilatory support.
Your neuromuscular sources repeatedly flag bulbar weakness, dysphagia, weak cough, aspiration risk and secretion retention as escalation concerns.
A backup rate cannot protect an unsafe airway. If the patient cannot manage secretions or protect the airway, invasive support may be required even when NIV can move air.
Your secretion-management source explains that a patient with neuromuscular weakness may have relatively few secretions but still fail because the cough is too weak to remove them.
Mechanical insufflation-exsufflation or assisted cough can augment removal; suction is used when secretions/obstruction are accessible.
More mobilization without a removal strategy can leave mucus sitting in the central airway.
The DVM-03 registry does not prescribe one disease-specific invasive mode or numeric setting set. Use the core MVF/MVS framework: PBW-based VT, adequate RR/minute ventilation, appropriate PEEP/FiO₂, safe pressure limits and early reassessment.
Because the lungs may be relatively preserved, do not automatically apply an ARDS-style high-PEEP strategy unless the patient has a separate oxygenation/recruitment problem.
The registry’s chest-wall pathway includes kyphoscoliosis, obesity-related mechanics, chest-wall limitation and hypoventilation risk.
These patients may need ventilatory assistance because moving the respiratory system requires more pump effort even when the lung parenchyma itself is not the primary disease.
Reassess pressure and delivered VT together. Pressure needs may reflect chest-wall load as well as lung mechanics.
Your weaning source requires that the underlying cause be reversed and flags neurologic problems that affect spontaneous effort.
It also includes VC, VT, MIP/NIF and MEP among bedside liberation parameters.
DVM-03 adds the disease logic: extubation also depends on cough strength, secretion clearance and bulbar airway protection—not just passing an oxygenation target.
After initiating or adjusting support, trend exhaled VT/minute ventilation, PaCO₂, work of breathing, mechanics, cough, secretion clearance, airway protection and mental status.
If CO₂ rises, mechanics decline, bulbar function worsens or secretions cannot be managed, escalate rather than waiting for severe hypoxemia.
