Ventilating Obstructive Lung Disease

In severe COPD or asthma, exhalation is slow. The ventilator strategy must create enough time for the lungs to empty before the next breath. Lower rate, shorter inspiratory time, adequate inspiratory flow, careful tidal-volume/pressure selection, and close monitoring for auto-PEEP help reduce dynamic hyperinflation.

Primary: III.C.3a Invasive mechanical ventilation
Socratic strategy string

Give the lungs time to empty

Obstructive ventilation framework

Control dynamic hyperinflation before chasing CO₂

Lower respiratory rate

Fewer mandatory breaths create more total expiratory time.

Shorter inspiratory time / adequate flow

Deliver inspiration efficiently so more of the cycle is available for exhalation.

Watch expiratory flow

If expiratory flow does not return to baseline before the next breath, incomplete exhalation and auto-PEEP are likely.

Reassess perfusion

Dynamic hyperinflation raises intrathoracic pressure and can reduce venous return, causing hypotension.

Key logic: a rising PaCO₂ in severe obstruction does not automatically mean “increase the rate.” More breaths can worsen trapping, auto-PEEP, pressures, and hemodynamics.
Disease-specific decisions

Review each concept

Obstructive disease increases airway resistance and prolongs the expiratory time constant. Gas leaves the lungs more slowly, so short expiratory time promotes breath stacking and dynamic hyperinflation.
Use a lower rate when clinically appropriate to create more expiratory time. Increasing rate may worsen trapping even when PaCO₂ is high.
A shorter inspiratory time—often achieved with adequate inspiratory flow in volume control—leaves more of the respiratory cycle for exhalation.
Avoid unnecessarily large tidal volumes or excessive pressure that increase lung volume and make complete exhalation harder. Reassess exhaled volume, pressures, blood gas, and hemodynamics after changes.
Intrinsic PEEP is generated by incomplete exhalation. External PEEP decisions must be individualized and reassessed; the primary problem is not solved by blindly increasing PEEP when severe gas trapping is present.
Expiratory flow that fails to return to zero before the next breath is a major clue to incomplete emptying and dynamic hyperinflation.
Your PulmoLearn source allows controlled hypercapnia when clinically appropriate if accepting a higher PaCO₂ helps avoid excessive rate, pressure, breath stacking, and ventilator injury.
Ventilator timing does not replace treatment of bronchospasm, secretions, kinks, or other causes of increased airway resistance. Correct the cause and reassess the ventilator response.
Air trapping and elevated intrathoracic pressure can reduce venous return. Sudden hypotension in a severely obstructed ventilated patient should raise concern for severe dynamic hyperinflation as well as other emergencies.
After changes, reassess SpO₂, ETCO₂/ABG, airway pressures, exhaled tidal volume, expiratory flow, auto-PEEP clues, breath sounds, work/patient interaction, heart rate, blood pressure, and perfusion.
Integrated RTE-style case

Do not worsen trapping to normalize CO₂

Ventilated patient with severe asthma:
  • RR 20/min
  • Expiratory flow does not reach baseline before the next breath
  • Peak pressure is rising
  • Blood pressure is falling
  • PaCO₂ is elevated
