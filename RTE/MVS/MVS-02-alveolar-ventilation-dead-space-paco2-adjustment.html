Alveolar Ventilation, Dead Space & PaCO₂ Adjustment

Total minute ventilation tells you how much gas moves. Alveolar ventilation tells you how much fresh gas actually reaches gas-exchanging alveoli. PaCO₂ follows effective alveolar ventilation—not the ventilator rate alone.

Primary: I.C.7 Cardiopulmonary calculationsShared: I.D.7 Cardiopulmonary calculations

VDGas that does not participate in exchange.
VAEffective gas reaching exchanging alveoli.
PaCO₂Moves inversely with VA when CO₂ production is stable.

What part of the breath actually clears CO₂?

Core formulas

Separate total ventilation from effective ventilation

Minute ventilation
V̇E = VT × f

Total volume moved each minute.

Alveolar ventilation
V̇A = (VT − VD) × f

Dead space is subtracted from every breath before multiplying by frequency.

Physiologic dead space
VDphys = VDanatomic + VDalveolar

Anatomic dead space is conducting-airway gas. Alveolar dead space is ventilated alveoli with absent or inadequate perfusion.

PaCO₂ relationship
PaCO₂₁ × V̇A₁ = PaCO₂₂ × V̇A₂

Valid for proportional reasoning when metabolic CO₂ production remains reasonably stable.

Equal minute ventilation does not mean equal CO₂ clearance.

With fixed dead space, a slower deeper pattern wastes a smaller fraction of each breath than rapid shallow breathing. A high rate can coexist with poor alveolar ventilation.

Dead space & PaCO₂ reasoning

Open each topic once

0 of 7 reviewedReview all seven topics.
Anatomic: gas in the conducting airways that never reaches a gas-exchanging surface.
Alveolar: gas reaches alveoli that are ventilated but inadequately perfused.
Physiologic: anatomic + alveolar dead space. Your source notes that in healthy lungs alveolar dead space is small, so physiologic dead space approximates anatomic dead space.
Example: VT 500 mL, VD 150 mL, f 12/min.
(500 − 150) × 12 = 4200 mL/min = 4.2 L/min
Your AHRC 155 guide specifically teaches calculating the effective tidal portion first, then multiplying by rate, then converting units.
Your source comparison: 500 mL × 12 and 250 mL × 24 both produce VE = 6 L/min when VD = 150 mL.
But VA is 4.2 L/min in the deeper pattern and only 2.4 L/min in the rapid shallow pattern.
Why: the fixed dead-space cost consumes a larger fraction of each small tidal breath.
Your PulmoLearn acid-base practice states:
V̇A₂ / V̇A₁ = PaCO₂₁ / PaCO₂₂
If VA rises, PaCO₂ falls. If VA falls, PaCO₂ rises—when CO₂ production remains stable.
Important: this is proportional reasoning, not a promise that every patient’s PaCO₂ will change perfectly according to one equation during rapidly changing physiology.
Rearrange the inverse relationship:
Desired V̇A = Current V̇A × (Current PaCO₂ ÷ Desired PaCO₂)
Your legacy optimization assignment uses this same proportional approach by substituting minute ventilation, respiratory rate, or VT when the other relevant factors are being treated as stable.
Clinical safety: the result is an estimated target for a rational change. It must be checked against VT safety, expiratory time, mechanics, dead-space changes, and the follow-up blood gas.
VD/VT = dead-space volume ÷ tidal volume

Your guide example: VD 200 mL / VT 500 mL = 0.40.

A rising VD/VT means a larger fraction of each breath is not participating in effective exchange.
Your source also teaches the Bohr relationship, but MVS-02 keeps the focus on ventilator consequences rather than re-teaching the full GX-03 dead-space lesson.
If physiologic dead space increases, total minute ventilation may rise without an equivalent rise in effective alveolar ventilation.
The AHRC 155 guide explicitly states that increased physiologic dead space requires greater total ventilation to maintain the same alveolar ventilation and CO₂ clearance.
RTE move: persistent hypercapnia after a VE change should trigger reassessment of dead space, actual delivered VT, rate/timing, leaks, mechanics, and the underlying clinical change—not automatic repeated increases.
