Humidification Troubleshooting & Complications

Recognize whether the problem is too little humidity, too much condensate, excess resistance/dead space, temperature failure, secretion retention, or an obstructed device—and choose the safest correction.

Socratic troubleshooting string

What is the problem—and what should you fix first?

Start with the patient, then trace the humidification system.

Troubleshooting framework

Patient → water/heat → circuit → device → airway

1. Patient firstAssess airway patency, breath sounds, secretions, work of breathing, oxygenation, and signs of obstruction before focusing on the machine.
2. Humidity sourceCheck water level/supply, heater operation, chamber setup, and whether the chosen humidifier can meet the patient’s demand.
3. CircuitLook for rainout, water traps, heated-wire function, dependent loops, kinks, and condensate migration toward the patient.
4. Patient interface / airwayCheck the HME or artificial airway for saturation, increased resistance, thick secretions, mucus plugging, and need for suction/airway clearance.
Core safety rule

Drain condensate away from the patient. Never shake or pour collected circuit water back toward the patient or humidifier.

Corrective action: drain accumulated water away from the patient and discard it.
A saturated or secretion-loaded HME can markedly increase resistance.
HME also adds apparatus dead space, which matters more when tidal volume is small.
Corrective action: remove/replace the obstructed HME and choose active humidification when HME use is no longer appropriate.
Heater failure can reduce humidification and contribute to hypothermia/airway drying.
Excessive temperature can cause thermal injury; the source lesson also warns of hyperthermia.
Corrective action: verify temperature controls/probes and heater function rather than compensating by blindly changing flow.
The source lesson links pressure-relief activation to downstream obstruction.
Check delivery tubing and the patient interface rather than assuming the humidifier itself is producing excess pressure.
Condensate can become a reservoir for bacterial growth.
Water collected in traps should be discarded—not returned to the circuit or humidifier chamber.
