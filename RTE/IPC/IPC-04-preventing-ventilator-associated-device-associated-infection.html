Preventing Ventilator-Associated & Device-Associated Infection

A ventilator-associated event (VAE) is a sustained worsening after a period of stability; it can have infectious or noninfectious causes. Prevention includes clean hands, oral and airway care, safer positioning when allowed, careful circuit and condensate handling, and daily team review of the need for invasive ventilation. Protect the patient without creating avoidable breaks in the circuit.

Which routine action lowers risk?

Prevention framework

Clean hands → care for airway → protect circuit → review need

Prevent hand-to-device transfer

Clean hands before and after patient contact and before airway or circuit handling, using gloves and other protection for expected secretion exposure. Keep sterile or processed items separate from used ones and follow the correct suction and oral-care methods.

Limit aspiration and oral burden

Provide regular oral care, including toothbrushing as appropriate. Elevate the head of the bed when clinically possible, and manage secretions using the patient's airway device and local protocol. Coordinate with the team when a position or oral-care plan is unsafe.

Protect the circuit

Avoid unnecessary circuit breaks and routine time-based changes. Replace a circuit if visibly soiled, malfunctioning, or required by its manufacturer. Collect condensate without draining it toward the patient, and handle it as contaminated fluid.

Reassess daily

Coordinate daily checks of readiness for less support and liberation, including spontaneous awakening and breathing trials when safe under protocol. A shorter appropriate duration of invasive ventilation can reduce infection and other complications.

Core rule: a VAE is not a diagnosis of pneumonia. When oxygenation worsens after stability, assess the patient, ventilator, and possible causes promptly while continuing the infection-prevention practices.
Practical cue: do not routinely add chlorhexidine oral care simply to prevent pneumonia; follow the current oral-care protocol. The patient's clinical condition and contraindications determine whether head elevation or a breathing trial is safe.
Daily infection-prevention actions

Review each concept

A VAE is identified by a sustained worsening of ventilator oxygenation measures after stability or improvement. Infection is one possible cause; fluid overload, atelectasis, and other problems can also worsen oxygenation.
Hand hygiene is required around patient contact and airway-device handling. Wear gloves and task-appropriate protection for secretions; remove them safely and clean hands afterward.
Use the facility's oral-care process, including toothbrushing when appropriate, to reduce oral buildup. Routine chlorhexidine is not recommended solely for prevention of ventilator-associated pneumonia.
Elevate the head of the bed when not contraindicated to lower aspiration risk. Confirm the patient's condition, feeding plan, skin needs, and procedural restrictions with the team.
Use the ordered airway suction method and device instructions. Avoid unnecessary circuit breaks; manage pooled oral or subglottic secretions using the applicable protocol and prevent contaminated items touching clean surfaces.
Prevent water in the breathing tubing from draining into the airway. Use gloves, direct contaminated fluid to an approved disposal route, and clean hands after handling it.
Do not routinely replace ventilator circuits just because time has passed. Change them when visibly soiled, malfunctioning, or required by the manufacturer or facility policy, while keeping ventilation safe.
Plan care to avoid avoidable circuit disconnections. If an opening is needed, use the approved procedure and verify ventilation, connections, and patient response afterward.
Coordinate with the team on daily sedation, spontaneous awakening trial, and spontaneous breathing trial eligibility as appropriate. Never start a trial when screening shows a contraindication.
Do not assume a new oxygen or pressure need is infection. Assess patient, tubing, secretions, fluid status, lung findings, and other causes; communicate the trend and response promptly.
Integrated RTE-style ventilator case

Handle condensate safely

A stable ventilated patient has water pooling in the circuit near the airway:
  • The tubing remains connected and the circuit works
  • The patient has had a routine circuit change only recently
  • Condensate could drain toward the endotracheal tube if moved carelessly
  • Hand hygiene and approved fluid handling are available
