Bronchoscopy Indications, Preparation & Safety

Bronchoscopy lets a trained provider view and work inside the airways. It may answer a diagnostic question or remove an obstruction, but the scope and sedation can worsen breathing. Choose a clear purpose, check patient readiness, confirm the equipment works, and reassess through recovery.

Is bronchoscopy ready and safe?

Bronchoscopy safety framework

Purpose → readiness → equipment → procedure response

Diagnostic or therapeutic?

Diagnostic bronchoscopy inspects a lesion or obtains samples when the answer will guide care. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) places sterile saline in a selected lung region and collects returned fluid for testing. Therapeutic bronchoscopy may remove a mucus plug or address an airway obstruction.

Assess readiness and risk

Review oxygenation, ventilation, blood pressure, airway support, allergies, consent, planned sampling, and bleeding risk. Severe or uncorrectable low oxygen, unstable circulation, or an unaddressed bleeding risk with biopsy warrants provider reassessment before a nonemergency procedure.

Set up before the scope enters

Confirm the right-size clean bronchoscope, working image and light, tip movement, suction, and specimen supplies. Verify oxygen and ventilator plan, suction, monitoring, emergency airway and resuscitation equipment, and the team’s roles.

Protect the patient during and after

Monitor oxygen saturation, breathing, heart rate, and blood pressure throughout. Sedatives can reduce breathing and airway protection. Pause and escalate with the provider for major desaturation, bleeding, rhythm change, or instability; continue observation during recovery.

Core rule: an indication does not erase procedure risk. Clarify what bronchoscopy will accomplish, prepare respiratory support and rescue equipment, and respond to clinical deterioration rather than simply completing the planned sample.
Ventilator cue: a scope inside an endotracheal tube narrows the space for airflow. Match scope and tube sizes with the proceduralist, monitor ventilation and airway pressure, and be ready to pause if oxygenation or ventilation worsens.
Preparing for a safe procedure

Review each concept

Use a diagnostic procedure to inspect or sample an unexplained finding. A therapeutic procedure treats an airway problem, such as a significant mucus plug that has not cleared with simpler measures.
A persistent focal collapse, suspected obstructing lesion, or unresolved infection may justify provider discussion. Routine bronchoscopy solely to remove ordinary secretions is not a default step.
The provider instills sterile saline into a selected airway segment and retrieves fluid for the ordered laboratory studies. Confirm target site, collection containers, labels, and prompt handling.
Review recent saturation, blood gas if relevant, blood pressure, rhythm, and ventilator settings. Severe low oxygen or instability raises the need to optimize and reassess risk before an elective procedure.
Biopsy adds bleeding and pneumothorax risk beyond simple airway inspection. Review anticoagulants, platelet count, and coagulation concerns with the provider before the planned sampling.
Check the correct-size, properly processed scope, clear image/light, tip steering, and suction channel. For an intubated patient, the scope must leave enough space for ventilation in the airway tube.
Have appropriate oxygen, suction, monitoring, airway equipment, and emergency response available. Establish a ventilation plan with the provider for any scope passed through an endotracheal tube.
Verify the ordered sedation plan, trained personnel, and ongoing oxygenation, ventilation, heart rate, and blood pressure monitoring. Sedation can reduce breathing and airway protection; follow local monitoring policy.
If oxygen saturation falls, airway pressure rises, bleeding occurs, or blood pressure drops, alert the proceduralist and pause while the team supports breathing and addresses the cause.
Track oxygenation, work of breathing, blood pressure, airway protection, and any bleeding. Confirm specimen handling and review any new symptoms or imaging need after biopsy or a complication.

Desaturation during a planned lavage

  • The scope enters the airway tube; airway pressure rises and delivered volume falls
