Documentation, Scope & Professional Accountability

Respiratory care is team care. Confirm the plan and your authority, prepare for an ordered procedure, communicate changes clearly, and record what you observed and did. When the plan is unclear or a patient’s condition changes, protect the patient and contact the responsible team member.

Who needs to know, and what should you record?

Professional decision framework

Verify → coordinate → reassess → document

Verify authority

Check the order or approved protocol, your role under applicable law and facility policy, and the patient’s current status. Clarify conflicting instructions through the responsible clinician; use emergency procedures within the applicable rules.

Prepare, assist, reassess

Before a provider-performed procedure, check the plan, needed equipment, monitoring, and your assigned role. During it, support breathing and communicate changes. Afterward, reassess oxygenation, ventilation, airway, and patient response.

Communicate across the team

Use a clear handoff: current concern, relevant history, assessment, actions, response, and the next step. Repeat back critical instructions and confirm the receiver accepts the handoff. Explain care in plain language and ask patients or caregivers to teach it back.

Document the actual care

Record time, objective findings, intervention, order or protocol basis, patient response, notifications, and follow-up. Correct errors through the approved record process; do not chart care that has not occurred.

Core rule: act within the authorized plan and your role, escalate a mismatch or deterioration, and chart the care and response you actually observed.
Goals-of-care cue: withdrawal of life support follows the patient’s authorized goals of care, a clear team plan and orders, and local policy. Coordinate comfort measures and family communication; never turn a vague request into an independent ventilator-withdrawal decision.
Accountability in team care

Review each concept

Practice depends on applicable law, orders, approved protocols, credentials, and facility policy. If a proposed step exceeds your authority or the plan is unclear, clarify it with the responsible clinician.
Compare a new request with the active order, patient condition, and approved protocol. Confirm any discrepancy before a nonurgent change; address immediate danger through the appropriate emergency response.
For a provider-performed airway procedure, confirm the plan and your role, check oxygen and ventilation equipment, establish a baseline, and prepare monitoring and a rescue plan with the team.
During the procedure, carry out your assigned tasks, monitor the patient, communicate meaningful oxygenation or ventilation changes, and support the team response.
After the procedure, verify airway patency and device function as appropriate, reassess oxygenation, ventilation, vital signs, and patient comfort, and report unexpected findings.
State the current concern, important context, assessment, action, response, and pending needs. Confirm that the receiving team understands and accepts responsibility.
Explain an inhaler or home oxygen plan in plain language, then ask the patient or caregiver to describe or show the steps. Clarify and repeat if the explanation reveals a gap.
Document objective observations, care given, response, notifications, and follow-up at the correct time. Label late entries honestly and use the approved correction process.
Share patient information with the care team through approved channels and only as needed for care. Raise concerns respectfully and promptly when safety or an ethical conflict arises.
For a planned withdrawal of mechanical ventilation, verify the documented goals, authorized orders, comfort plan, team roles, and family communication. Support the patient and reassess comfort throughout according to the plan.

Change after an airway procedure

A provider has performed a planned tracheostomy tube change while you assisted:
  • The new tube is in place but oxygen saturation is falling
  • Breath sounds are reduced and the patient looks distressed
  • A colleague asks you to finish documenting before a reassessment
  • The provider and nursing team remain nearby
