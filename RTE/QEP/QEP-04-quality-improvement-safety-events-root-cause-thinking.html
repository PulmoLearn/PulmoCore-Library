Quality Improvement, Safety Events & Root-Cause Thinking

A safety event calls for prompt care first. After the patient is stable, record what happened, examine the conditions that allowed it, and test a practical change. A near miss deserves attention too: a catch made in time may reveal the same weak step before a patient is harmed.

What happens now, and what changes next?

Safety and improvement framework

Protect the patient → understand the event → change the system

Care comes first

For a disconnected oxygen supply or falling oxygen saturation, assess the patient, restore effective oxygen delivery and ventilation, call for help, and reassess the response. Begin a review after the immediate danger is controlled.

Separate QA, QC, and QI

Quality assurance (QA) checks whether care follows a required standard. Quality control (QC) checks whether an instrument or testing process performs within limits. Quality improvement (QI) tests changes that improve care over time.

Debrief and examine causes

Build a factual timeline, ask what people saw and expected, and look at equipment, handoffs, workload, training, and safeguards. Root-cause analysis examines contributing system factors and actionable ways to prevent recurrence.

Measure the change

For a new oxygen-source handoff check, track completion of the check (process), transport oxygen-delivery failures (outcome), and delays caused by the added step (balancing measure). Adjust the change if results do not improve.

Core rule: stabilize → report → debrief → improve. Preserve the facts, invite team perspectives, and assign a testable system action with follow-up.
Near-miss cue: a potential harm caught before it reaches the patient can reveal a weak process. Report and review it through the local safety process, even when no injury occurred.
Event review and improvement

Review each concept

If oxygen delivery fails during transport, check the patient and equipment at once, restore oxygenation and ventilation, call for assistance, and assess the response. Stabilization takes priority over forms or a debrief.
Once immediate care is underway and the patient is stable, follow the local event-reporting process. Document the timeline, observations, actions, patient response, and notifications without guesses about motives.
A near miss is caught before it reaches the patient. An adverse event may cause harm. Both can expose the same unsafe handoff or equipment step and support learning.
Bring the involved team together after stabilization. Establish what happened, what each person knew, what made the task hard, what went well, and which safeguards failed or worked.
Quality assurance (QA) asks whether performance meets an established requirement. Auditing whether required ventilator checks were recorded is a QA activity.
Quality control (QC) verifies that a test or device process meets specified limits. Checking analyzer controls before accepting a blood-gas result is QC.
Quality improvement (QI) tests a change and studies its effects over time. Trying a transport oxygen-source verification step, then tracking results, is QI.
Root-cause analysis uses a timeline and contributing factors to explain how an event could occur. Check equipment design, handoffs, interruptions, and missing safeguards; design actions that address those conditions.
Process: percent of transport handoffs with a verified oxygen source. Outcome: transport oxygen-delivery failures. Balancing: avoidable transport delays after the new check.
Assign an owner, test the change, review measures over time, share results with staff, and revise the process if it does not work. A recommendation alone has not yet prevented another event.
Integrated RTE-style safety case

Oxygen delivery fails after transport

A ventilated patient arrives from transport with a low-oxygen alarm:
  • Oxygen saturation is falling and breathing support is interrupted
  • The oxygen line is disconnected from its source
  • The transport and receiving teams are at the bedside
  • An event report and team review have not begun
