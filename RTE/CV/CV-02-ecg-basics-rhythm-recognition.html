ECG Basics & Rhythm Recognition

An electrocardiogram (ECG) records the heart’s electrical activity. A bedside rhythm strip helps you follow rate and rhythm; a diagnostic 12-lead ECG gives more views when new chest symptoms or concerning rhythm changes need evaluation. Confirm the patient’s pulse and condition before deciding what the tracing means.

ECG assessment framework

Chart context → clean signal → rhythm pattern → clinical action

Review before the tracing

Note symptoms, vital signs, medications, potassium and other relevant results, prior ECG, and imaging in the chart. A chest image may suggest pulmonary edema, but it does not identify the rhythm or rule out heart ischemia.

Acquire reliable data

Check patient identification, correct lead placement, skin contact, and recording quality. Motion or a loose electrode can imitate a rhythm change. A monitor strip and a diagnostic 12-lead ECG answer different questions.

Read a consistent sequence

Estimate rate; decide if the rhythm is regular; look for a P wave before each QRS complex and the P-to-QRS relationship; then examine PR and QRS duration. A QRS complex 0.12 second or longer is wide.

Connect pattern to the patient

Palpate a pulse and assess blood pressure, mental status, breathing, and chest discomfort. Report new bradycardia, an irregular rhythm, or wide-complex tachycardia. Request an urgent 12-lead ECG for possible acute coronary symptoms without delaying care of an unstable patient.

Core rule: treat the patient, not the monitor label. Check the pulse and perfusion, verify the tracing, and escalate instability while obtaining a diagnostic ECG when indicated.
Waveform cue: the P wave represents atrial activation; the QRS complex represents ventricular activation. The PR interval runs from the start of P to the start of QRS; the QRS width helps sort narrow and wide rhythms.
From tracing to decision

Review each concept

Compare symptoms, baseline ECG, medicines and electrolytes with the current tracing. A chest radiograph can give context for breathlessness, such as congestion, but it cannot establish an electrical rhythm or exclude a cardiac cause.
Confirm identity, place electrodes correctly, ensure good skin contact, and ask the patient to remain still when possible. Correct a loose lead or movement before labeling a pattern as arrhythmia.
Count QRS complexes over a known strip interval or use a validated monitor rate after checking signal quality. Compare the electrical rate with a palpable pulse.
Compare consecutive R-to-R intervals. Regular spacing suggests a regular rhythm; irregularly irregular spacing without distinct P waves may suggest atrial fibrillation.
Identify whether a P wave precedes each QRS in a consistent relationship. A fast or noisy strip may make P waves hard to see; avoid certainty when the tracing is poor.
The PR interval is measured from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS. A changing or prolonged relationship can help identify conduction problems when interpreted with the full rhythm.
A QRS duration of 0.12 second or more is wide. New wide-complex tachycardia needs prompt team review; do not assume the exact rhythm from a single poor-quality lead.
Movement, muscle activity, or poor electrode contact can produce an alarming monitor pattern. Check the patient and palpable pulse while restoring a reliable tracing.
A rate or rhythm becomes urgent when paired with hypotension, altered mental status, chest discomfort, or worsening breathing. Start appropriate team response without waiting for a perfect strip.
A new chest discomfort or concerning rhythm merits a diagnostic 12-lead ECG and timely interpretation. A bedside monitor strip alone is insufficient to evaluate possible acute coronary symptoms.
Integrated RTE-style ECG case

A fast rhythm and new symptoms

An adult with new chest pressure is on a bedside monitor:
  • The monitor shows a fast, apparently regular rhythm
  • Blood pressure is falling and the patient looks pale
  • A prior chest image in the chart shows pulmonary congestion
  • Only a monitor strip is available; no 12-lead ECG has been obtained
