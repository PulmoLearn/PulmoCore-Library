Capnography, Airway & Ventilation During CPR

During cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), provide effective breaths while protecting chest-compression time. A continuous exhaled carbon dioxide waveform helps confirm an endotracheal tube and shows changes in blood flow during compressions. Share meaningful changes clearly with the team and verify that the message was heard.

Are breaths effective without slowing CPR?

CPR airway and waveform framework

Breaths → airway check → waveform → team action

Before an advanced airway

Use bag-mask ventilation with oxygen and a good seal while compressions and breaths are coordinated. For adult CPR without an advanced airway, the usual compression-to-breath ratio is 30:2. Avoid long pauses and excessive ventilation.

After an advanced airway

With an endotracheal tube or other advanced airway in place, give about one breath every 6 seconds while compressions continue. Confirm tube position with continuous waveform capnography plus clinical assessment; avoid inserting a tube if it would cause a long compression interruption.

Interpret exhaled carbon dioxide

End-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO₂) reflects both ventilation and blood flow to the lungs. A low or falling value during CPR prompts a check of compression quality, ventilation, circuit, and airway. An abrupt rise can suggest return of spontaneous circulation but needs a pulse and perfusion check.

Close the communication loop

Use a clear call-out: “ETCO₂ rose sharply; check for a pulse at the next appropriate pause.” A teammate repeats the action, and the sender confirms. Share any loss of waveform immediately while checking the airway and circuit.

Core rule: keep compression pauses short, deliver effective breaths at the airway-appropriate rate, and interpret ETCO₂ with the pulse and patient rather than as a stand-alone answer.
Waveform cue: a sustained exhaled CO₂ waveform supports tracheal tube placement. A lost waveform needs immediate patient, airway, circuit, and perfusion checks; very low flow during arrest can also reduce the signal.
Breaths, waveforms, and team calls

Review each concept

Use oxygen, good mask seal, an open airway, and visible chest rise. In adult CPR without an advanced airway, coordinate 30 compressions and 2 breaths without long pauses.
If an advanced airway attempt would interrupt chest compressions, defer it until an appropriate point or use an effective bag-mask strategy. Early CPR and indicated shocks remain urgent.
With an advanced airway, give one breath about every 6 seconds as compressions continue. Avoid rapid large breaths that raise chest pressure and can impair blood flow.
Use a continuous exhaled carbon dioxide waveform alongside chest rise, bilateral breath sounds, and other clinical findings to confirm and monitor tube placement during CPR.
End-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO₂) is measured at the end of exhalation. During CPR it depends on ventilation, circulation through the lungs, and airway/circuit function; no single number tells the whole story.
Review chest compression quality and check the mask or airway, ventilation rate, tubing, and patient. Alert the team; a low value alone should not decide when resuscitation stops.
An abrupt sustained rise during compressions can signal return of spontaneous circulation. Announce the change and verify pulse and perfusion at an appropriate check.
If the waveform disappears, assess the patient and look for tube dislodgment, obstruction, circuit disconnection, or very low pulmonary blood flow. Act urgently without assuming a single cause.
Address a named team member, state the observation and requested action, listen for their repeat-back, and confirm. This closed-loop pattern reduces missed or misunderstood messages during a code.
After a pulse returns, reassess airway position, breathing, oxygenation, blood pressure, waveform, and patient response. Shift to the post-arrest plan with the team.
Integrated RTE-style CPR case

A sudden capnography rise

An adult in cardiac arrest has an endotracheal tube in place:
  • Compressions continue and breaths are delivered every 6 seconds
  • A sustained exhaled CO₂ waveform confirms ongoing ventilation
  • ETCO₂ abruptly rises from 12 to 36 mm Hg
  • The team leader is directing the next rhythm and pulse check
