Cardiovascular Assessment for the Respiratory Therapist

Breathlessness can reflect a heart or circulation problem as well as a lung problem. Check blood pressure, pulse quality, skin perfusion, neck veins, swelling, work of breathing, and breath sounds together. Compare findings with the patient’s baseline and act promptly when perfusion or breathing worsens.

What do the circulation findings add?

Bedside cardiopulmonary framework

Inspect → palpate → auscultate → compare the trend

Check circulation

Measure blood pressure and heart rate, then palpate pulse strength and rhythm and feel skin temperature. A new weak pulse, cool or clammy skin, or altered mental status alongside low blood pressure raises concern for poor perfusion.

Look for congestion

Ask about breathlessness when lying flat (orthopnea), inspect neck veins, and check for dependent swelling. These findings can suggest fluid congestion but have other causes; interpret them with the full exam and trend.

Listen to heart and lungs

Compare breath sounds on both sides. New crackles can occur with pulmonary fluid but are not specific. A new extra heart sound can add context; irregular or unexpected findings require assessment with the team.

Match the response to urgency

New chest discomfort, severe dyspnea, falling oxygenation, hypotension, or worsening perfusion calls for rapid team assessment. Support breathing as indicated, give a concise report of findings and changes, and prepare for appropriate diagnostics.

Core rule: combine pulse and perfusion findings with breath sounds, breathing effort, and symptoms. New hypotension with poor perfusion or new chest discomfort needs prompt escalation.
Assessment cue: neck-vein distention is observed, not felt. Edema can be checked by gently pressing over a dependent area; record the location and whether an indentation remains.
Palpation and auscultation

Review each concept

Check rate, rhythm, and strength at an appropriate pulse site. A newly weak or irregular pulse can change the urgency of a respiratory assessment. Compare central and peripheral findings when relevant.
Cool, pale, clammy skin can support concern for reduced peripheral perfusion when paired with hypotension or mental-status change. Skin temperature or color alone cannot identify the cause.
Repeat a surprising blood pressure with proper technique. New low pressure plus a weak pulse or altered mentation suggests inadequate circulation and warrants immediate team assessment.
Inspect and gently press over dependent areas, often the lower legs in an ambulatory patient or the sacrum in a bedbound patient. Note location, symmetry, and change from baseline.
Distended neck veins can support a finding of increased venous pressure, especially alongside edema or dyspnea. Patient position matters, and a single visual finding does not establish heart failure.
Orthopnea is breathlessness that worsens when lying flat and improves when upright. Ask about any new pillows needed to sleep and compare with the usual pattern.
Listen to both sides and compare upper and lower regions. New bilateral crackles with breathlessness may point toward fluid in the lungs, but infection and other conditions can also produce crackles.
Note rate, regularity, and any new extra sound when the setting allows. A third heart sound can occur with volume overload, but interpretation requires context and may be difficult in a noisy setting.
Ask where, when, and how discomfort began and whether dyspnea, sweating, or faintness accompanies it. Notify the team promptly and support breathing while cardiac evaluation proceeds.
State symptoms, blood pressure and trend, palpated pulse and skin findings, lung and heart sounds, oxygenation, actions, and patient response. Say which findings are new or worsening.
Integrated RTE-style assessment case

Breathlessness with poor perfusion

An adult reports new chest pressure and worsening breathlessness:
  • Blood pressure has fallen from the prior reading
  • Peripheral pulse is weak and the skin is cool and clammy
  • New bilateral crackles are heard at the lung bases
  • The patient is more restless and needs additional breathing support
