Tachyarrhythmias: Stable vs Unstable Decisions

A fast electrocardiogram (ECG) rhythm matters most when it harms perfusion. Confirm that the patient has a pulse, assess blood pressure, mental status, chest discomfort, and breathing, then describe whether the rhythm is narrow or wide and regular or irregular. An unstable tachyarrhythmia needs an urgent team response; a stable patient allows more time to evaluate the rhythm and treatment options.

Tachyarrhythmia decision framework

Pulse → perfusion → rhythm → treatment support

Confirm pulse and pattern

First assess the patient and palpable pulse. On the ECG, identify rate, regularity, and QRS width. A QRS complex at least 0.12 second wide is a wide-complex rhythm; do not assume every wide complex has the same cause.

Identify instability

Hypotension, acute mental-status change, shock, ischemic chest discomfort, or acute heart failure may signal an unstable tachyarrhythmia when caused by the fast rhythm. Call for urgent help while supporting airway, breathing, and oxygenation as needed.

Understand the two paths

For a stable regular narrow-complex rhythm, the team may use vagal maneuvers or adenosine. Stable wide-complex rhythms require careful evaluation and expert-directed treatment. When the rhythm causes instability, prepare for prompt synchronized cardioversion when appropriate.

Assist and reassess

For provider-performed cardioversion, help confirm the rhythm and pulse, position pads, ensure the device is set to synchronize to QRS complexes, support sedation when feasible, and maintain electrical and oxygen safety. Afterward, check rhythm, pulse, pressure, breathing, and comfort.

Core rule: a fast rate alone does not decide treatment. A patient with poor perfusion from the tachyarrhythmia needs an urgent team response; no pulse means follow the cardiac-arrest pathway instead.
Shock cue: synchronized cardioversion times a shock to the QRS. Pulseless ventricular tachycardia and sustained polymorphic ventricular tachycardia are not treated with a synchronized shock; follow the applicable emergency algorithm.
Rhythm and patient response

Review each concept

Palpate the pulse and assess the patient, not only the monitor. A patient without a pulse enters the cardiac-arrest response; a patient with a pulse is assessed for tachyarrhythmia-related instability.
Estimate rate, compare R-to-R spacing, and check QRS width. Narrow versus wide and regular versus irregular patterns help the team choose a pathway.
Hypotension, acute altered mentation, shock signs, ischemic chest discomfort, or acute heart failure are danger signs when due to the fast rhythm. Escalate and support breathing immediately.
If perfusion is preserved, obtain a 12-lead ECG when available, monitor the patient, and allow the team to evaluate a cause and treatment. Stable is a current assessment, not a guarantee that the rhythm will stay safe.
For a stable regular narrow-complex tachycardia, vagal maneuvers or adenosine may be used under the team’s protocol. Reassess rhythm and blood pressure; avoid assuming every fast rhythm is suitable for adenosine.
A wide QRS is at least 0.12 second. A stable regular monomorphic wide-complex rhythm needs expert-directed evaluation; an unstable wide-complex rhythm with a pulse may need urgent synchronized cardioversion.
A synchronized shock is timed to the QRS to avoid delivering energy during the vulnerable repolarization period. Confirm visible synchronization indicators on the displayed complexes before a planned synchronized shock.
Help the provider verify the patient, pulse, rhythm, pads, monitoring, equipment, and team roles. Support airway, breathing, oxygen safety, and sedation when feasible without delaying urgent treatment.
If no pulse is present, activate the arrest pathway. Sustained polymorphic ventricular tachycardia cannot be reliably synchronized and needs the defibrillation pathway, not routine synchronized cardioversion.
After medication or cardioversion, assess pulse, rhythm, blood pressure, oxygenation, breathing, and symptoms. Report recurrent instability and confirm synchronization again if another synchronized shock is planned.
Integrated RTE-style tachyarrhythmia case

Fast rhythm with falling pressure

An adult has a regular wide-complex rhythm at 170/min and a palpable pulse:
  • Blood pressure falls and the patient becomes confused
  • The patient reports new chest discomfort
  • Breathing is labored and oxygenation is falling
  • The provider and resuscitation equipment are available
