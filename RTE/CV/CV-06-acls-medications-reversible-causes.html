ACLS Medications & Reversible Causes

Advanced cardiovascular life support (ACLS) medications support a cardiac-arrest response; they do not replace cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), defibrillation when indicated, or treatment of a reversible cause. Know how epinephrine timing differs by rhythm and how respiratory findings can point to hypoxia, tension pneumothorax, or pulmonary embolism.

Arrest medication and cause framework

Protect the basic response

Start high-quality CPR, provide oxygen and effective bag-mask ventilation, and attach the defibrillator. Shock ventricular fibrillation (VF) or pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT) promptly; do not shock pulseless electrical activity (PEA) or asystole.

Time epinephrine by rhythm

For PEA or asystole, give epinephrine as soon as possible during CPR. For VF/pVT, prioritize early shocks and give epinephrine after initial defibrillation attempts per the algorithm. The adult dose is 1 mg through intravenous or intraosseous access every 3–5 minutes.

Use antiarrhythmics selectively

Amiodarone or lidocaine may be considered when VF/pVT persists despite defibrillation. These are not routine treatments for PEA or asystole. The current adult algorithm lists amiodarone 300 mg first and 150 mg second; use the team’s protocol for preparation and administration.

Find a reversible cause

Look for hypoxia, severe volume loss, abnormal potassium, acidosis, hypothermia, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins, or a pulmonary or coronary clot. In respiratory care, immediately check oxygen supply, airway position, ventilation effectiveness, and clues to chest pressure or embolism.

Core rule: do CPR and ventilation now, shock only shockable rhythms, give the medication at the right point, and correct a cause without delaying the basic response.
Respiratory cue: a displaced airway or failed oxygen supply can cause hypoxia. Check effective ventilation during CPR and report sudden unilateral findings or signs of a possible pulmonary clot so the team can address them.
Medication timing and respiratory clues

Review each concept

Begin compressions and give oxygen and effective breaths. Medications support but do not replace early CPR and timely defibrillation of VF or pVT.
For a nonshockable arrest, epinephrine should be given as soon as possible while CPR continues. Recheck rhythm at planned intervals and search for reversible causes.
For shockable arrest, provide initial defibrillation and CPR first. The algorithm places epinephrine after the second shock, then every 3–5 minutes while the arrest response continues.
The current adult cardiac-arrest dose is 1 mg intravenously or intraosseously every 3–5 minutes. Use closed-loop medication communication and record each dose and time.
When VF or pVT persists despite shocks, an antiarrhythmic may be considered. The adult algorithm lists amiodarone 300 mg first, then 150 mg; lidocaine is an alternative, not a routine PEA drug.
Check oxygen source, airway patency and position, mask seal or tube connection, chest rise, and ventilation. Restore effective oxygenation and ventilation while compressions continue.
Sudden severe resistance to ventilation, unequal chest findings, and collapse after trauma or positive-pressure ventilation can raise suspicion. Alert the team for urgent assessment and decompression by an authorized clinician.
A large pulmonary embolism can cause abrupt collapse and PEA. Share risk factors and sudden dyspnea or chest findings with the team; cause-directed treatment requires team assessment.
Look for severe hypovolemia, abnormal potassium, acidosis, hypothermia, tamponade, toxins, and coronary thrombosis. Give the team the patient history and signs that support a specific cause.
Keep CPR interruptions brief. Reassess rhythm on schedule, and check pulse and perfusion when an organized rhythm appears. After return of spontaneous circulation, shift to post-arrest airway and oxygenation care.
Integrated RTE-style arrest case

PEA after ventilation stops

A ventilated adult becomes unresponsive and pulseless:
  • CPR has begun and the monitor shows organized electrical activity
  • The endotracheal tube circuit is disconnected from oxygen delivery
  • No shockable rhythm is identified
  • The team is establishing medication access while you assess ventilation
