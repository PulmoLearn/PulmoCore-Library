Bradycardia, Heart Block & Pacing

A slow heart rate is dangerous when it fails to perfuse the patient. Compare the electrocardiogram (ECG) with the pulse, blood pressure, mental status, chest symptoms, and breathing. Recognize when atrioventricular (AV) conduction is failing and prepare to support the team’s treatment, including transcutaneous pacing when needed.

Is the slow rhythm perfusing the patient?

Bradycardia assessment framework

Pulse and perfusion → P–QRS relationship → response

Rate is not the whole story

Bradycardia often means a heart rate below 60/min, but the emergency pathway commonly focuses on rates below 50/min with poor perfusion. Compare with baseline; a slow rate without symptoms calls for observation and cause assessment, not automatic pacing.

Read the conduction pattern

A P wave should lead to a QRS in normal AV conduction. Progressive PR lengthening before a dropped QRS suggests second-degree type I block. Intermittent dropped QRS complexes with stable PR intervals suggest type II. Independent P and QRS rhythms suggest complete block.

Respond to poor perfusion

New hypotension, confusion, shock, ischemic discomfort, or acute heart failure with a slow pulse calls for urgent support. Check airway and breathing, monitor pulse and rhythm, and seek causes such as low oxygen, ischemia, medicines, or electrolyte abnormalities.

Know treatment and pacing checks

The team may use atropine for symptomatic bradycardia. If ineffective, transcutaneous pacing or an epinephrine/dopamine infusion may be used under the current algorithm. With pacing, confirm both ECG capture and a corresponding pulse or improved perfusion.

Core rule: treat symptomatic poor perfusion, not an isolated monitor number. If pacing is started, spikes and QRS complexes alone do not prove that the patient is perfusing.
Block cue: type I: PR lengthens before a dropped QRS. Type II: dropped QRS without progressive PR lengthening. Complete block: P waves and QRS complexes beat independently.
Slow-rhythm patterns and response

Review each concept

Check the patient and palpate a pulse. Compare palpable pulse rate with the monitor and check for poor perfusion; artifact or nonconducted beats can make the displayed number misleading.
Hypotension, new confusion, shock, ischemic chest discomfort, or acute heart failure with bradycardia warrants urgent team response. A fit patient with a stable low resting rate may not need acute treatment.
Consider hypoxemia, myocardial ischemia, medications, and electrolyte problems in context. Treat reversible causes with the team while supporting airway and breathing.
Every P wave conducts to a QRS, but the PR interval is longer than 0.20 second. This alone usually does not explain severe instability; assess the patient and other causes.
The PR interval becomes progressively longer until one P wave is not followed by a QRS. Describe the pattern and assess symptoms; do not call every dropped beat type II.
Some P waves are not followed by QRS complexes, without progressive PR lengthening before the drop. This higher-risk pattern calls for prompt evaluation and pacing readiness.
P waves and QRS complexes occur independently because atrial impulses do not conduct to the ventricles. A slow escape rhythm may not supply enough blood flow; escalate promptly.
Atropine is an initial treatment option for symptomatic bradycardia in the adult algorithm. Do not delay pacing readiness if it fails or a high-grade block with poor perfusion persists.
Apply pads and assist the team as directed. Electrical capture means a pacing spike is followed by a broad QRS; mechanical capture requires a corresponding pulse or improved circulation.
Repeat pulse, pressure, mental status, breathing, rhythm, and cause assessment. Pacing artifacts can obscure the ECG, so use independent perfusion findings to evaluate the result.
Integrated RTE-style bradycardia case

Slow pulse with confusion

An adult becomes confused and pale with a palpable pulse near 36/min:
  • Blood pressure is 78/44 mm Hg
  • ECG shows P waves and QRS complexes with no consistent relationship
  • Oxygenation and breathing are being assessed
  • The team is preparing atropine and pacing equipment
