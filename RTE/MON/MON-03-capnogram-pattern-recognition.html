Capnogram Pattern Recognition

Read the shape and the trend before reacting to a single ETCO₂ number. A slanted “shark-fin” pattern points toward obstructive emptying, an elevated baseline suggests rebreathing, a plateau notch suggests spontaneous inspiratory effort, loss of the plateau suggests leak, and abrupt loss of exhaled CO₂ demands immediate patient-and-system assessment.

Socratic pattern string

Match the capnogram clue to the physiology

Pattern-recognition framework

Start with morphology, then ask what changed physiologically

Shark-fin

Slanted expiratory upstroke and rising/angled alveolar plateau = delayed, uneven exhalation from airway obstruction such as asthma, COPD, or mucus plugging.

Baseline stays above zero

Inspired CO₂ is present when it should not be. This suggests rebreathing rather than normal washout to a zero baseline.

Notch in plateau

An inspiratory effort occurring during mechanical ventilation can pull the waveform downward, producing the classic “curare cleft” appearance.

Loss of plateau / downward slope

Your source associates this with a cuff or circuit leak. Compare with airway/circuit findings rather than interpreting shape alone.

Abrupt change matters.

Sudden loss to near zero after a previously normal waveform can indicate apnea, arrest/profound perfusion loss, disconnection, tube displacement, severe obstruction, or a sampling-system problem.

Scope boundary: MON-03 teaches recognition of airway-placement and CPR-related capnography patterns because the registry requires them. MON-04 will own the full decision framework for airway confirmation, CPR quality/ROSC, and abrupt deterioration.
Common abnormal capnograms

Review each pattern

Your PulmoLearn source explicitly identifies a slanted shark-fin upstroke with airway obstruction such as asthma, COPD, or mucus plugging. The breakout guide explains that slow, uneven emptying changes both the expiratory upstroke and alveolar plateau. Improvement should move the shape toward a more normal rectangle while bedside wheeze, air movement, work of breathing, RR, and ETCO₂ trend also improve.
The inspiratory baseline should return near zero. If it remains elevated, the patient is inspiring CO₂-containing gas. Interpret this as a rebreathing pattern and then evaluate the breathing system, exhalation pathway, fresh gas flow, and equipment as appropriate to the clinical setup.
Your PulmoLearn waveform table identifies a notch in the alveolar plateau as inspiratory effort, often called a curare cleft. In a mechanically ventilated patient, it may indicate spontaneous respiratory effort occurring during the ventilator-delivered breath.
Your PulmoLearn waveform table associates loss of the plateau with a downward slope with a cuff or circuit leak. Correlate the waveform with delivered/exhaled volumes, cuff/circuit assessment, airway position, and patient condition.
Your AHRC guide states that elevated or rising ETCO₂ with bradypnea commonly indicates hypoventilation and CO₂ retention. Consider sedation/opioid effect, airway obstruction, neuromuscular weakness, or reduced minute ventilation.
A falling ETCO₂ may accompany hyperventilation, but reduced cardiac output, pulmonary embolism/increased dead space, leak, disconnection, or sampling error can also lower the value. Use RR, waveform, perfusion, and the whole clinical picture.
Your faculty guide lists tube displacement, circuit disconnection, apnea, severe obstruction, cardiac arrest/profound perfusion loss, and sampling-line/monitor failure. A vanished waveform after previously normal capnography is an urgent change until proven otherwise.
Persistent absence of exhaled CO₂ immediately after intubation strongly suggests esophageal placement or a disconnected sampling system. Rare profound low-perfusion states can also markedly reduce CO₂ delivery. MON-04 will practice the full airway-confirmation response.
During cardiac arrest, very low ETCO₂ may reflect poor pulmonary blood flow during compressions. Your PulmoLearn activity teaches that a sudden sustained rise—for example from 5 to 30 mmHg—may be an early clue to return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). MON-04 will handle the full CPR interpretation and response framework.
A waveform becoming progressively more shark-fin shaped, ETCO₂ steadily rising with a falling RR, or an abrupt disappearance after a stable tracing carries more information than one isolated number. Describe the direction, rate, associated waveform change, and bedside findings.
Capnography is excellent for continuous trend and waveform interpretation. If the exact arterial CO₂ and acid-base status are needed—or if the waveform/value and clinical picture disagree—recommend an ABG rather than forcing a capnogram to answer a question it cannot.
Integrated RTE-style pattern case

One number, three clues

Mechanically ventilated patient with worsening wheeze:
  • ETCO₂ rises from 39 to 51 mmHg
  • Respiratory rate is unchanged
  • Expiratory upstroke becomes slanted
  • Alveolar plateau becomes steeply angled
  • Air movement is reduced and expiration is prolonged
