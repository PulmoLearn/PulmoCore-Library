Capnography Fundamentals & Normal Waveform

Capnography continuously displays exhaled carbon dioxide over time. Read the waveform before naming a problem: identify the inspiratory baseline, expiratory upstroke, alveolar plateau, end-tidal point, and inspiratory downstroke. Then interpret PetCO₂ in the context of ventilation, perfusion, dead space, respiratory rate, and the PaCO₂–PetCO₂ relationship.

Can you read a normal capnogram before diagnosing an abnormal one?

Normal capnogram framework

Read the waveform from inspiration through expiration

Inspiratory baseline

During inspiration, exhaled CO₂ should return near zero because fresh inspired gas contains little or no CO₂.

Expiratory upstroke

As exhalation begins, dead-space gas is followed by progressively more CO₂-rich gas from the lungs, producing the rapid rising limb.

Alveolar plateau

Later expiration is dominated by alveolar gas, creating the plateau. The shape and slope provide information about how evenly the lungs empty.

End-tidal point + inspiratory downstroke

PetCO₂ is measured at the end of expiration. Inspiration then rapidly washes CO₂ back toward the baseline.

Typical adult PetCO₂: approximately 35–45 mmHg.

Your PulmoLearn source emphasizes that the number must be interpreted with the waveform, respiratory rate, perfusion, dead space, and relationship to PaCO₂.

Physiology behind the waveform

Review each topic

At the beginning of exhalation, gas from the conducting airways reaches the sensor first. Because this anatomic dead-space gas has not participated in alveolar gas exchange, its CO₂ is low and the tracing remains near baseline.
As exhalation continues, dead-space gas mixes with progressively more alveolar gas. CO₂ rises quickly, forming the expiratory upstroke. This transition is why the waveform rises rather than jumping instantly to the plateau.
During the alveolar plateau, exhaled gas is predominantly alveolar. The plateau normally has a slight upward slope because different lung units empty at slightly different times and with slightly different CO₂ concentrations.
PetCO₂ (often written ETCO₂ clinically) is measured at the end of the expiratory plateau. It is a noninvasive indicator of exhaled CO₂ and ventilatory status, but it is not identical to arterial PaCO₂.
Your existing PulmoLearn material teaches that PetCO₂ usually reads lower than PaCO₂ because dead-space gas dilutes exhaled CO₂. One source gives a usual PaCO₂–PetCO₂ difference of about 2–5 mmHg. The gradient can widen when dead space or perfusion abnormalities increase.
Exhaled CO₂ reaches the detector only if CO₂ is produced, transported to the lungs by blood flow, and ventilated out. Reduced pulmonary perfusion—such as low cardiac output or pulmonary embolism—can lower PetCO₂ even if ventilation has not increased.
Your source lists hyperventilation, reduced cardiac output, pulmonary embolism, increased dead space, leak, disconnection, and sampling error as causes of low PetCO₂. Use respiratory rate, waveform quality, perfusion, and the patient’s condition before naming the cause.
Elevated or rising PetCO₂ with bradypnea commonly supports hypoventilation and CO₂ retention. Consider sedation/opioid effect, airway obstruction, neuromuscular weakness, or reduced minute ventilation while assessing the patient.
A steadily rising PetCO₂, falling respiratory rate, changing waveform, or sudden loss of exhaled CO₂ may be more clinically important than whether one value lies within the usual reference range.
If the capnogram and clinical picture disagree—or if the exact arterial CO₂/pH is needed—recommend an ABG. If the concern is exhaled-gas trend/ventilation over time, capnography provides continuous noninvasive information.
A low or absent value may reflect patient physiology or a sampling/device problem. Before interpreting a sudden change, verify the sampling line, airway/circuit connection, cannula or adapter position, moisture/occlusion, and whether the waveform is being sampled correctly.
