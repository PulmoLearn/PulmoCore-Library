Capnography for Airway Confirmation, CPR & Deterioration

Waveform capnography is most useful when the situation changes quickly. Use sustained exhaled CO₂ to support tracheal airway placement, use ETCO₂ trends during CPR to judge perfusion/compression effectiveness and recognize possible ROSC, and treat abrupt waveform loss or major trend changes as urgent clues that require immediate patient-and-system assessment.

Socratic decision string

What does the capnogram mean right now?

High-stakes capnography framework

Confirm, trend, and respond

Airway placement

Your PulmoLearn Foundations source teaches that detection of exhaled CO₂ supports tracheal tube placement and that waveform capnography is preferred as soon as available because it shows both the value and sustained waveform trend.

CPR quality

During cardiac arrest, ETCO₂ reflects CO₂ delivery to the lungs by blood flow. Persistently very low values may indicate poor perfusion from ineffective compressions.

Possible ROSC

A sudden sustained ETCO₂ rise during CPR can be one of the earliest clues that circulation has returned. Confirm with the patient/resuscitation assessment rather than treating the monitor as the only evidence.

Abrupt deterioration

Loss or major change in a previously stable waveform may represent apnea, tube displacement, circuit disconnection, severe obstruction, profound perfusion loss/arrest, or sampling-system failure.

Capnography is strongest as a continuous trend.

One number matters less than whether exhaled CO₂ is present, sustained, changing, or abruptly disappearing in the clinical context.

Source boundary: your source gives approximate CPR teaching ranges (“less than about 10–15 mmHg” in Foundations and “less than 10–20 mmHg” in the review activity) rather than one single universal cutoff. This lesson therefore emphasizes the source-supported principle: persistently very low ETCO₂ during CPR suggests poor perfusion/compression effectiveness, and the trend should guide reassessment.
Critical interpretation decisions

Review each topic

Your PulmoLearn source says a colorimetric CO₂ detector can quickly support tracheal placement when it changes from purple to yellow, but waveform capnography is preferred as soon as available because it provides continuous exhaled CO₂ and waveform trends. A persistent, repeating exhaled CO₂ waveform is stronger evidence than one isolated color change.
If exhaled CO₂ is persistently absent immediately after intubation, assess for esophageal placement, disconnected sampling/circuit, severe obstruction, or profound low pulmonary blood flow. Do not accept tube placement based solely on chest movement or condensation when capnography is inconsistent.
Your Foundations lesson states that very low ETCO₂ can suggest poor perfusion from ineffective compressions. Its example uses less than about 10–15 mmHg; your separate review activity uses less than 10–20 mmHg. The consistent concept is that very low values during ongoing CPR should prompt reassessment of compression quality and the overall resuscitation.
Your review activity specifically ties very low ETCO₂ to improving compression depth/recoil and rotating compressors about every 2 minutes when possible because fatigue can reduce quality. Use ETCO₂ as one real-time perfusion clue alongside the full CPR assessment.
Your PulmoLearn activity uses a rise from 5 to 30 mmHg as the classic example. This does not mean “stop everything because the monitor says ROSC”; it means rapidly assess for return of circulation according to the resuscitation process.
Treat abrupt loss as urgent until proven otherwise. Assess the patient, check the airway and tube position, inspect the circuit/sampling line, verify ventilation, and consider profound perfusion loss or cardiac arrest if the system remains intact.
A sharp ETCO₂ decline can reflect reduced pulmonary perfusion, sudden increase in dead space, major leak, partial disconnection, or sampling problems. Pair the trend with blood pressure/pulse, breath delivery, waveform shape, oxygenation, and the patient's condition.
A steadily rising ETCO₂ can indicate worsening hypoventilation when respiratory rate/minute ventilation is falling. It can also rise when perfusion improves. The clinical context decides which mechanism fits.
A value that is “low” can mean hyperventilation, poor perfusion, increased dead space, leak, disconnection, or sampling error. A “normal” value does not guarantee a stable airway if the waveform is disappearing intermittently. Interpret presence, morphology, trend, and patient findings together.
For high-stakes changes, state what changed, how fast, the current waveform/value, the patient's ventilation/perfusion status, airway/circuit findings, and the action being taken. Example: “ETCO₂ abruptly fell from 36 to near zero with waveform loss; patient is not receiving effective breaths; checking tube and circuit now.”
Integrated RTE-style case

Use capnography as a continuous alarm for physiology

During resuscitation:
  • Patient is intubated
  • Waveform capnography initially shows ETCO₂ 8–10 mmHg during compressions
  • Compression quality is improved and compressors are rotated
  • ETCO₂ then abruptly rises to 31 mmHg and remains elevated
  • A perfusing rhythm appears on the monitor
