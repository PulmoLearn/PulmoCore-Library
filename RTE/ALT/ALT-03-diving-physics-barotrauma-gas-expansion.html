Diving Physics, Barotrauma & Gas Expansion

Water pressure rises during descent and falls during ascent. Boyle's law explains the result: gas shrinks as surrounding pressure rises and expands as it falls. Air spaces that cannot equalize may be injured. A diver with chest pain or breathing difficulty after a rapid ascent needs immediate oxygen, emergency assessment, and evaluation for a lung overexpansion injury.

What does pressure do to gas?

Diving pressure framework

Pressure change → gas change → injury → action

Apply Boyle's law

For a fixed amount of gas at a steady temperature, pressure and volume move in opposite directions. At about 10 m (33 ft) of seawater, pressure is roughly twice surface pressure, so an enclosed gas volume is about half as large. Returning to the surface lets it double again if gas cannot escape.

Recognize descent injury

As pressure rises, gas in the middle ear or sinuses compresses. Failure to equalize can cause ear fullness, pain, bleeding, or sinus pain. Stop descent, move slightly shallower if needed, and equalize gently; never force a painful descent.

Recognize ascent injury

As pressure falls, trapped gas expands. Breath-holding or blocked small airways can overexpand the lungs, causing chest pain, shortness of breath, air under the skin, pneumothorax (air outside a lung), or gas entering an artery. Even a shallow ascent can cause harm.

Prioritize emergency response

Bring a symptomatic diver to safety, activate emergency medical help, give high-concentration oxygen when available, and assess breathing, pulse, and neurologic function. Sudden one-sided breath sounds with hypotension after ascent suggests a possible tension pneumothorax and needs immediate trained intervention.

Core emergency rule: a diver with chest pain, breathing trouble, or sudden neurologic change after ascent needs urgent assessment and oxygen, even if symptoms initially improve.
Physics reminder: pressure changes are proportionally greatest near the surface. A breath held during ascent from shallow water can still overexpand the lungs.
Pressure, trapped gas & emergency clues

Review each concept

Ambient pressure rises as the diver goes deeper. Around 10 m (33 ft) in seawater adds about one atmosphere to the atmosphere already present at the surface, for roughly two atmospheres total.
At constant temperature, pressure times gas volume stays approximately constant for a fixed gas amount. Doubling pressure halves the volume; halving pressure doubles it if the gas remains trapped.
From about 10 m to the surface, surrounding pressure falls from roughly two atmospheres to one. A trapped 1 L pocket at 10 m would tend toward 2 L at the surface. Lung injury can happen in shallow water.
During descent, external pressure rises while middle-ear gas compresses. If equalization fails, ear pain and fullness increase; continuing down can damage tissues or the eardrum.
Blocked sinus passages can trap gas. Descent may pull on sinus tissues and cause facial pain or bleeding; ascent can cause a reverse block as trapped gas expands.
During ascent, expanding gas must leave the lungs. Breath-holding or airway obstruction can trap it, stretch or tear alveoli, and release air into the chest or circulation.
Chest pain and shortness of breath after an ascent raise concern for lung barotrauma, including pneumothorax. Marked distress, falling blood pressure, and reduced breath sounds on one side require immediate emergency evaluation.
A tear in the lung can allow gas into the arterial circulation. Sudden confusion, weakness, seizure, or collapse after surfacing is a diving emergency. Prioritize oxygen and urgent referral; the next lesson examines this diagnosis in detail.
Once the diver is safely out of the water, call emergency services, provide high-concentration oxygen when available, and support breathing if needed. Do not wait for imaging before responding to severe breathing or circulatory compromise.
Record depth, time, ascent rate, any breath-holding, symptoms and onset, vital signs, oxygen given, and changes. This history helps the receiving team evaluate lung injury and other dive-related emergencies.

Respond to possible trapped gas

Scuba diver after a rapid ascent while holding a breath:
  • Sudden right-sided chest pain and severe shortness of breath
  • Very reduced breath sounds on the right
  • Blood pressure 82/48 mm Hg and increasing confusion
  • Oxygen is available at the dock
