Altitude Physiology & Hypoxemia

As altitude rises, air pressure falls. Oxygen remains about 21% of the air, but each breath brings a lower oxygen pressure, so less oxygen moves into blood. Faster breathing helps at first; acclimatization improves ventilation over days. Reassess oxygenation, symptoms, sleep, and activity to decide when to add oxygen, slow ascent, or arrange evaluation.

How should the plan change?

Altitude and oxygen framework

Pressure → oxygenation → response → plan

Explain the first change

The fraction of oxygen in air stays close to 21%, but lower barometric pressure means lower inspired oxygen partial pressure (PIO₂). Less oxygen pressure reaches the alveoli, and arterial oxygen pressure and saturation can fall, especially after rapid ascent.

Recognize early compensation

Low oxygen stimulates faster, deeper breathing. Increased ventilation can improve alveolar oxygen while lowering carbon dioxide and causing an early respiratory alkalosis. Over the first days, the kidneys remove more bicarbonate, making it easier to sustain ventilation.

Reassess individual needs

Compare oxygen saturation, symptoms, breathing effort, and activity with the patient's baseline and the expected range at the current altitude. Sleep may bring greater hypoxemia. People with chronic lung or heart disease may need assessment and supplemental oxygen sooner.

Change the care plan

If clinically important hypoxemia or symptoms persist, provide supplemental oxygen as ordered and reassess its effect. Limit exertion, pause further ascent, and arrange evaluation when needed. Descent raises inspired oxygen pressure if oxygenation is worsening or support is not enough.

Core care-plan rule: treat the patient and the trend. Recheck oxygenation and symptoms after oxygen or a change in altitude, then revise the plan if the response is inadequate.
Altitude distinction: a lower saturation may be expected at elevation, but severe or progressive symptoms need assessment. A single number cannot distinguish normal acclimatization from illness or worsening cardiopulmonary disease.
Oxygen pressure & care-plan changes

Review each concept

Ambient air remains roughly 21% oxygen at high altitude. The pressure of the air decreases, so oxygen's share of the total pressure also decreases.
Lower barometric pressure reduces inspired oxygen pressure. Less pressure driving oxygen into the blood helps explain why arterial oxygen pressure and saturation can fall after ascent.
Low oxygen stimulates an increase in ventilation. This brings more air to the alveoli and can partly improve oxygenation, but it also removes more carbon dioxide.
Faster breathing lowers arterial carbon dioxide, producing respiratory alkalosis at first. Over days, renal bicarbonate loss partly compensates and helps the higher ventilation continue.
Breathing and oxygenation often improve over the first several days at altitude. Gradual ascent allows more time for this response; going higher before adapting increases risk of altitude-related problems.
Oxygenation can worsen during sleep and exertion compared with a quiet daytime reading. Reassess the setting where symptoms occur rather than relying on one resting measurement.
Preexisting lung disease, heart disease, or low baseline oxygenation can reduce reserve at altitude. A person who is well at sea level may need a planned assessment and oxygen support at elevation.
Supplemental oxygen raises the oxygen pressure the patient breathes and can improve saturation and symptoms. Choose the delivery method and target based on the patient and clinical setting, then check the response.
Avoid further ascent when hypoxemia or symptoms are not improving. Descending increases ambient and inspired oxygen pressure; arrange evaluation when symptoms persist, progress, or suggest a serious illness.
Record altitude, baseline and follow-up oxygenation, oxygen device and flow, symptoms at rest and activity, and response to changes. Escalate when oxygen support is insufficient or the patient deteriorates.
Integrated RTE-style care-plan case

Reassess and recommend a change

Adult with chronic lung disease arrives rapidly at a mountain town:
  • At home, resting oxygen saturation was usually 94%
  • At 3,000 m, saturation is 85% on room air with new breathlessness
  • Ordered oxygen raises saturation to 92% and eases breathing
  • The patient plans to go still higher that afternoon
