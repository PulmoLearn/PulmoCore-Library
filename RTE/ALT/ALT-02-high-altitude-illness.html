High-Altitude Illness

After rapid ascent, headache with nausea or fatigue suggests acute mountain sickness (AMS). Worsening cough and breathlessness may signal high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE), while confusion or trouble walking suggests high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE). Stop further ascent when illness begins. Suspected HAPE or HACE requires urgent oxygen, descent or evacuation, and close respiratory assessment.

Which illness is developing?

Altitude illness emergency framework

Recognize → stop ascent → oxygenate → descend

Recognize acute mountain sickness

AMS often begins hours after ascent with headache plus nausea, dizziness, fatigue, or loss of appetite. Stop further ascent and reduce exertion. Mild stable symptoms may be observed and treated at the current altitude; worsening symptoms call for descent and reassessment.

Recognize lung edema

HAPE can begin with poor exercise tolerance, cough, and unusual breathlessness, then progress to breathlessness at rest, low oxygen, and crackles. Give oxygen, minimize exertion, arrange urgent descent or evacuation, and support ventilation if needed.

Recognize brain edema

HACE causes altered mental status or an unsteady walk after ascent. It is an emergency even if headache is not prominent. Protect the airway if alertness is poor, give oxygen, arrange immediate descent or evacuation, and prepare provider-directed dexamethasone.

Use backup measures wisely

If descent is delayed and oxygen is unavailable, a portable pressure chamber can temporarily raise effective oxygen pressure while evacuation is arranged. Acetazolamide helps acclimatization and may help AMS; dexamethasone treats HACE. Provider-directed nifedipine has a limited role for HAPE when descent and reliable oxygen or chamber support are unavailable.

Core emergency rule: never continue ascending with worsening symptoms. HAPE or HACE needs urgent oxygen when available, descent or evacuation, and repeat assessment.
Pressure-chamber limit: a portable pressure chamber is a temporary bridge when descent is delayed. It requires supervision and does not replace evacuation or definitive medical care.
Three syndromes, different urgency

Review each concept

Headache after recent ascent with nausea, dizziness, or fatigue supports acute mountain sickness (AMS). Stop further ascent, limit exertion, treat symptoms, and reassess. If symptoms worsen despite care, descend.
Worsening headache, repeated vomiting, inability to function, confusion, or trouble walking needs urgent evaluation. New neurologic changes raise concern for high-altitude cerebral edema rather than routine AMS.
High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is fluid buildup in the lungs at altitude. Increasing dyspnea with activity, cough, and reduced exercise ability may progress to breathlessness at rest, crackles, and marked hypoxemia.
Provide oxygen if available, limit exertion, arrange urgent descent or evacuation, and monitor breathing and oxygenation. In a medical facility, other respiratory support may be considered if the patient is alert and tolerates it; expert care directs medications.
High-altitude cerebral edema (HACE) causes brain dysfunction. Confusion, altered behavior, drowsiness, or ataxia (an unsteady walk) after ascent is an emergency, even when the patient denies a headache.
Give oxygen if available, prepare airway support if consciousness declines, and arrange immediate descent or evacuation. Dexamethasone may be given under protocol as an adjunct; it does not replace descent.
Lower altitude increases available oxygen pressure. Reduce the patient's exertion during evacuation and reassess mental status, breathing effort, oxygen saturation, and response to treatment throughout transport.
A portable hyperbaric or pressure chamber temporarily simulates lower altitude when descent cannot begin promptly and oxygen is unavailable. It requires a trained attendant and continued plans for evacuation.
Acetazolamide speeds acclimatization and may help AMS. Dexamethasone is used for HACE and selected severe AMS. Nifedipine for HAPE is a provider-directed option mainly when descent is impossible or delayed and reliable oxygen or chamber treatment is unavailable.
Not every cough, headache, or low oxygen reading at altitude is altitude illness. Recheck the trend and consider infection, asthma, cardiac disease, carbon monoxide exposure, or other causes while urgent support proceeds.
Integrated RTE-style emergency case

Prioritize a high-risk patient

Unacclimatized hiker at 3,800 m after rapid ascent:
  • Cough and marked breathlessness at rest
  • Oxygen saturation 70% on room air and crackles on exam
  • Increasing fatigue; a lower trailhead is accessible
  • Supplemental oxygen and a transport team are available
