Decompression Sickness & Arterial Gas Embolism

Decompression illness includes decompression sickness (DCS), caused when dissolved inert gas forms bubbles after a pressure drop, and arterial gas embolism (AGE), often caused when expanding lung gas enters arteries after a lung injury. Symptoms can overlap. After a dive, new pain, weakness, confusion, or breathing trouble calls for high-concentration oxygen, emergency care, and dive-medicine consultation.

What explains the new symptoms?

Decompression illness emergency framework

Recognize → oxygenate → stabilize → refer

Understand DCS

During a dive, extra inert gas dissolves in blood and tissues under pressure. If pressure falls faster than the gas can leave through the lungs, bubbles may form. DCS can cause joint pain, unusual fatigue, skin changes, numbness, weakness, or other neurologic symptoms.

Understand AGE

During ascent, trapped lung gas can overexpand and tear tissue. Gas may enter arteries and block blood flow, especially to the brain. Sudden confusion, weakness, seizure, or collapse during or soon after surfacing raises concern for AGE. The timing is a clue, not a sure diagnosis.

Provide immediate support

Remove the diver from the water, activate emergency medical services, assess breathing and pulse, and give high-concentration oxygen when available. Assist ventilation or begin resuscitation if needed. Do not return the diver underwater to treat symptoms.

Arrange definitive evaluation

Transport to the nearest appropriate emergency facility for stabilization and assessment, including possible lung injury. Consult a dive-medicine or hyperbaric team early. Hyperbaric oxygen may be indicated; a specialist directs the decision and safe transfer.

Core emergency rule: oxygen and emergency evaluation start promptly for suspected DCS or AGE, even when symptoms improve after the first oxygen treatment.
Diagnostic limit: DCS and AGE can share pain, weakness, and neurologic changes. A dramatic early onset favors AGE but does not rule out DCS or another emergency.
Mechanism, presentation & referral

Review each concept

At depth, inert gas dissolves into tissues under higher pressure. During ascent it must leave gradually. If excess gas comes out as bubbles, pain, skin changes, neurologic findings, or other problems may follow.
Expanding lung gas may rupture tissue during ascent, especially if gas is trapped. Bubbles that enter an artery can interrupt blood flow to the brain or other organs. This requires immediate emergency care.
AGE often begins during ascent or within minutes of surfacing. DCS may begin soon after a dive or appear later. Timing helps assess probability but is not a stand-alone diagnostic test.
Deep joint or limb pain, unusual fatigue, and mottled or itchy skin may occur with DCS. Symptoms may be subtle at first; repeat assessment and document when each began.
Weakness, numbness, trouble walking, confusion, vision change, or seizure after a dive is an emergency. Check mental status and motor function while oxygen and transport are arranged.
AGE can follow lung overexpansion. Chest pain, shortness of breath, or unequal breath sounds suggests associated barotrauma such as pneumothorax. Alert the receiving team because lung injury affects safe treatment planning.
Provide the highest feasible oxygen concentration with appropriate equipment to a breathing patient; support ventilation if breathing is inadequate. Oxygen is first aid for suspected decompression illness and should continue during transfer as available.
Call emergency services and take the diver to an appropriate emergency facility for airway, breathing, circulation, neurologic, and chest assessment. A hyperbaric chamber is not a substitute for initial stabilization.
A dive-medicine or hyperbaric specialist decides whether and how to provide recompression with oxygen. Prompt consultation supports timely treatment, while assessment for pneumothorax or other emergencies remains essential.
Handoff depth, duration, breathing gas, ascent rate, prior dives, symptom onset, neurologic findings, chest symptoms, oxygen given, and response. Improvement with oxygen does not end the need for evaluation.
Integrated RTE-style emergency case

Respond before the diagnosis is certain

Adult surfaces after a rapid ascent from a scuba dive:
  • New left arm weakness and confusion within five minutes
  • Breathing is spontaneous; oxygen saturation is 94% on room air
  • Mild chest discomfort accompanies the neurologic change
  • Emergency oxygen and transport are available
