NIV & CPAP Fundamentals

Noninvasive ventilation (NIV) supports breathing through an interface rather than an artificial airway. CPAP uses one continuous pressure. Bilevel NIV uses a higher inspiratory pressure and a lower expiratory pressure; the difference between them is pressure support and is a major driver of ventilatory assistance.

Which pressure does what?

Core pressure framework

Separate baseline pressure from inspiratory assistance

CPAP

Continuous positive airway pressure provides the same positive baseline pressure through inspiration and expiration. There is no separate inspiratory pressure-support difference.

Bilevel NIV

Bilevel support alternates between a higher inspiratory positive airway pressure (IPAP) and a lower expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP).

EPAP

EPAP functions as the expiratory baseline pressure and primarily supports airway/alveolar patency and oxygenation.

IPAP

IPAP is the higher inspiratory pressure. Its relationship to EPAP determines the amount of pressure assistance available during inspiration.

Pressure support = IPAP − EPAP
The pressure difference matters.

If IPAP is 15 and EPAP is 5, pressure support is 10 cmH₂O. If EPAP rises to 8 while IPAP remains 15, pressure support falls to 7 cmH₂O, so inspiratory assistance and tidal volume may fall unless IPAP is also adjusted.

CPAP maintains continuous positive pressure through the respiratory cycle.
It can improve airway patency and oxygenation/recruitment, but it does not provide separate inspiratory pressure support the way bilevel NIV does.
IPAP is the higher inspiratory pressure and EPAP is the lower expiratory baseline pressure.
The gap between IPAP and EPAP is pressure support and is a major determinant of how much inspiratory assistance is delivered.
Example: 12/4 gives 8 cmH₂O of pressure support.
Example: 15/5 gives 10 cmH₂O of pressure support.
If EPAP is increased without increasing IPAP, the pressure-support gap narrows and tidal-volume assistance may decrease.
A spontaneous NIV breath may be patient-triggered when the device detects inspiratory effort/flow.
If the patient does not trigger within the allowed interval and a timed backup is available, a machine-timed breath may occur.
Many spontaneous bilevel breaths are flow-cycled: inspiration ends as inspiratory flow falls to the device's cycling criterion.
Timed cycling may be used for mandatory/backup breaths depending on the mode/device.
A backup rate provides machine-triggered support when spontaneous breaths do not occur frequently enough.
The exact appropriate rate depends on the patient, mode, device and clinical goal; NIV-04 and NIV-05 later address adjustment and response in more depth.
