Why Mechanical Ventilation Is Needed

Mechanical ventilation supports a patient who can no longer maintain adequate ventilation, oxygenation, respiratory muscle work, or airway protection. The reason for intubation should drive the goal of support.

Primary: III.C.3a Invasive mechanical ventilationNo shared mapping in current crosswalk
Ventilation failureInadequate alveolar ventilation → rising PaCO₂ / respiratory acidosis.
Oxygenation failureSevere hypoxemia despite appropriate noninvasive support.
Work/fatigueRespiratory muscles can no longer sustain the demand.
Airway protectionThe patient cannot safely maintain/protect the airway.

Four reasons to escalate

Ventilation • oxygenation • work of breathing • airway protection

VentilationYour MV 1.1 source separates ventilation from oxygenation and uses rising PaCO₂, acidosis, inadequate tidal volume, and failure of noninvasive support as clues that ventilatory support is insufficient.
OxygenationThe source COPD scenario escalates directly to intubation when profound hypoxemia and poor cooperation make NIV unsafe or unlikely to succeed.
Respiratory muscle failureThe source emphasizes trending spontaneous breathing measurements in progressive neuromuscular disease before overt gas-exchange collapse occurs.
Airway protectionThe source explicitly states that a patient who cannot maintain the airway or has copious secretions may need intubation.
Mechanical ventilation is support—not the diagnosis.

Ask: What physiologic function is failing? The ventilator can support gas exchange and unload respiratory muscles, while the artificial airway can provide a protected route for ventilation when airway safety is inadequate.
