Obstructive Physiology & How to Recognize It

Obstruction means air leaves the lungs more slowly because airflow is limited. Forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) falls relative to forced vital capacity (FVC), and some air may remain trapped after exhalation. Compare the flow pattern, measured lung volumes, imaging, symptoms, and test quality before naming the patient’s condition.

Obstruction, restriction, or neither?

Obstructive-pattern framework

Airflow ratio → lung volumes → timing → patient

Airflow limitation

A narrowed airway or reduced elastic recoil raises resistance during expiration. FEV1/FVC below an appropriate lower limit of normal supports obstruction after checking test quality; the expiratory flow-volume curve may look scooped.

Trapped gas and hyperinflation

Air that cannot escape increases residual volume (RV) and often RV/total lung capacity (TLC). Hyperinflation raises the resting or total lung volume. A low FVC can result from air trapping, so true restriction requires a measured low TLC.

Dynamic change with fast breathing

If exhalation ends before flow finishes, the next breath starts with air still trapped. End-expiratory volume rises; intrinsic positive end-expiratory pressure (auto-PEEP) may develop, increasing inspiratory effort. A fast rate can worsen this cycle.

Clinical and imaging clues

Prolonged expiration, wheeze, diminished breath sounds, and exertional dyspnea can support obstruction; wheeze is not required. Imaging may show hyperinflation or a low, flat diaphragm. These clues guide assessment but do not replace well-performed pulmonary function testing (PFT).

Core rule: low FEV1/FVC supports airflow obstruction. A low FVC by itself does not prove restriction; confirm a restrictive defect with measured TLC and assess whether gas trapping explains the small FVC.
Chart cue: interpret the measured values with the patient’s age-appropriate reference limits, test quality, symptoms, risk factors, and imaging. Asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and other disorders can produce obstruction for different reasons.
The obstructive pattern

Review each concept

This ratio compares air expelled in the first second with all air exhaled during the forced maneuver. If it is below the appropriate lower limit of normal on a quality test, airflow obstruction is present.
Obstruction often produces a scooped, concave expiratory limb and slow late flow. A curve supports the numerical finding but also exposes poor effort or early termination that may make the result unreliable.
Narrowed or collapsible airways resist outgoing flow. The patient may take longer to empty the lungs and use more effort; airway inflammation, smooth-muscle narrowing, mucus, or lost elastic recoil can contribute.
Residual volume (RV) is air left after maximal exhalation. RV or RV/TLC above the upper reference limit supports air trapping; it is measured with lung volumes, not read directly from basic spirometry.
Air trapping means extra gas remains after exhaling. Hyperinflation means lung volumes such as resting end-expiratory volume or TLC are elevated. They can occur together but are not interchangeable labels.
A patient with obstructed, slowly emptying airways may stop the forced blow with air still inside, lowering FVC. A measured low TLC is needed to establish restriction; a mixed pattern requires both findings.
During fast breathing, expiration may end before the lungs empty. The next inhalation adds to retained gas and raises end-expiratory volume. Work of breathing increases and a flattened diaphragm works less effectively.
Incomplete exhalation can leave positive pressure in the lungs before the next breath: intrinsic PEEP or auto-PEEP. On a ventilator, expiratory flow failing to return toward zero is an important clue, not the sole diagnostic test.
A low, flattened diaphragm, increased lung volumes, or hyperlucent fields can accompany hyperinflation. A normal image does not exclude obstruction, and imaging cannot replace a valid spirometry interpretation.
Ask about wheeze, cough, triggers, exertional dyspnea, smoking or exposure history, and prior response to treatment. A quiet chest or fatigue during acute distress can be more concerning than loud wheezing.
