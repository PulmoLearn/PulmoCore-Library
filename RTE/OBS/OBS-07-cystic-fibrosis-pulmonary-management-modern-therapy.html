Cystic Fibrosis: Pulmonary Management & Modern Therapy

Cystic fibrosis (CF) changes how salt and water move at the airway surface. Thick mucus is harder to clear and can support repeated infection and lung damage. Effective care joins an individualized airway-clearance routine, mucus-directed inhaled medicines, infection surveillance, nutrition support, and a check for treatments that address the faulty protein.

Which treatment targets which problem?

CF care framework

Protein → mucus → infection → whole-person care

Understand the protein problem

The cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) protein moves chloride and helps maintain water at the airway surface. When CFTR does not work well, mucus becomes thick and difficult to clear; infections and inflammation can follow.

Match clearance and mucus therapy

Individualize airway-clearance techniques with a trained team. Dornase alfa helps break down DNA in mucus; inhaled hypertonic saline draws water toward airway secretions. These can support, but do not automatically replace, physical clearance.

Monitor infection and systemic needs

Compare cough, sputum, weight, activity, lung function, and respiratory cultures with baseline. Treat infection based on current and prior microbiology and clinical severity. Pancreatic insufficiency may require enzymes and nutrition support.

Recognize modulator eligibility

CFTR modulators improve function of protein made by eligible variants. Review the patient’s genotype and current treatment eligibility with a CF center; these drugs do not work for every genotype and do not automatically eliminate infection monitoring or other care.

Core rule: airway clearance, mucus-directed medicine, infection care, nutrition, and eligible CFTR modulation solve different parts of the disease. Reassess the patient rather than assuming any one therapy replaces the rest.
Chart cue: a new decline in forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), increased sputum, or weight loss warrants review of clearance, cultures, nutrition, and the overall treatment plan.
CF treatment targets

Review each concept

CFTR is a protein channel involved in chloride transport. Reduced function alters salt and water at epithelial surfaces, making airway mucus unusually thick and harder to remove.
Poor clearance can allow bacteria to persist and inflammation to continue. Chronic infection and airway injury may cause bronchiectasis and progressively reduce lung function; care aims to interrupt the cycle.
Methods include positive expiratory pressure, percussion, active-cycle breathing, and oscillating devices. No single method suits every patient. Check technique, tolerance, and the amount of sputum actually cleared.
Inhaled dornase alfa cuts extracellular DNA released by inflammatory cells. That can reduce mucus thickness and support clearance; it is a mucus-directed treatment, not an antibiotic or CFTR modulator.
Inhaled hypertonic saline pulls water into the airway surface and can make mucus easier to move. Watch for irritation or bronchospasm, and integrate the treatment into the patient’s clearance routine.
Respiratory cultures help identify organisms such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and inform treatment choices. A change in sputum or lung function should be interpreted with prior results, symptoms, and new testing.
More cough or sputum, worse exercise tolerance, falling FEV1, weight loss, or increased work of breathing may indicate a flare-up. Prompt clinical assessment and a plan for infection and clearance are important.
Many people with CF have pancreatic insufficiency and need prescribed pancreatic enzyme replacement and nutritional monitoring. Poor weight gain can worsen overall resilience and deserves multidisciplinary review.
Correctors help some CFTR protein fold and reach the surface; potentiators help some protein channels work. Eligibility depends on genotype, current approvals, age, and specialist assessment, not on lung symptoms alone.
Even with a strong response to a modulator, lung infection, airway damage, nutrition, and treatment burden still need follow-up. Do not stop other prescribed therapies without an individualized CF-team review.

One drug is not the whole plan

A young adult with CF takes a prescribed CFTR modulator and reports new cough:
  • Sputum production increased and FEV1 fell from the patient’s usual value
  • Airway-clearance sessions have been missed because of a difficult schedule
  • Past cultures grew Pseudomonas; a new respiratory culture is pending
  • Weight is falling and prescribed pancreatic enzymes are often missed
