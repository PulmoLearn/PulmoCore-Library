Bronchiectasis & Chronic Secretion Disease

Bronchiectasis means permanently widened, damaged airways that have trouble clearing mucus. Patients may cough up sputum every day and develop repeated infections. Compare the current sputum and breathing with baseline, keep airway clearance practical, and use sputum testing and the patient’s response to guide changes in care.

Usual sputum—or a new flare-up?

Bronchiectasis care framework

Structure → secretions → culture → response

Confirm the airway problem

Chronic productive cough and repeated chest infections raise suspicion. Thin-section computed tomography (CT) demonstrates abnormally widened airways; review the history and investigate a treatable cause when appropriate.

Compare sputum with baseline

Record usual and current volume, thickness, color, blood, and ease of clearance. New sputum changes together with worse cough, breathlessness, or fatigue suggest an exacerbation. Obtain a lower-airway sample for culture when feasible, ideally before new antibiotics.

Make airway clearance workable

Teach an individualized airway-clearance method such as active breathing techniques or a positive expiratory pressure device. Assess whether the patient can bring up mucus, tolerates the method, and has a realistic daily plan; adjust frequency or technique during a flare-up.

Treat, monitor, and prevent

Use prior and new cultures plus clinical severity to guide antibiotic choices in collaboration with the treating team. Track exacerbations and persistent pathogens, support exercise and vaccinations, and consider specialist-directed prevention in frequent exacerbators.

Core rule: mucus and infection can reinforce each other. Effective individualized clearance plus attention to sputum changes, cultures, and exacerbation response guides the next care-plan change.
Chart cue: sputum color alone neither identifies an organism nor selects an antibiotic. Compare the whole patient with baseline and use a quality lower-airway specimen and prior microbiology.
Airways and secretions

Review each concept

Damaged bronchi may lose normal tapering and clear mucus poorly. CT confirms the structural change. Airway widening alone is not a substitute for interpreting symptoms and any underlying cause.
Retained mucus can support repeated infection and inflammation; ongoing injury makes clearance harder. Treatment aims to break this cycle while preserving activity and reducing future flare-ups.
Ask how much sputum a patient normally produces, what it looks like, whether it is easy to cough up, and how far the patient can walk. A baseline makes later changes meaningful.
A good specimen comes from a deep cough into a sterile container, not mainly saliva. Note quantity, color, thickness, and blood; send for requested tests promptly under local procedure.
A new purulent appearance can support the picture of an exacerbation, but it cannot name the pathogen. Obtain sputum for culture before antibiotics when feasible; review prior organisms and susceptibility as results return.
More cough or sputum, new purulence, breathlessness, fatigue, fever, or reduced exercise capacity can signal a flare-up. Assess oxygenation, work of breathing, bleeding, and whether the patient needs urgent evaluation.
Options include active-cycle breathing, huff coughing, positioning, and positive expiratory pressure devices. A trained clinician selects techniques around symptoms, tolerance, and the patient’s ability to perform them.
Track sputum brought up, comfort, breathlessness, and adherence. If mucus remains hard to move, review technique and consider an adjusted regimen rather than repeating an ineffective session unchanged.
Repeated Pseudomonas aeruginosa growth may signal greater risk and prompt specialist review. Long-term inhaled antibiotics can be considered in patients with chronic infection and high exacerbation risk, not by reflex after one unconfirmed sample.
Support daily clearance, exercise, recommended immunizations, and review of treatable causes. Specialist-directed long-term macrolides or inhaled antibiotics may fit selected high-risk patients; inhaled steroids are not routine without another indication such as asthma.

A different sputum pattern this week

An adult with CT-confirmed bronchiectasis reports a new three-day change:
  • Usual morning sputum is small-volume and pale; now it is much more plentiful, thick, and yellow-green
  • Cough and breathlessness increased and home clearance no longer brings up mucus easily
  • A prior culture grew Pseudomonas aeruginosa; no new antibiotic has been started
  • The patient is alert but needs clinical review; a fresh deep-cough sample is available
