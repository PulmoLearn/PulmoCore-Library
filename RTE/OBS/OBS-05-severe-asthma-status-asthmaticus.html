Severe Asthma & Status Asthmaticus

A severe asthma attack can move from loud wheezing to barely moving air. A major drop in peak flow, difficulty speaking, exhaustion, altered alertness, and a silent chest demand rapid action. If carbon dioxide rises as the patient tires, ventilation may be failing even while oxygen is being given.

More wheeze—or less airflow?

Severe-asthma rescue framework

Severity → simultaneous care → trend → airway

Measure the attack, not just the sound

Assess speech, respiratory rate, work of breathing, oxygen saturation, mental status, and peak expiratory flow (PEF) against predicted or personal-best values when the patient can perform it safely. A silent chest or drowsiness can mean life-threatening loss of airflow.

Give concurrent initial therapy

Arrange urgent higher-level care for severe attacks. Start repeated inhaled short-acting beta2 agonist (SABA), add inhaled ipratropium when indicated, give systemic corticosteroids early, and titrate oxygen as needed. Do not wait for a blood gas before treating an unstable patient.

Watch the ventilation trend

An arterial blood gas (ABG) is useful when the patient deteriorates or fails initial care. Early attacks often lower arterial carbon dioxide pressure (PaCO2) from rapid breathing; a normalizing or rising PaCO2 with fatigue can signal failure. Follow pH and the patient, not PaCO2 alone.

Escalate before arrest

If effort increases, airflow remains poor, alertness falls, or gases worsen, request critical-care and airway help. Intravenous magnesium sulfate may be considered for selected severe attacks that do not respond well. Prepare for controlled intubation and ventilation when respiratory failure or poor airway protection develops.

Core rule: fewer wheezes are reassuring only if airflow and effort improve. A tired, quiet patient with falling PEF or rising PaCO2 is at risk of respiratory failure.
Chart cue: an acute severe attack is not the same label as chronic severe asthma, which is judged by the treatment needed for long-term control. Here the priority is immediate rescue and response-based escalation.
An attack that changes quickly

Review each concept

Short phrases, marked accessory muscle use, fast breathing, low oxygen saturation, and very low PEF point to severe obstruction. Do not delay care because an exact peak-flow number cannot be obtained safely.
Wheeze needs moving air to make sound. Markedly reduced breath sounds with exhaustion or altered consciousness may mean almost no air is flowing and requires immediate airway-capable help.
Compare quality PEF attempts with predicted or personal-best values and repeat after treatment if the patient can perform them. A very low or falling value despite therapy adds urgency; it never outweighs obvious clinical deterioration.
Titrate supplemental oxygen using oximetry and clinical status; a typical adult target during acute asthma treatment is about 93–95%. Oxygen addresses hypoxemia but does not reverse severe bronchospasm or ventilatory fatigue.
Repeated inhaled SABA, commonly albuterol, is a first-line acute bronchodilator. Delivery may use a metered-dose inhaler with spacer or a nebulizer as the situation allows; evaluate airflow and effort after doses.
In emergency care, an inhaled short-acting antimuscarinic such as ipratropium added to SABA can improve early outcomes in moderate or severe attacks. It complements rather than replaces bronchodilator and steroid treatment.
Systemic corticosteroids reduce inflammation and relapse risk but take time to help. Giving them early is important; waiting for steroid effect is unsafe if airflow, fatigue, or mental status worsens.
Patients often lower PaCO2 by breathing rapidly early in an attack. A normal or rising PaCO2 during ongoing severe distress, especially with falling pH, suggests inadequate ventilation and possible exhaustion.
Intravenous magnesium sulfate may be considered for selected adults with a severe attack or a poor response to initial treatment. It does not replace repeated bronchodilation, corticosteroids, monitoring, or airway preparation.
Falling alertness, silent chest, exhaustion, or worsening blood gases require urgent critical-care review and preparation for intubation. During invasive ventilation, obstructed lungs need time to exhale to limit air trapping.

The wheeze is fading for the wrong reason

An adult with asthma receives repeated albuterol, ipratropium, oxygen, and systemic corticosteroid:
  • Initial PEF is 190 L/min against a personal best of 500 L/min
  • After treatment PEF remains near 200 L/min; accessory muscle use persists
  • Wheeze fades, breath sounds become very faint, and the patient grows drowsy
  • ABG changes from pH 7.39/PaCO2 35 to pH 7.30/PaCO2 48 mm Hg
