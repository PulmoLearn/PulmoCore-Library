Asthma: Assessment, Severity & Long-Term Control

Asthma often causes wheeze, cough, chest tightness, or breathlessness that changes over time. Pair that history with objective evidence of variable expiratory airflow. Then assess current control and future risk, check inhaler technique and adherence, and recommend an anti-inflammatory treatment plan that fits the patient.

Is asthma controlled—and what changes?

Asthma decision framework

Variable airflow → control and risk → controller plan

Establish variable airflow

Ask about changing symptoms, triggers, and timing; confirm variability with suitable spirometry or serial peak expiratory flow (PEF) when indicated. A normal reading on a quiet day does not rule out asthma. Repeat testing if suspicion remains.

Separate control from severity

Control describes recent symptom burden and future exacerbation risk. Severity is judged later by the level of treatment needed to keep asthma controlled; frequent symptoms today do not by themselves prove severe asthma.

Use an anti-inflammatory foundation

Inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)-containing treatment reduces attack risk. For many adults and adolescents, low-dose ICS–formoterol as needed is the preferred reliever strategy; the exact step depends on symptoms, risk, availability, and the patient’s regimen. Avoid a short-acting beta2 agonist (SABA)-only long-term plan.

Assess before stepping up

Watch the patient use the inhaler, ask about actual use and access, identify exposures and other conditions, and review a written action plan. If control remains poor despite these checks, recommend a guideline-consistent treatment change and schedule reassessment.

Core rule: a symptom-free day does not exclude asthma; and low symptoms do not erase attack risk. Confirm variable airflow when possible, assess both symptom control and risk, and keep an ICS-containing strategy in the long-term plan.
Chart cue: PEF trends can reveal changing airflow and guide an individualized action plan, but one isolated peak-flow value cannot establish severity or replace the whole clinical assessment.
The asthma control pathway

Review each concept

Wheeze, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and cough may change with time, exercise, viral illness, allergens, or irritants. Night or early-morning symptoms are useful clues, but other diagnoses may share these complaints.
Bronchodilator response on spirometry or a meaningful pattern of changing peak flow can support asthma. If objective evidence is missing, arrange suitable testing; a single normal test between symptomatic periods does not always exclude it.
Peak expiratory flow (PEF) is the highest flow during a forceful blow. Compare good-technique readings with the patient’s personal best or serial values; a downward trend can prompt action under the individualized plan.
Assess daytime symptoms, night waking, reliever use in the context of the reliever prescribed, and limits on activity. A good week may still coexist with future attack risk.
Prior severe attacks, low lung function, excess SABA use, smoke exposure, and lack of ICS treatment can increase exacerbation risk. A history of hospitalization deserves attention even when current symptoms are few.
Asthma severity is inferred from the intensity of therapy needed to maintain good control after treatment has been optimized. Difficult-to-treat symptoms due to poor device technique or missed medication are not proof of intrinsically severe asthma.
ICS treats airway inflammation and reduces attack risk. For many adults and adolescents, an ICS–formoterol combination can serve as an anti-inflammatory reliever; some use a maintenance-and-reliever regimen, according to the prescribed step.
A SABA relieves bronchospasm quickly but does not treat airway inflammation. A SABA-only plan is not a safe long-term default; if a SABA reliever is used, pair it with an appropriate ICS-containing strategy.
Ask the patient to show every step: preparation, inhalation, timing, and device-specific use. Review adherence and affordability without blame before increasing doses or labeling treatment failure.
Provide a written action plan with the patient’s usual symptoms or peak flow, steps for worsening control, and when to seek urgent care. Review triggers, smoking exposure, vaccinations, and the response after any change.

Symptoms despite a “mild” label

  • Night waking twice a week and reduced activity
  • Rescue SABA use most days, without an ICS-containing regimen
  • Usual personal-best PEF 480 L/min; repeat good-technique readings this week near 340 L/min
  • No current severe distress; inhaler technique has not been checked
