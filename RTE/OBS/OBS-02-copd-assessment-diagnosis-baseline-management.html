COPD: Assessment, Diagnosis & Baseline Management

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is persistent airflow limitation with symptoms and exposure history that vary from person to person. Suspect it from the patient story, confirm it with quality spirometry after a bronchodilator, then measure symptoms and prior flare-ups before planning long-term care.

Is this COPD, and what matters next?

COPD assessment framework

Story → confirmation → burden → plan

Build the clinical story

Ask about breathlessness, cough, sputum, activity limits, smoking or other inhaled exposures, prior respiratory infections, and competing causes. Check current inhalers, use technique, and comorbid disease.

Confirm persistent obstruction

COPD requires quality post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in one second / forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC) below 0.70 in the right clinical context. If the result is near the threshold or conflicts with the story, review test quality and repeat or clarify as appropriate.

Measure burden and future risk

Record symptom burden, daily function, and the number and severity of prior exacerbations, including emergency visits or admissions. Spirometric severity, symptoms, and future flare-up risk are related but are not the same measure.

Create a practical long-term plan

Review exposure cessation, immunizations, activity and pulmonary rehabilitation when indicated, correct inhaler technique, and an individualized maintenance bronchodilator plan. Reassess symptoms and exacerbations; consider inhaled corticosteroids selectively rather than automatically.

Core rule: chronic symptoms and risk exposure raise suspicion, but a quality post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.70 is required to establish COPD. Then assess symptom burden and exacerbation history to guide care.
Chart cue: a chest image, smoking history, or single symptom score does not by itself establish COPD or determine its complete treatment plan.
The COPD baseline

Review each concept

Tobacco smoke is common, but occupational dust or fumes, indoor biomass smoke, and other risks matter. Ask about family history and consider alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency testing in people diagnosed with COPD according to clinical guidance.
Gradual breathlessness, chronic cough, and sputum are common; any can be absent. Ask what walking, work, sleep, and ordinary activities cost the patient, not just whether wheeze is present.
A quality post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.70 establishes persistent airflow obstruction in the right setting. A low ratio before the bronchodilator alone is not enough to confirm COPD; review borderline or poor-quality tests.
Emphysema involves destruction of air-space walls and loss of recoil; chronic bronchitis describes long-standing productive cough after other causes are excluded. These patterns can overlap, and neither replaces diagnostic spirometry.
A structured breathlessness or health-status questionnaire can make function easier to compare over time. The modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scale focuses on breathlessness; the COPD Assessment Test (CAT) covers broader symptoms.
An exacerbation is an acute worsening of breathing symptoms beyond usual variation that leads to additional treatment. Ask about recent courses of treatment, urgent visits, and hospitalizations; prior events signal future risk.
Short-acting inhaled bronchodilators relieve intermittent symptoms. Long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMAs) and long-acting beta2 agonists (LABAs) are maintenance options; using both may fit patients with greater symptom or flare-up burden.
An inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) may help reduce exacerbations in selected patients, especially when blood eosinophils support benefit or asthma also exists. It is not a routine standalone COPD inhaler; weigh pneumonia and other risks.
Support smoking cessation, reduce harmful exposures, check recommended vaccinations, encourage activity, and refer for pulmonary rehabilitation when indicated. Ask the patient to demonstrate inhaler use; reassess technique and adherence at follow-up.
Long-term oxygen can improve outcomes in severe chronic resting hypoxemia when formal criteria are met. A single low reading during an illness or exertion does not by itself establish the need for lifelong oxygen; evaluate comorbidities too.
