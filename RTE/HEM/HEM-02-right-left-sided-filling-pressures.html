Right- and Left-Sided Filling Pressures

Catheter pressures help locate pressure in the right heart, pulmonary artery, and left-sided filling pathway. Central venous pressure (CVP), pulmonary artery pressure (PAP), and pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) are most useful when read with a reliable waveform, a trend, and the patient’s breathing and perfusion. A pressure alone cannot tell whether more fluid will increase cardiac output.

Which side is under pressure?

Filling-pressure framework

Validate → identify location → compare trend → act on patient

CVP reflects right-sided filling pressure

Central venous pressure (CVP) approximates right atrial pressure. It may rise with right-heart strain, positive-pressure ventilation, or excess venous pressure. A single CVP is not a direct measurement of circulating blood volume.

PAP is pressure in the lung arteries

Pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) reports systolic, diastolic, and mean pressure in the pulmonary artery. It can rise with pulmonary vascular disease, increased flow, or high left-sided pressure; interpret it with CVP and wedge pressure.

Wedge estimates left-sided pressure

A valid pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) estimates left atrial pressure under suitable conditions. A high wedge with new crackles can support left-sided congestion; it is not a direct measure of left ventricular volume or fluid responsiveness.

Trust the trend only after checking quality

Check the waveform, transducer level and zero, respiratory timing, and effects of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP). Compare a prior value measured the same way, then integrate oxygenation, blood pressure, urine output, and perfusion.

Core rule: CVP is mainly right-sided, wedge mainly reflects left atrial pressure, and PAP lies between. None of these static pressures alone proves that a fluid bolus will raise stroke volume.
Measurement cue: end-expiratory readings and consistent transducer leveling help compare pressures. High intrathoracic pressure, an invalid wedge, or a changed measurement setup can mislead interpretation.
Interpreting filling pressure trends

Review each concept

CVP approximates right atrial pressure. A rising CVP can occur with right-heart failure, pulmonary vascular strain, positive-pressure ventilation, or increased venous filling; compare with the rest of the patient.
PAP is measured as systolic, diastolic, and mean pulmonary arterial pressure. A high PAP can reflect pulmonary vascular disease or pressure passed back from the left heart.
PAWP is measured with an occluded pulmonary artery catheter tip and can estimate left atrial pressure under appropriate conditions. Verify the waveform and avoid prolonged balloon inflation.
When PAWP rises along with new crackles, orthopnea, and oxygen need, elevated left-sided filling pressure may contribute to pulmonary congestion. Consider other causes and notify the team.
High right-sided pressure with a relatively normal wedge can suggest right-heart or pulmonary vascular strain. It does not alone identify pulmonary embolism or a unique cause.
Compare readings at consistent transducer level and respiratory phase. A changed zero, line problem, or invalid wedge tracing may create an apparent pressure change.
PEEP raises intrathoracic pressure and can affect measured CVP and wedge. Review ventilator changes before assuming a pressure rise reflects added fluid.
Compare prior CVP, PAP, and wedge measurements with oxygen requirement, breath sounds, cardiac output if available, blood pressure, and urine output. A trend matters more than one isolated number.
CVP and wedge pressure are not reliable stand-alone tests of fluid responsiveness. The team may use a dynamic response in stroke volume or other appropriate assessment when the fluid decision is uncertain.
Report the actual pressures and time, waveform quality, ventilator or PEEP changes, respiratory findings, and perfusion trend. Explain what the pattern suggests without claiming it proves volume status.
Integrated RTE-style pressure case

Rising wedge with new crackles

A ventilated adult has new dyspnea and bilateral crackles:
  • Wedge pressure has risen from 12 to 24 mm Hg with a valid waveform
  • CVP has risen from 8 to 15 mm Hg on the same setup
  • Oxygen need and breathing effort are increasing
  • PEEP and transducer position have not changed
