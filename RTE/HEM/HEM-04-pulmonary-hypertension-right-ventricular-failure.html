Pulmonary Hypertension & Right-Ventricular Failure

Pulmonary hypertension raises pressure in the arteries of the lungs. The right ventricle must pump against that load. As it strains, blood can back up on the right while less reaches the body. Compare pulmonary pressure, filling pressures, cardiac output, oxygenation, and blood pressure before deciding what a change means.

Where is the pressure rising?

Right-heart pressure framework

PAP → wedge and PVR → right-heart flow → response

Pressure in the lung arteries

Pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) rises when blood faces greater resistance through the lungs or when high left-heart pressure backs up. Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) describes the opposition to flow through the lung vessels. PAP alone cannot identify the cause.

Compare the wedge

Pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) estimates left atrial pressure when the tracing is valid. A high PAP with a high wedge may reflect pressure transmitted from the left heart. High PAP with a normal wedge and high PVR points toward a precapillary process.

Check the right ventricle and flow

Central venous pressure (CVP) approximates right atrial pressure. Rising CVP with falling cardiac output, low blood pressure, and a wedge that stays near normal raises concern for right-ventricular strain or failure. Check perfusion and the complete clinical picture.

Balance breathing support

Low oxygen, excess carbon dioxide, and acidemia can tighten lung vessels. Correct these problems while watching blood pressure and output. Too much positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) can reduce venous return and add right-heart load; enough pressure may still be needed to recruit the lung.

Core rule: high right-sided pressure with falling output and a relatively normal wedge suggests right-heart strain. Confirm the measurements, support oxygenation, report poor perfusion, and investigate the cause.
Hemodynamic cue: right-heart catheterization is used to define pulmonary hypertension. A mean PAP above 20 mm Hg meets the pressure definition. A precapillary pattern also requires wedge at or below 15 mm Hg and PVR above 2 Wood units. Do not label a specific disease from PAP alone.
Reading the pressure pattern

Review each concept

PAP records pressure in the lung artery. A high reading signals an increased pressure load, but it does not alone show whether the cause is lung vessels, left-heart pressure, or a temporary change in flow.
A correctly measured PAWP estimates left atrial pressure. High PAP plus high wedge can reflect left-heart pressure passing backward; high PAP with normal wedge calls for other causes to be considered.
PVR relates the pressure difference across the lungs to cardiac output: (mean PAP − PAWP) ÷ cardiac output. For example, (35 − 10) ÷ 5 = 5 Wood units. A higher result means more resistance to forward flow.
The right ventricle normally pumps into a low-resistance circuit. An abrupt rise in lung-vessel resistance can enlarge the ventricle and reduce its forward flow.
A rising CVP can reflect blood backing up before the right ventricle. Compare it with cardiac output, wedge, blood pressure, and the patient; one CVP number does not predict fluid response.
Falling cardiac output and blood pressure may lead to cool skin, confusion, and less urine. A high PAP is especially urgent when the patient also shows signs of poor tissue blood flow.
Low oxygen can tighten pulmonary vessels and add right-heart load. Support oxygenation while monitoring breathing, blood pressure, and the response to each intervention.
Excess carbon dioxide and acidemia can raise lung-vessel resistance. Check blood gas trends when available and correct worsening ventilation without causing harmful lung overdistension.
Positive-pressure ventilation and excessive PEEP may reduce venous return or raise lung-vessel resistance. Review recent changes with the team and balance lung recruitment against the effect on the right heart.
Possible causes include pulmonary embolism, chronic lung disease or low oxygen, and left-heart disease. Compare the onset, imaging and echo findings, pressure waveforms, output, and response; report sudden deterioration promptly.
Integrated RTE-style right-heart case

Oxygenation falls as right-sided pressure rises

A ventilated adult with known pulmonary hypertension worsens:
  • PAP is 62/30 mm Hg, CVP rises from 8 to 18 mm Hg, and the valid wedge stays at 10 mm Hg
  • Cardiac output falls, blood pressure reaches 82/48 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation is 84%
  • A recent PEEP increase is documented; the cause of deterioration remains under evaluation
