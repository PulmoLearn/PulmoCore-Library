Fluid Responsiveness & Dynamic Assessment

A low blood pressure does not by itself show that more fluid will improve blood flow. Fluid responsiveness means the heart pumps more blood after a temporary increase in filling. Compare a change in stroke volume or cardiac output with perfusion and signs of fluid harm before recommending more fluid.

Will more fluid improve flow?

Dynamic assessment framework

Perfusion → reversible test → measured flow → risk

Start with the clinical need

Look for poor tissue blood flow: falling blood pressure, cool skin, confusion, low urine output, or rising lactate. Also look for lung congestion and heart dysfunction. A fluid response is useful only if more fluid is needed and reasonably safe.

Test a reversible change

A passive leg raise (PLR) briefly shifts blood toward the heart without giving fluid. Measure stroke volume (SV) or cardiac output (CO) while the legs are raised, then observe whether the change reverses. Blood pressure alone can miss a flow response.

Check a measured fluid challenge

When appropriate, a small clinician-directed fluid challenge tests whether SV or CO rises. Record the amount and timing, reassess perfusion and breathing, and stop or reconsider if congestion or hypoxemia develops.

Know when variation misleads

Pulse pressure variation (PPV) and stroke volume variation (SVV) can help in selected mechanically ventilated patients. Spontaneous breathing, irregular rhythm, small tidal volumes, and right-heart failure can make these measures unreliable.

Core rule: a central venous pressure (CVP) or wedge value describes pressure at one moment; it cannot alone predict whether another fluid dose will raise flow. Test a change and measure its effect when feasible.
Decision cue: a positive response to a PLR or fluid challenge suggests the heart can raise flow with more filling. It does not by itself prove that giving fluid is safe, needed, or the best treatment. Assess the patient and cause of shock.
Testing whether flow rises

Review each concept

A patient is fluid responsive when a rise in filling leads to a meaningful rise in stroke volume or cardiac output. The definition is about flow, not simply a rise in blood pressure.
Assess blood pressure, mental status, skin, urine output, and lactate trends. Even if the heart can increase flow, extra fluid may not help a stable patient.
CVP and a valid pulmonary artery wedge pressure describe filling pressures. They can be useful in context, but one value cannot reliably predict a flow response to fluid.
When suitable, move from a semirecumbent position to a position with legs raised and torso lowered. The temporary shift of blood can test a response without adding fluid; use a real-time flow measure.
Track stroke volume or cardiac output before and during the leg raise, then after returning to the starting position. A reproducible rise during the maneuver supports fluid responsiveness.
A team-directed small fluid dose is another test. Measure flow and reassess the clinical response promptly; the dose cannot be taken back, so weigh the risk of congestion.
PPV reflects beat-to-beat arterial pulse pressure changes during ventilation. It is most useful under appropriate controlled ventilation and regular rhythm, with a trustworthy arterial waveform.
SVV tracks variation in stroke volume across ventilator breaths. It has similar limitations to PPV: breathing effort, rhythm changes, small tidal volumes, and right-heart problems can distort the result.
New crackles, worsening oxygen need, lung edema, or rising venous congestion may signal harm from fluid. Report these changes even when a dynamic test suggests a possible flow response.
State what was measured, how much it changed, whether the patient’s perfusion improved, and whether breathing worsened. Reassess the cause of low flow and treatment with the team.
Integrated RTE-style fluid-response case

Pressure stays low; what does the test show?

An adult with possible infection has low blood pressure and cool skin:
  • CVP is 11 mm Hg; a single filling pressure does not settle the fluid decision
  • Cardiac output rises from 4.0 to 4.6 L/min during a passive leg raise and returns to 4.0 afterward
  • Oxygenation is stable, with no new crackles; the team is considering additional fluid
