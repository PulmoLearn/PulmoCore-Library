Shock Patterns & Hemodynamic Profiles

Shock means tissues are not receiving enough useful blood flow. The cause may be low circulating volume, loss of vascular tone, poor heart pumping, or an obstruction to flow. Use pressure and cardiac-output data with skin, mental status, urine output, lactate, and breathing to recognize the likely pattern and urgent risk.

What mechanism is limiting flow?

Shock profile framework

Perfusion → mechanism → filling and flow → team response

Hypovolemic: too little effective volume

Bleeding or major fluid loss reduces filling and stroke volume. Central venous pressure (CVP) and pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) may be low, cardiac output falls, and vessels often tighten. Look for history of loss and signs of poor perfusion.

Distributive: vessels are too dilated

In sepsis or anaphylaxis, systemic vascular resistance (SVR) may fall sharply. Skin can be warm early in sepsis, and cardiac output may initially be normal or high; it can fall later. A single warm-skin finding does not make shock safe.

Cardiogenic: the pump is failing

Poor ventricular pumping lowers cardiac output. Left-sided filling pressure may rise, and crackles or pulmonary edema can appear. Blood pressure and perfusion may fall despite abundant circulating volume.

Obstructive: flow is blocked

A massive pulmonary embolism or tension pneumothorax can restrict blood through the heart or lungs. Cardiac output falls and right-sided pressure may rise, while a left-sided wedge may remain normal or low. Identify the cause urgently.

Core rule: a profile suggests a cause; it does not prove one. Mixed shock, positive-pressure ventilation, medicines, and measurement errors can change the expected numbers.
Pattern cue: low filling suggests volume loss; low SVR suggests vasodilation; high wedge plus low output suggests pump failure; high right pressure with normal wedge and sudden dyspnea raises concern for blocked pulmonary flow.
Recognizing the four mechanisms

Review each concept

Hypotension can accompany shock, but assess the whole patient: altered mentation, cool or mottled skin, reduced urine output, and rising lactate may reveal inadequate delivery.
Hemorrhage or severe fluid loss can lower venous filling, stroke volume, and cardiac output. The body often raises heart rate and vascular tone to compensate.
Marked vasodilation lowers systemic vascular resistance. In early septic shock, cardiac output may be high and skin warm; later perfusion may worsen. Anaphylaxis is another distributive cause.
A weakened pump lowers flow despite filling. High wedge pressure and crackles can point toward left-sided failure, but mixed shock or right-sided disease may change the pattern.
Pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax, or tamponade can block filling or forward flow. A sudden collapse with elevated right-sided pressure needs urgent cause-focused assessment.
CVP approximates right atrial pressure. It can be low in volume loss or high in right-heart strain; ventilation and transducer setup also affect the value.
A valid PAWP approximates left atrial pressure. Higher wedge plus new lung congestion supports left-sided pressure overload; normal wedge despite high CVP can support right-sided strain.
Cardiac output can be low in volume loss, pump failure, or obstruction. Low SVR helps suggest distributive shock, but late sepsis or mixed shock may show different output.
Review pressure and output trends, medication or ventilator changes, recent blood loss, fever, and sudden chest symptoms. A static pressure does not tell whether a fluid bolus will help.
Support oxygenation and ventilation, alert the team, and report the likely mechanism and evidence. Treat a reversible cause promptly through the appropriate team pathway and reassess perfusion.
Integrated RTE-style shock case

Sudden collapse with right-sided pressure

An adult becomes suddenly breathless and hypotensive:
  • Cardiac output is falling and CVP rises from 7 to 18 mm Hg
  • Wedge pressure remains near 10 mm Hg with a reliable tracing
  • Oxygenation falls and a new right-ventricular strain pattern is reported
  • The team is assessing for a possible large pulmonary embolism
