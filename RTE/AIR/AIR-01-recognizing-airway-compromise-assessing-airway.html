Rapid airway assessment

Patency, protection, difficulty, urgency

The source airway guide emphasizes assessing whether the airway is open before thinking about a device.

PatencyIs air moving? Inspect chest movement and listen for breath sounds, snoring, gurgling, or stridor.
ProtectionCan the patient maintain tone and manage blood, vomitus, secretions, or other material without losing the airway?
Difficult-airway riskCombine anatomy, history, and pathology. No single predictor is perfect.
UrgencyProgressive swelling, worsening stridor, weak/absent air movement, inability to protect the airway, or rapid deterioration means intervention cannot wait.
Exam move

Do not jump straight to “intubate” from one clue. First identify the immediate threat: soft-tissue obstruction, fluid/secretions, upper-airway narrowing, loss of protection, or anticipated difficult airway. Then choose the safest next airway action while advanced equipment is prepared when needed.

Gurgling: source clue for fluid or secretions in the airway.
Stridor: source clue for upper-airway narrowing; it identifies location, not the cause by itself.
Very poor/absent air movement: severe obstruction may become quieter as airflow falls.
Reduced tone can allow the tongue/soft tissue to obstruct the airway.
Blood, vomitus, or secretions can threaten patency and aspiration protection.
Practical assessment: mental status, ability to handle secretions, cough/gag effectiveness, visible material, chest movement, and air movement all matter.
Small mouth opening, micrognathia, overbite, short neck, and limited neck movement.
Source threshold: thyromental distance <6 cm is a difficult-airway predictor.
Mallampati III–IV increases concern for difficult visualization.
Prior documented difficult intubation is emphasized in the source as the strongest historical predictor.
Other source clues: obstructive sleep apnea, neck radiation/surgery/trauma, rheumatoid arthritis/cervical instability, and obesity.
Airway edema from anaphylaxis/angioedema, burns, laryngotracheobronchitis, or other inflammatory processes.
Epiglottitis, airway mass, facial/neck trauma with blood/swelling, Ludwig’s angina, and foreign body are source examples.
Urgency cue: progressive edema or distorted anatomy can turn a manageable airway into a failed airway.
I: soft palate, fauces, full uvula, pillars visible.
II: soft palate and much of uvula visible.
III: soft palate and base of uvula visible.
IV: hard palate only.
Source interpretation: classes III–IV predict increasing airway difficulty; they do not guarantee failed intubation.
Poor head/neck position can worsen soft-tissue obstruction and make visualization/ventilation harder.
The source pediatric lesson notes that a large occiput may flex an infant’s neck when lying flat; a small shoulder roll can improve neutral alignment.

Gurgling is not the same as stridor

Scenario: An obtunded patient has gurgling respirations and visible secretions in the mouth.
Primary task: recognize a difficult airway

Several predictors are present

Scenario: Mallampati IV, small mouth opening, short immobile neck, and documented prior difficult intubation.
Airway protection

An airway can be open and still be unsafe

Scenario: A deeply obtunded patient has some spontaneous air movement but repeatedly pools vomitus and secretions and cannot clear them.
Shared III.A.1: positioning

Position can create or relieve obstruction

Scenario: An infant lying flat has the large occiput pushing the head forward into neck flexion, with reduced air movement.
Immediate airway threat

Progressive swelling changes the timeline

Scenario: Facial/neck swelling is increasing, the voice is becoming hoarse, and inspiratory stridor is worsening.
