Chest Tube Troubleshooting & Emergencies

A chest tube must keep an open, one-way path out of the pleural space. A kink, clot, loose connection, empty water seal, or displaced tube can defeat that purpose. Start with breathing and circulation, then trace the drain and act under the device instructions and local emergency plan. Suspected tension pneumothorax or rapid blood loss needs immediate escalation.

Patient first, or device first?

Chest-tube rescue framework

Patient → tubing → seal → drainage → emergency response

Blocked or kinked pathway

If drainage and tidaling stop unexpectedly, assess the patient and trace the tubing for bends, dependent loops, closed clamps, or a full collection chamber. Remove a simple external kink; do not force, strip, or irrigate a tube without an order and protocol.

Broken one-way path

A disconnected tube or depleted water seal can admit air or defeat safe drainage. Assess breathing, call for help, and re-establish a sterile, one-way connection or seal using the device and facility emergency plan. Do not leave an open tube or clamp a known active air leak as a routine fix.

Tube displaced from the chest

If the tube accidentally comes out, do not push it back through the site. Cover the site with the facility-approved sterile occlusive dressing, notify the team immediately, and monitor for respiratory deterioration or tension physiology.

Bleeding or tension physiology

Rapid new bright-red output with hypotension suggests hemorrhage. Worsening unilateral chest findings, low oxygen, and low blood pressure with failed air drainage suggest tension pneumothorax. Activate emergency support; the qualified clinician provides urgent source control or decompression.

Core rule: do not let a quiet device falsely reassure you when the patient is deteriorating. Loss of drainage with dyspnea and hypotension may be an emergency, not a sign the pleural problem resolved.
Team cue: know the local disconnection and dislodgement supplies and procedure before a crisis. Avoid prolonged clamping when an air leak is present; follow specialist direction for any brief diagnostic or procedural exception.
Failure mode to safe response

Review each concept

If flow stops, assess respiratory status and follow the tubing from the patient to the collection unit. Correct an obvious kink or dependent loop without pulling on the tube, then reassess tidaling, drainage, and patient response.
A clot may block drainage, but routine stripping, forceful milking, or irrigation can harm the patient or device. Notify the responsible clinician and use the ordered, device-specific plan.
If patient tubing separates from the unit, assess the patient and call for help. Restore a sterile one-way drainage pathway promptly using the device and facility emergency protocol; ensure the new connection is secure and functioning.
A wet-seal chamber below its marked level may lose one-way protection. Check the patient, refill with sterile water to the device line per instructions, and confirm proper level, connections, and function.
Check the patient, insertion site, connections, tubing, and chamber for a new external leak while considering an ongoing pleural leak. Document the changed pattern and escalate if it persists or the patient worsens.
If the chest tube comes completely out, do not reinsert it. Apply the facility-approved sterile occlusive dressing, summon the team, and monitor oxygenation, breathing, and tension signs during reassessment.
Visible side holes outside the chest or a changed insertion depth can make the tube ineffective or allow air entry. Do not advance it blindly; secure it against further movement and seek urgent clinician assessment.
A sudden large amount of bright-red drainage, especially with hypotension or tachycardia, can signal active bleeding. Notify the emergency team, support oxygenation and circulation, and report the amount and time.
A patient with pneumothorax and failed drainage who develops severe distress, low oxygen, unilateral reduced breath sounds, and hypotension needs an emergency response. Do not wait for a routine radiograph before alerting the team for decompression.
After any correction, reassess symptoms, vital signs, oxygenation, tube connections, water seal, bubbling, tidaling, and drainage. Document what failed, what changed, and whether urgent imaging or a new tube is planned.
Integrated RTE-style emergency case

Drainage stops; the patient worsens

An adult with a chest tube for pneumothorax suddenly becomes distressed:
  • The tubing has a sharp bend and the water seal no longer moves
  • Oxygen saturation and blood pressure are falling
  • Breath sounds are reduced on the affected side
