Chest Drainage Systems: How They Work

A chest tube connects the pleural space to a collection device that allows air or fluid to leave without flowing back. Follow the path through the collection chamber, one-way water seal or dry seal, and any suction-control mechanism. Before assisting with insertion or troubleshooting, identify the specific device: dry suction can still use a water seal.

Drainage-system framework

Patient tube → collection → one-way seal → suction control

Collection

The first chamber receives and measures pleural fluid. Keep the device upright and below the insertion site, trace tubing for kinks or dependent loops, and document drainage amount and character.

One-way seal

A filled water-seal chamber or a manufacturer-designed dry seal lets air leave while limiting its return. In a water-seal unit, fill to the marked line with sterile water. A dry suction regulator does not tell you whether this seal is wet or dry.

Suction control

Wet suction control uses a water column at the ordered depth; gentle bubbling is expected in that suction-control chamber when wall suction is active. Dry suction uses a built-in regulator or dial and a device-specific indicator. Wall suction powers the system but is not itself the prescribed pleural setting.

Safety checks

Confirm device type, connections, seal fill or valve, suction setting and indicator, and unit position before and after the provider inserts the tube. Assess the patient first if the system disconnects, tips, stops draining unexpectedly, or shows a new air leak.

Core rule: first identify whether the device uses wet suction, dry suction, a water seal, or a dry seal. Check the labeled chamber and indicator; do not interpret bubbling in the suction-control chamber as an air leak in the water seal.
Team cue: keep the unit upright below the chest, preserve the one-way path, and never routinely clamp a bubbling tube connected for pneumothorax. Follow the specific manufacturer instructions and ordered drainage plan.

Review each concept

Identify the chest tube, connector, patient tubing, collection chamber, seal, and suction port. A disconnection or kink can interrupt drainage or expose the patient to air entry. Assess breathing while fixing setup problems.
This chamber collects and measures fluid. Mark level and time as ordered, note rate and appearance, and keep the unit below the insertion site. An unexpected rapid bloody output needs prompt escalation.
In a wet-seal model, sterile water to the manufacturer’s line creates a one-way valve. An unfilled or depleted chamber may not protect against backflow; add water to the proper line according to device directions.
Water may rise and fall with pressure changes during breathing. Lack of tidaling can mean lung re-expansion, blockage, or a kink; pair the observation with the patient, imaging, and system checks. Detailed air-leak interpretation comes next.
For a wet suction-control chamber, fill to the ordered water depth. When wall suction is connected, gentle bubbling in this suction chamber indicates operation; excessive bubbling wastes water and creates noise without raising the set suction.
A dry-suction model uses a dial or preset regulator. Adjust and verify the manufacturer’s suction indicator as instructed. Some dry-suction units still require a separate water-seal chamber to be filled.
A dry seal is a waterless one-way valve. Dry suction is the mechanism that regulates negative pressure. The two features are independent; read the device label rather than assuming one implies the other.
This reduces backflow and supports gravity drainage. If the unit tips over or is raised above the chest, assess the patient, restore position, and check water levels, connections, and function according to instructions.
