Thoracentesis & Pleural Procedures

Thoracentesis uses a needle or catheter to sample pleural fluid or ease breathlessness. The respiratory therapist helps the qualified operator prepare the patient, confirm the correct side and ultrasound site, monitor breathing and vital signs, and recognize when drainage should stop. Some pleural problems need an ongoing chest tube rather than a one-time aspiration.

When should drainage stop?

Pleural-procedure framework

Indication → ultrasound site → controlled removal → response

Know the indication

Diagnostic aspiration helps explain a new or unexplained effusion; therapeutic aspiration may ease dyspnea from a sizeable effusion. The provider decides whether fluid tests, symptom relief, or sustained drainage with a chest tube is the goal.

Prepare the right site

Verify consent, identity, side, imaging, bleeding risks and medications, specimen orders, and monitoring. The qualified operator uses thoracic ultrasound to locate fluid and choose an entry site; avoid relying on an old skin mark after repositioning.

Control the drainage

Watch symptoms, vital signs, oxygenation, and volume removed. Therapeutic aspiration should be slow; avoid routine wall suction or vacuum bottles. Stop promptly for chest tightness, pain, persistent cough, or increasing breathlessness, even before a planned volume is reached.

Reassess and escalate

Check breathing, breath sounds, oxygenation, blood pressure, and the puncture site afterward. New dyspnea or pleuritic pain may signal pneumothorax; new hypoxemia and cough after larger-volume drainage may raise concern for re-expansion pulmonary edema. Imaging depends on symptoms and procedure course.

Core rule: new chest tightness, pain, persistent cough, or worsening dyspnea is a stop-and-reassess signal during therapeutic aspiration, not a reason to reach a volume target.
Team cue: choose the procedure to match the problem. A diagnostic sample of a stable effusion differs from an ongoing drain for a persistent air leak, infected pleural collection, or another condition requiring continuous drainage.
Safe aspiration and chest-tube cues

Review each concept

Diagnostic thoracentesis removes enough fluid for ordered tests. Therapeutic aspiration removes fluid to relieve symptoms. The patient’s stability and reason for the effusion guide whether either is appropriate.
Real-time or immediately preprocedure thoracic ultrasound verifies the fluid, nearby lung and diaphragm, and a suitable site. Recheck after repositioning; an old mark can no longer represent the same anatomy.
Confirm identity, correct side, indication, consent, prior imaging, relevant anticoagulant or bleeding risk, allergy and local-anesthetic plan, monitoring, specimen labels, and the operator’s time-out.
Often the patient sits and leans forward with support, but positioning depends on condition and the imaged pocket. Help maintain the planned position and sterile field; notify the operator if the patient cannot tolerate it.
Common orders can include cell count, protein, lactate dehydrogenase, pH, microbiology, or cytology. Match labels to the patient and side; an infection question may need promptly processed pleural pH and cultures.
For therapeutic aspiration, use the operator’s controlled method, not rapid vacuum suction. Record volume removed and patient response. The British Thoracic Society advises no more than 1.5 L in one attempt and stopping sooner if symptoms arise.
Chest tightness, pain, repeated cough, or worsening shortness of breath can signal non-expandable lung or impending re-expansion pulmonary edema. Tell the operator and stop aspiration rather than pursuing the planned volume.
New pleuritic pain, dyspnea, falling oxygen saturation, or reduced breath sounds after thoracentesis warrants assessment. Symptom-directed imaging may be needed; a routine chest radiograph is not required after every uncomplicated aspiration.
Chest tube insertion by a qualified provider gives ongoing pleural drainage for selected pneumothoraces, infected effusions, hemothoraces, or recurrent/large collections. Prepare the ordered drainage system and assist with sterile setup and monitoring.
Document site, collected specimens, total volume, patient tolerance, vital signs, oxygen need, and any complication. Continue observation and report symptoms that persist or develop later, including worsening breathing or chest pain.
Integrated RTE-style procedure case

Symptoms before the volume target

An adult is undergoing ultrasound-guided therapeutic thoracentesis:
  • Fluid has been draining slowly; the planned volume has not been reached
  • The patient now reports chest tightness and persistent cough
  • Oxygen saturation is beginning to fall
