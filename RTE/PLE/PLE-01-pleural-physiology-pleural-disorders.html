Pleural Physiology & Pleural Disorders

The pleural space normally contains a thin film of fluid that lets the lung move against the chest wall. Air, extra fluid, blood, pus, or lymph-like fluid in that space changes lung expansion and points to a different next action. First decide whether the patient is unstable; then match findings with pneumothorax, effusion, hemothorax, empyema, or chylothorax and recommend the right evaluation.

Socratic pleural decision string

Air, fluid, or an emergency?

Pleural decision framework

Air or fluid → stability → cause → next evaluation

Air in the pleural space

Pneumothorax separates lung from chest wall and can reduce expansion. Look for sudden dyspnea, pleuritic pain, reduced breath sounds, and often hyperresonance. If rising pleural pressure also impairs circulation, suspect tension pneumothorax and mobilize immediate emergency treatment.

Fluid in the pleural space

Pleural effusion can cause dyspnea, reduced breath sounds, dullness to percussion, and reduced expansion. Bedside ultrasound helps confirm and locate fluid; a new unexplained unilateral effusion often needs ultrasound-guided diagnostic thoracentesis if clinically appropriate.

What the fluid may contain

Hemothorax is blood in the pleural space, often after trauma or a procedure. Empyema is infected pleural fluid or pus. Chylothorax is lymph-rich fluid, often from lymphatic injury or obstruction; appearance alone cannot confirm it.

Why fluid accumulated

Transudates usually reflect systemic pressure or protein-balance changes, such as heart failure or cirrhosis. Exudates suggest local pleural inflammation, infection, or malignancy. Pleural and paired serum protein and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) values allow Light’s criteria to classify the fluid.

Core rule: a patient with suspected tension physiology needs urgent decompression by the appropriate clinician; do not delay for a routine image. A stable unexplained effusion instead calls for evaluation of the fluid and its cause.
Team cue: compare onset, trauma or procedure history, blood pressure, oxygenation, percussion, breath sounds, and chest imaging or ultrasound. Reassess after the intervention—size alone does not tell you how ill the patient is.
Air, fluid, and etiology

Review each concept

A tiny amount of fluid lubricates the space between visceral and parietal pleura. The usual pressure relationship helps keep the lung expanded; new air or excess fluid can separate the pleural layers.
Air enters the pleural space and can partially collapse the lung. Sudden pleuritic pain and reduced breath sounds on one side can occur; percussion may be hyperresonant. Confirm a stable patient using the chosen imaging pathway.
Progressive distress plus low blood pressure, poor oxygenation, or signs of obstructed venous return suggests tension physiology. Alert the emergency team for immediate decompression rather than waiting for a routine chest radiograph.
Fluid outside the lung can cause shortness of breath, dullness to percussion, and reduced breath sounds. Ultrasound clarifies size and location and helps distinguish fluid from some other causes of an opaque chest.
Systemic hydrostatic or oncotic pressure changes can drive a transudate; heart failure and cirrhosis are common examples. Bilateral effusions with a clear responsive systemic cause may not require immediate sampling.
Local pleural disease increases permeability or blocks drainage. Pneumonia, malignancy, and inflammation are examples. Light’s criteria call fluid exudative if any one of the protein or LDH comparisons meets its threshold. The cutoffs are pleural-to-serum protein ratio >0.5, pleural-to-serum LDH ratio >0.6, or pleural LDH >two-thirds the upper normal limit for serum LDH.
Hemothorax is blood collecting in the pleural space, often after trauma or a procedure. New hypotension and unilateral decreased breath sounds raise concern for both respiratory compromise and blood loss.
Empyema is infected pleural fluid or frank pus, often linked to pneumonia. Fever, illness, and a complex effusion warrant prompt team evaluation; pleural sampling can guide antibiotics and whether drainage is needed.
Chylothorax is lymph-rich pleural fluid from injured or obstructed lymphatic channels, sometimes after chest surgery. It may look milky, but appearance is not definitive; pleural triglycerides and related studies support diagnosis.
A stable patient with a new, unexplained unilateral effusion often needs fluid analysis. Recommend image-guided thoracentesis by a qualified provider when appropriate; plan tests based on infection, malignancy, or other suspected cause.
