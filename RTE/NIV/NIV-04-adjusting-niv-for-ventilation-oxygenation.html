Adjusting NIV for Ventilation & Oxygenation

Adjust the variable that matches the problem. Use IPAP and the IPAP−EPAP pressure-support difference to improve ventilation. Use EPAP and FiO₂ to improve oxygenation. Whenever EPAP changes, recalculate the pressure-support difference so you do not accidentally remove inspiratory assistance.

NIV adjustment framework

Ventilation and oxygenation use different levers

Ventilation problem

Low returned VT, high PaCO₂, respiratory acidemia, or excessive work of breathing with otherwise acceptable oxygenation suggests the need for more inspiratory assistance. Increase IPAP/pressure support and reassess.

Oxygenation problem

Persistent hypoxemia with adequate ventilation points toward EPAP/recruitment and/or FiO₂ rather than simply increasing pressure support.

Mixed problem

When both oxygenation and ventilation are inadequate, both components may need adjustment—but preserve the pressure-support difference when EPAP rises.

Comfort matters

Pressure that produces an unusable mask seal, intolerance, severe leak, or worsening synchrony does not represent a successful adjustment.

Pressure support = IPAP − EPAP
Think in pairs.

EPAP supports baseline pressure/oxygenation. IPAP must sit high enough above EPAP to provide the intended inspiratory assistance.

The core source-derived rule is: when ventilation is inadequate, increase inspiratory assistance rather than treating the PaCO₂ problem with FiO₂.
EPAP increases the expiratory baseline pressure and can improve alveolar recruitment/oxygenation.
FiO₂ changes inspired oxygen concentration. Which variable is changed depends on the oxygenation problem, current settings, disease, and response.
15/5 gives 10 cmH₂O pressure support. If EPAP rises to 8 and IPAP stays 15, pressure support falls to 7.
If oxygenation requires higher EPAP but the patient still needs the same inspiratory assistance, raise IPAP appropriately to preserve the desired gap and reassess.
Your supplied exam uses a COPD patient on bilevel support with persistent hypercapnia to practice changing ventilatory support.
A PaCO₂ problem is not fixed by simply increasing FiO₂. Use the pressure-support/ventilation variable while reassessing pH, VT, total rate and work of breathing.
Your AHRC source uses cardiogenic pulmonary edema with persistent hypoxemia and normal pH/PaCO₂ as a CPAP example because ventilation is already adequate.
With CPAP there is no IPAP−EPAP pressure-support difference. Increasing CPAP changes the continuous distending pressure.
For PAP titration during sleep, gather the current pressure, residual respiratory events/AHI, oxygen saturation, leak/interface tolerance, and response to the prior step.
Interpret whether obstructive events and oxygenation are improving before making the next pressure adjustment.
The registry maps NIV-04 to both information gathering and evaluation of CPAP/NPPV titration during sleep, so the lesson includes both data collection and pressure-selection practice.
NIV-04’s registry core explicitly says to preserve adequate VT and comfort while adjusting pressures.
After increasing IPAP, reassess returned VT, RR, pH/PaCO₂, work of breathing, leak and patient comfort rather than assuming the higher setting succeeded.
A successful adjustment should move the intended physiologic problem in the right direction without creating unacceptable leak, discomfort, asynchrony, worsening hemodynamics, or airway-protection concerns.
