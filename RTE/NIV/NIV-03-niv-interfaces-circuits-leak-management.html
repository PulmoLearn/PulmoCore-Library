NIV Interfaces, Circuits & Leak Management

NIV only works when the interface and circuit deliver pressure while still allowing exhaled gas to leave safely. The RT must distinguish the system's intentional leak from an unwanted mask leak, confirm the exhalation pathway, protect the skin, and troubleshoot fit without simply tightening the mask harder.

NIV equipment framework

Interface → circuit → exhalation port → leak → skin

1. Verify the interface.

The interface must be compatible with the device/circuit, seal well enough to deliver support, and remain tolerable for the patient.

2. Verify the exhalation pathway.

Your supplied Chapter 19 NIV source describes a single-limb continuous-flow circuit with an intentional leak/exhalation port near the mask.

3. Distinguish intentional from unintentional leak.

Flow at the designed exhalation port is expected. Air escaping around the mask edge is an unintended leak that may reduce delivered pressure and disturb triggering/cycling.

4. Protect the skin.

The registry specifically requires skin protection. Reassess pressure points and fit rather than solving every leak by tightening straps.

Do not block the intentional leak.

In a single-limb leak circuit, the exhalation port is part of the breathing circuit. Blocking or removing the exhalation pathway can increase rebreathing risk.

Source boundary: the current registry requires comparison of circuit types, but the retrieved source set only provides detailed mechanics for the single-limb continuous-flow intentional-leak system. Device-specific alternate circuit arrangements should be verified with the device/manufacturer configuration rather than guessed.

Choose an interface that can deliver the ordered support, fits the patient, minimizes unintended leak, and remains tolerable enough for continued therapy.
RTE clue: a technically correct pressure setting is ineffective if the interface cannot maintain usable support.
In the single-limb system from your source, an intentional leak/exhalation port near the mask lets exhaled gas leave the circuit.
Flow from that designed port is expected and should not be mistaken for a mask-seal failure.
An unintended leak occurs outside the designed exhalation pathway, such as around the mask edge or a loose connection.
Large unintended leaks can interfere with pressure delivery, measured VT, triggering/cycling and patient comfort.
Your Chapter 19 source specifically directs learners to identify whether flow is coming from the expected exhalation port or from the mask edge.
If the exhalation pathway is absent, obstructed or incorrectly assembled, exhaled CO₂ may not wash out as intended.
Your supplied NIV source states that inadequate EPAP in its single-limb continuous-flow setup can contribute to CO₂ rebreathing.
The source gives a teaching minimum of about 4–5 cmH₂O EPAP for this context. If CO₂ is rising with EPAP 3, it teaches increasing EPAP to 4–5 and then raising IPAP as needed to preserve the pressure-support difference/VT.
Do not generalize this into a universal device rule. It is the specific board-style teaching point in the supplied source.
Inspect pressure points and skin beneath the interface regularly.
A tighter mask is not automatically a better mask. Refit/reposition the interface and protect pressure areas rather than overtightening straps to chase every small leak.
Before starting NIV, trace the circuit from device to patient and identify the intended exhalation pathway.
A safe setup requires that the interface, tubing and exhalation configuration match the device being used.
The retrieved source does not provide enough detail to teach every alternate circuit architecture, so this lesson intentionally focuses on correct recognition of the single-limb intentional-leak arrangement and device-compatibility checks.
After correcting interface/circuit problems, reassess leak, delivered support, VT when available, patient comfort, synchrony, SpO₂ and work of breathing.
A corrected setup should improve effective support—not merely make the leak display smaller.
