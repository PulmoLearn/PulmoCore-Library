Chronic Disease Self-Management & Exacerbation Prevention

Patients with a chronic lung condition need a plan for ordinary days and for days when breathing changes. A personalized action plan links symptoms to treatment steps, contacts, and emergency care. Check medication and device use, identify triggers, review recommended vaccines, and use teach-back so the patient or caregiver knows when and how to act.

When should the plan change?

Self-management framework

Baseline → warning change → planned action → reassessment

Know the usual pattern

Ask how the patient normally breathes, sleeps, moves, uses rescue treatment, and handles mucus or cough. Record baseline symptoms and the personalized action plan. A flare, or exacerbation, is a worsening beyond the usual pattern that calls for a response.

Prevent avoidable problems

Check daily treatment use and inhaler or device technique. Discuss smoke, irritants, infections, and personal triggers. Review influenza, pneumococcal, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and other vaccines according to the patient's age, risks, and current recommendations.

Act early on change

Teach the written plan for increased breathlessness, a changed cough or mucus, more rescue treatment, or falling activity tolerance. The plan specifies which prescribed treatment to use, when to contact the team, and when reassessment is needed. Do not invent a medication course that is not in the patient's plan.

Escalate danger signs

Severe breathing trouble at rest, new confusion, blue or gray lips, or symptoms that do not improve with the emergency steps in the plan require urgent emergency care. Review how to call for help and what to do while waiting. At follow-up, check what happened and update the plan.

Core teaching rule: turn a symptom change into a specific action, a contact, and a time to check the response. Have the patient explain those steps in their own words.
Plan distinction: green, yellow, and red zones are useful teaching labels, but the symptoms and medicines written in each zone must match the individual patient's condition and orders.
Self-management checkpoints

Review each concept

Document usual breathlessness, cough, sputum, activity, sleep, oxygen or monitoring values when applicable, and rescue treatment use. The patient needs a comparison point to recognize a flare early.
A written plan states what to do while well, what changes need a same-day call or prescribed step, and which signs require emergency care. Review the actual patient-specific symptoms and orders with the patient or caregiver.
Ask what treatment is taken regularly, how rescue medication is used, and whether any doses are missed. Watch inhaler or device technique; correct confusion and coordinate changes with the prescribing team.
Ask about smoke, dust, fumes, allergens, weather, infections, and any trigger the patient has noticed. Make a practical avoidance or exposure-reduction plan that fits home and work.
Check current recommendations for influenza, pneumococcal, RSV, and other applicable vaccines based on age, risk, prior doses, and current guidance. Coordinate with the clinician or pharmacist for eligibility and timing.
More breathlessness than usual, changed cough or mucus, more rescue treatment, or reduced activity can signal worsening. Compare with baseline and follow the prescribed early-response step rather than waiting for severe distress.
The patient follows the written plan's medicines and contacts. If a plan includes a rescue medicine or a clinician-directed course, teach the exact dose and timing from that plan. Do not improvise an antibiotic, steroid, or oxygen change.
Severe breathing difficulty, new confusion, blue or gray color, inability to speak due to distress, or poor response to emergency treatment requires emergency help. A routine callback is not enough for an unsafe airway or severe symptom.
Check whether the response step worked, what symptoms remain, and whether more care is needed. After a flare, review treatment technique, barriers, triggers, and the action plan with the care team.
Ask the patient or caregiver to name a personal warning sign, show the prescribed action, and explain when to call the clinic or emergency services. Correct errors and repeat until the plan is usable.
Integrated RTE-style self-management case

Respond before severe distress

An adult with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease has a written action plan:
  • Usual walking causes mild breathlessness; today a short trip across the room is harder
  • Cough and mucus have increased since yesterday
  • The patient is alert, speaking in full sentences, and not severely distressed
  • The plan lists an early treatment step and a same-day care-team contact
