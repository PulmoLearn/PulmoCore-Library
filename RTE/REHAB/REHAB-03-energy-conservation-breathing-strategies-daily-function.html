Energy Conservation, Breathing Strategies & Daily Function

Breathlessness can turn dressing, bathing, or preparing a meal into a demanding task. Teach patients to plan and pace activity, arrange the space, sit for parts of a task, and use breathing strategies during effort and recovery. Match the teaching to a real daily goal, then have the patient explain and demonstrate a workable plan.

How can the task be easier?

Daily function framework

Plan → position → pace → breathe → recover

Plan the day

Ask which activities matter most and when energy is usually best. Gather supplies before starting, spread harder tasks across the day, and schedule short rests. A pause before severe breathlessness makes the next step easier.

Modify the task

Sit to wash, dress, or prepare food when practical. Keep often-used items within easy reach, slide or carry smaller loads, and use safe aids when needed. Arrange a clear route and use prescribed oxygen during activity as ordered.

Teach breathing cues

For pursed-lip breathing, breathe in gently through the nose and exhale slowly through lightly pursed lips for longer than the inhale. Avoid forceful exhalation or breath-holding. For lifting or standing, breathe out during the harder part of the movement.

Recover and verify

If breathless, stop the task, sit or lean forward in a supported position if comfortable, relax the shoulders, and slow the exhale. Reassess before resuming. Severe or new symptoms need the patient's action plan and clinical help. Have the patient demonstrate the strategy in the actual activity.

Core teaching rule: turn a daily task into manageable steps, coordinate breathing with effort, and confirm the patient can use the plan independently.
Activity distinction: energy conservation helps patients do meaningful activities. It does not mean avoiding all exercise; a safe training plan remains part of pulmonary rehabilitation.
Daily task strategies

Review each concept

Ask what the patient wants to do more easily, such as bathing, cooking, carrying laundry, or walking into a store. The best teaching starts with the actual task and the step where breathlessness begins.
Set supplies within reach before starting and break large jobs into smaller parts. Place demanding tasks when energy is better and allow short planned rests rather than waiting until the patient is exhausted.
Start slowly, pause between steps, and avoid rushing to finish. Use a rhythm of movement and breathing that can be sustained. A rest break is part of the task plan, not a failure.
A stable chair or shower seat may make washing, dressing, or meal preparation less demanding. Keep supplies close and check that the setup is safe for the patient's balance and mobility.
Carry lighter loads, use a cart when safe, and store frequent items between shoulder and hip level. Avoid repeated overhead reaching and unnecessary trips across the room.
Breathe in gently through the nose; breathe out slowly through lightly pursed lips for longer than the inhale. Keep the neck and shoulders relaxed. Ask the patient to try it and describe when it helps.
For standing from a chair or lifting a small item, breathe in before the movement and breathe out through the hard part. Avoid breath-holding; coordinate a comfortable rhythm instead of forcing a breath.
If breathless, stop safely, rest in a supported position, relax the shoulders, and slow the exhale. Resume only when symptoms improve. Follow the patient's action plan for symptoms that do not settle or become severe.
If oxygen is ordered for activity, plan tubing and equipment so the patient can use it safely through the task. Do not change prescribed flow independently; report activity symptoms or equipment problems to the care team.
Have the patient show a safer task sequence, breathing cue, and recovery step, then explain when to seek help. Correct gaps and adapt the plan until it fits the actual home routine.
Integrated RTE-style daily-function case

Make the task workable

A patient with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease wants to do laundry:
  • The washer and dryer are across the room from the laundry basket
  • The patient rushes and carries the full basket while holding the breath
  • Breathlessness rises before the clothes are folded
  • The patient has a prescribed activity plan and no new warning symptoms
