Smoking & Vaping Cessation

Every clinical visit is a chance to ask about cigarettes, vaping, and other nicotine use without judgment. For adults who smoke, counseling paired with an appropriate cessation medication improves the chance of quitting. Help the patient choose a next step, plan for cravings, and return for follow-up. An e-cigarette being authorized for sale does not mean it is approved as a smoking-cessation treatment.

What support fits the goal?

Cessation support framework

Ask → advise → assist → arrange follow-up

Ask without judgment

Ask about smoked tobacco, vaping, dual use, past quit attempts, cravings, and readiness. Listen for what worked, what caused a return to use, and what the patient wants to change now. Document current use and offer help again at later visits.

Offer behavioral support

Give a clear, personal recommendation to quit smoking and ask permission to plan the next step. Counseling, a quitline, text support, and a quit date or gradual plan can help. Identify triggers and rehearse an alternative response to a craving.

Recognize proven medicine options

Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) includes patches, gum, and lozenges; prescription forms also exist. Varenicline and bupropion are prescription non-nicotine options. The prescriber reviews suitability, interactions, instructions, and follow-up; counseling plus medication is often more effective than either alone.

Address vaping accurately

Ask whether vaping has replaced cigarettes or occurs alongside them. No e-cigarette is approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as a quit-smoking aid, even if a tobacco product has marketing authorization. Avoid promising that vaping is safe or that dual use protects health; offer evidence-based cessation support.

Core counseling rule: make a supportive offer at every opportunity, choose an evidence-based plan with the patient, and arrange follow-up to adjust it.
Setback response: a cigarette or return to vaping is information about triggers and support needs. Review what happened, restart the plan, and keep the follow-up connection.
Cessation counseling checkpoints

Review each concept

Ask about cigarettes, cigars, vaping, and other nicotine products in neutral words. Record frequency and whether products are used together. A patient may describe vaping as quitting cigarettes while still smoking some.
Connect quitting to the patient's health goal and offer help without blame. Ask about readiness and preferences rather than repeating a warning alone.
Identify situations such as morning routines, stress, driving, or social cues. Plan a specific response for one likely trigger and check that it fits the patient's day.
Offer behavioral counseling, a quitline, or digital support. Arrange a follow-up contact and make it easy to return for help. Counseling can help patients plan, practice, and recover after a lapse.
NRT reduces withdrawal and craving. Over-the-counter choices include patch, gum, and lozenge; the patient needs correct use teaching and a suitable plan. Review medicines and individual considerations with the prescribing team.
Varenicline and bupropion are prescription medicines for smoking cessation. The clinician chooses based on history, precautions, other medicines, and patient preference; a respiratory therapist can reinforce the plan and report concerns.
For a nonpregnant adult who smokes, guidelines support behavioral help and approved medication when appropriate. Combining counseling with medication improves quit success. Pregnancy requires a separate individualized approach emphasizing behavioral support.
A vaping product allowed to be sold is not FDA-approved as a smoking-cessation aid. Ask about dual use, explain uncertainty and potential harm, and direct the patient to established quit supports without making unsupported promises.
Ask what preceded the return to smoking or vaping, what helped before, and what change would help now. A lapse does not erase prior progress. Reconnect the patient to counseling and medication review.
Ask the patient to explain the next step, craving plan, medication instructions as prescribed, and the follow-up date. Document progress, side effects, and barriers so support can be adjusted.
Integrated RTE-style cessation case

Offer a workable next step

An adult with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease wants to quit cigarettes:
  • The patient smokes daily and vapes on some days
  • A prior quit attempt ended after strong morning cravings
  • The patient asks whether a legally sold vape is an approved quit treatment
  • The patient is willing to discuss counseling and medicine
