Readiness for Ventilator Liberation

Before attempting to liberate a patient from mechanical ventilation, verify that the reason for ventilatory support has improved enough, gas exchange is acceptable on a sustainable level of support, hemodynamics are stable enough, the patient can initiate breathing, and mental status and ventilatory demand make a spontaneous-breathing assessment reasonable.

Readiness framework

Screen the whole patient before the SBT

1. Original disease is improving.

Ask why the patient was intubated and whether that process is now sufficiently improved or controlled to consider less ventilator support.

2. Gas exchange is acceptable.

Readiness means the patient is not dependent on escalating oxygenation or ventilatory support just to remain stable.

3. Hemodynamics are stable enough.

Significant shock or major instability increases the likelihood that a spontaneous-breathing assessment will be poorly tolerated.

4. Spontaneous drive is present.

The patient must be able to initiate spontaneous breathing. Heavy sedation, paralysis, or absent respiratory drive works against readiness.

5. Mental status supports participation.

The patient should be awake enough for the planned assessment and not deteriorating neurologically.

6. Ventilatory demand is reasonable.

Very high work of breathing, marked tachypnea, or an unresolved high ventilatory requirement argues against readiness.

LIB-01 asks “Should we test?”—not “Did the patient pass?”

The detailed spontaneous breathing trial belongs in LIB-02. Numeric liberation parameters belong primarily in LIB-03. Airway/cough/secretions for extubation belong in LIB-04.

Readiness domains

Review each one

A patient whose pneumonia, bronchospasm, pulmonary edema, postoperative problem, or other original cause remains uncontrolled is not ready simply because the ventilator has been on for several days.
The registry requires assessing gas exchange before liberation. This lesson intentionally avoids inventing universal FiO₂/PEEP/ABG cutoffs because the supplied registry does not provide them.
The registry explicitly includes hemodynamics. Significant instability or shock argues against proceeding with a liberation attempt until the underlying problem is addressed.
The patient should be able to participate in spontaneous breathing and should not be showing worsening neurologic status that makes a liberation assessment unsafe or uninterpretable.
A patient who is not initiating breaths because of absent drive, deep sedation, or paralysis is not ready for a spontaneous-breathing assessment.
Readiness requires that the patient's current ventilatory demand is compatible with testing spontaneous breathing. Persistently severe respiratory distress or escalating support means the cause should be addressed first.
The NBRC outline explicitly lists weaning as an example of respiratory-driven protocol use. Follow the protocol's readiness screen systematically rather than skipping criteria because one number looks favorable.
Passing a readiness screen only means it is reasonable to proceed to the next liberation step. Airway protection, cough, secretion burden, and extubation-specific issues are assessed later in the pathway.
