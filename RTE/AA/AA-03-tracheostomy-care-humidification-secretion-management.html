Routine tracheostomy assessment

Patient → tube → stoma → humidity → secretions → response

PatientRespiratory pattern, work of breathing, oxygenation, air movement, cough effectiveness, and overall tolerance.
TubePatency, securement, connection/interface, obvious obstruction, secretion burden, and whether the airway remains stable.
Stoma/skinThe NBRC specifically includes the stoma site under skin-integrity assessment; inspect for device-related injury or other concerning change.
HumidificationBecause the upper airway is bypassed, adequate airway conditioning becomes an artificial-airway safety requirement.
SecretionsTrend amount, thickness, color/character, ease of clearance, and whether the current humidity strategy is helping or worsening them.
ResponseAfter any care or interface change, reassess patency, breathing, oxygenation, secretion clearance, and the equipment setup.
Core principle

A tracheostomy patient with increasingly thick secretions may have a secretion-clearance problem, a humidification problem, or both. Correcting only the visible mucus without fixing inadequate airway conditioning may allow the problem to recur.

Your humidification source explains that an artificial airway bypasses the upper-airway conditioning system.
Dry gas reaching the trachea shifts conditioning work deeper into the bronchial tree and promotes mucosal drying and thicker secretions.
Exam move: humidification is not a comfort-only add-on in an artificial airway; it supports airway integrity and secretion management.
The source defines the HME as the only passive humidifier. It captures heat/moisture from exhaled gas and returns part of it during inspiration.
It is placed between the patient/artificial-airway connection and the breathing circuit when appropriate.
Do not choose HME when the source contraindicates it: thick/bloody secretions, hypothermia, high minute ventilation, or very small tidal volume. Do not combine it with heated humidification or aerosol therapy.
The source describes heated humidification as active humidification and the standard high-output option for artificial airways/mechanical ventilation.
It is specifically preferred when secretions are thick or bloody and when an HME is inappropriate.
Troubleshoot the whole system: water chamber, temperature, circuit, condensation, water traps, and patient interface all affect delivery.
Your lab competency connects the trach collar or T-piece to large-bore tubing and a humidified/aerosol-generating system.
The source setup includes a heater unless another humidifier is used, a temperature monitor when available, and a water trap positioned so condensate drains away from the patient.
Interface clue: if a T-piece repeatedly disconnects when a mobile tracheostomy patient turns, an existing review item supports switching to a tracheostomy collar that tolerates movement better.
Your humidification source says water traps belong at low circuit points and condensate should be drained away from the patient.
Excess water can also create back pressure in aerosol systems and alter delivered FiO₂.
Safety: do not pour circuit condensate toward the airway or back into the humidifier.
Thick secretions are a direct signal to reassess humidity adequacy in this lesson.
Assess whether the patient can cough/clear secretions and whether suction or another airway-clearance intervention is indicated.
Boundary: detailed suction-catheter sizing, pressures, open/closed technique, and suction complications are taught in SUC-02 rather than duplicated here.
The AA-03 RTE review explicitly requires preparation for, assistance with, and reassessment after provider-performed tracheotomy.
At this lesson level, that means anticipating airway/oxygenation support, appropriate tracheostomy equipment, suction and humidification needs, monitoring, and postprocedure airway assessment.
Source boundary: the retrieved PulmoLearn source set does not provide a step-by-step surgical tracheotomy technique, so this lesson does not invent one.
