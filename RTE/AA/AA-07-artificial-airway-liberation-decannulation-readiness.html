Artificial-Airway Liberation & Decannulation Readiness

Decannulation readiness is more than “the patient is breathing.” The airway must remain patent without the cuffed tracheostomy, secretions must be manageable, cough must be adequate, and the patient must tolerate progressive upper-airway use.

Decannulation-readiness framework

Patent airway + manageable secretions + adequate cough + tolerated progression

1. Cuff-deflation toleranceAA-07’s RTE review explicitly includes cuff-deflation tolerance. The patient must tolerate the airway without relying on an inflated cuff for the current tracheostomy setup.
2. Secretion burdenThis RTE review assessing secretion adequacy. Heavy or poorly managed secretions argue against removing airway access too soon.
3. Cough effectivenessthis RTE review and LIB-04 both require evaluating cough adequacy before artificial-airway removal.
4. Upper-airway patencyAA-04 establishes that speaking-valve exhalation depends on gas moving around the tube and through the upper airway.
5. Speaking-valve/capping progressionThe AA-07 RTE framework specifically names this progression as part of decannulation readiness.
6. Overall airway protectionLIB-04 adds mental status and ability to protect the airway to extubation-readiness assessment.
Do not confuse ventilatory improvement with airway readiness.

A patient may no longer need invasive ventilatory support yet still be a poor candidate for airway removal because of weak cough, heavy secretions, poor upper-airway patency, or inability to protect the airway.

Is this patient ready for decannulation?

Patient:
  • Stable respiratory pattern
  • Tolerates cuff deflation
  • Upper-airway exhalation appears patent during speaking-valve use
  • Secretions are manageable
  • Cough is effective enough to clear secretions
  • Patient can protect the airway
  • Progression toward capping has been tolerated according to the care plan
