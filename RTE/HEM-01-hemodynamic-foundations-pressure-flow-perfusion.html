Hemodynamic Foundations: Pressure, Flow & Perfusion

Hemodynamics describes how blood moves and reaches tissues. A blood-pressure number helps, but it does not tell the whole story: flow from the heart and signs of tissue perfusion matter too. Does the pressure match tissue perfusion?

Hemodynamic reasoning framework

Pressure → flow → tissue response → monitoring

Define the variables

Preload describes filling before contraction. Afterload is the load the heart pumps against. Contractility is pumping strength. Stroke volume is blood pumped per beat, and cardiac output equals stroke volume multiplied by heart rate.

Interpret pressure and flow

Mean arterial pressure (MAP) estimates average driving pressure through the arteries. A bedside estimate is (systolic pressure + two times diastolic pressure) divided by three at usual heart rates. Pressure can improve while flow or tissue perfusion remains poor.

Check tissue delivery

Cardiac output and blood oxygen content both matter for oxygen delivery. Check skin temperature, mental status, urine output, capillary refill, and lactate trend in context. A normal pulse-oximeter oxygen saturation (SpO₂) does not prove adequate circulation.

Ask for better data

Compare baseline and repeat measurements before acting. If cuff readings are unreliable or pressure changes rapidly, discuss continuous arterial-pressure monitoring. If shock remains unexplained, discuss cardiac function assessment and other testing with the team.

Core rule: read a blood-pressure trend beside perfusion findings. A normal SpO₂ shows oxygen saturation of sampled arterial blood; it does not establish adequate cardiac output or tissue oxygen delivery.
Formula cue: cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate; cardiac index = cardiac output divided by body surface area. MAP is a pressure estimate, not a direct measure of cardiac output.
Interpreting flow and pressure

Review each concept

Preload describes ventricular filling at the end of relaxation. Too little filling can lower stroke volume, while more filling does not always improve output, especially when the heart is impaired.
Afterload is the resistance or pressure the ventricle must work against to eject blood. A marked rise may reduce output from a weak ventricle.
Contractility is the heart muscle’s pumping strength at a given loading condition. Reduced contractility can lower stroke volume despite an adequate heart rate.
Stroke volume is the amount pumped per beat. Cardiac output equals stroke volume multiplied by heart rate; a faster rate does not guarantee greater output if each beat ejects much less.
Cardiac index divides cardiac output by body surface area so flow can be compared across patient sizes. It remains one measurement to interpret with perfusion and the full clinical picture.
For a usual heart rate, MAP is approximately (systolic pressure + 2 × diastolic pressure) ÷ 3. The estimate can be less reliable at unusual rates or waveforms; use a directly measured value when available.
Compare the latest cuff or arterial-line pressure with earlier values and note the patient’s baseline, interventions, fluid changes, and response. One isolated number may hide deterioration.
Check mental status, skin temperature, capillary refill, urine output, and lactate in context. These can reveal poor perfusion even if the oxygen saturation looks normal.
Oxygen delivery depends on both cardiac output and oxygen carried in blood, including hemoglobin and saturation. Increasing saturation alone may not fix poor tissue delivery from low flow.
If cuff pressure is unreliable or fluctuating rapidly, discuss an arterial line for continuous pressure with the team. If flow remains uncertain, ask whether cardiac ultrasound or output assessment is needed.

Normal saturation, falling perfusion

A ventilated adult has SpO₂ 97% on stable settings:
  • Cuff pressure has fallen from 112/72 to 84/48 mm Hg
  • The patient becomes cool, confused, and less responsive
  • Urine output has fallen and lactate is rising
