Two receptor pathways, four practical categories

ClassExamplesMechanism / role from sourceKey source caution
SABAAlbuterol, levalbuterolStimulate beta-2 receptors in airway smooth muscle → relaxation and bronchodilation. Rescue therapy for acute bronchospasm.Tachycardia, tremor, dizziness; monitor response during treatment.
LABASalmeterol, formoterolSame beta-2 target with prolonged bronchodilation; maintenance/controller use.Not acute rescue in this source. LABA monotherapy in asthma is taught as contraindicated.
SAMAIpratropiumBlocks muscarinic effects and parasympathetic bronchoconstriction.Can be used acutely, especially in COPD; dry mouth and urinary retention are source adverse effects.
LAMATiotropiumLong-acting muscarinic blockade for maintenance bronchodilation.Maintenance only in this source; not for acute bronchospasm.
Mechanism anchor

Beta-2 stimulation opens the airway. Muscarinic blockade prevents parasympathetic bronchoconstriction. Different receptor targets can be combined when clinically appropriate rather than being automatically redundant.

The source identifies SABAs as rapid bronchodilators for acute bronchospasm in asthma or COPD.
Albuterol and levalbuterol are the source examples.
Response check: reassess symptoms, wheezing/air movement, respiratory rate, oxygenation, heart rate, and—when possible—PEFR.
The source teaches LABAs as scheduled maintenance bronchodilators, not rescue medications.
Salmeterol is specifically described as slower onset with 12+ hour duration; formoterol is also listed as a LABA maintenance medication.
Asthma source rule: LABA monotherapy is not used alone in asthma in this lesson.
Source onset: within about 15 minutes; duration 4–6 hours.
Can be used in acute COPD exacerbation and is combined with albuterol in Combivent/DuoNeb-type therapy in the source material.
The source describes tiotropium as once-daily, 24+ hour maintenance therapy.
It is specifically labeled not appropriate for acute bronchospasm.
The source summary lists tachycardia, tremor/shakiness, and dizziness with SABA therapy.
The source case directs the RT to stop treatment, reassess, document, and notify the provider when significant symptomatic tachycardia develops.
Do not grade effectiveness from heart rate alone. Compare respiratory response and adverse effects together.
Dry mouth is the most emphasized common effect in the source material.
Urinary retention is also listed; the source advises attention in patients with BPH, plus caution if spray reaches the eyes in glaucoma.
PEFR is the source answer for quantifying bronchodilator effectiveness when the patient can perform the maneuver.
The quiz set also teaches that louder wheezing after treatment can reflect air moving through previously constricted airways rather than automatic worsening.
