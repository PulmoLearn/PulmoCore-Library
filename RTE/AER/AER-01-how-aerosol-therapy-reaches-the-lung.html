Particle-size map

MMAD helps predict deposition

Mass median aerodynamic diameter (MMAD) is the particle diameter at which half the aerosol mass consists of smaller particles and half consists of larger particles.

Particle sizeLikely depositionMechanism emphasized in the course
≥5 µmUpper airway / oropharynxInertial impaction
2–5 µmBronchi and bronchioles; key bronchodilator targetSedimentation
1–3 µmMore distal / alveolar depositionDiffusion contributes as particles become very small
<0.5 µmMay remain suspended and be exhaledBrownian motion / limited deposition
Common mistake

Smaller is not always better. Large particles may be lost in the mouth and upper airway, while extremely tiny particles may stay suspended and be exhaled. The useful size depends on the therapeutic target.

What changes pulmonary delivery?

Particle size is only one part of the chain

0 of 6 reviewedReview all six factors.
Large, fast-moving particles have momentum and may not follow sharp changes in airflow direction.
They strike the mouth, pharynx, and larger conducting airways instead of traveling distally.
Course implication: fast inhalation during MDI use increases turbulence and proximal impaction.
Once particles reach smaller airways, slower airflow gives gravity time to settle particles onto airway surfaces.
A breath hold increases residence time and can improve deposition by sedimentation.
Very small particles undergo Brownian motion.
As size becomes extremely small, some particles remain suspended and are exhaled rather than depositing.
Narrowed airways create more complex/turbulent flow and can reduce distal delivery.
A severely obstructed patient may not be able to perform the ideal inspiratory maneuver.
Selection rule: choose the device for the patient’s current ability, not just the medication.
Slow, controlled inhalation reduces inertial impaction for MDI delivery.
Breath holding gives smaller particles more time to settle in the lower airway.
Course distinction: MDI technique is slow/controlled; DPI technique is fast/forceful because the patient must aerosolize the powder. Device-specific technique comes in AER-02 and AER-03.
Delivery efficiency is affected by device assembly, inspiratory flow, respiratory rate, breath hold, technique, coordination, age, distress, airway anatomy, and respiratory-support equipment.
A device can generate an appropriate aerosol yet still deliver little medication to the lung if technique or patient capability is poor.
Clinical chain: target → particle size → device → patient technique → respiratory support → actual delivery → clinical response.

Scenario: A patient fires an MDI and inhales very rapidly. Much of the aerosol deposits in the mouth and pharynx.

Why does a breath hold help?

Scenario: A stable patient can comfortably hold the breath after inhaling an aerosolized dose.

Scenario: A severely distressed patient is tachypneic, has marked airway obstruction, and cannot perform a long coordinated inhalation with breath hold.
