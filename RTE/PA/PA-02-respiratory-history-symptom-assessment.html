Quick recognition

Know what to ask for

Open each area once. Focus on clues that quickly separate a routine history from a meaningful change.

0 of 7 reviewedReview all areas to continue.
  • Dyspnea
  • Cough
  • Sputum amount/character
  • Wheeze/chest tightness
  • Hemoptysis
  • New at rest
  • New productive cough
  • Increased sputum
  • Blood-streaked sputum
Define the symptom before interpreting it. A symptom name alone is rarely enough.
  • Onset
  • Duration
  • Progression
  • Triggers
  • Relieving factors
  • Treatment response
  • “Started yesterday”
  • “Worse over 3 days”
  • “Only with exertion”
  • “Albuterol no longer helps”
Acute change + worsening pattern deserves more attention than a stable chronic complaint.
  • Current/former smoking
  • Vaping or inhaled products
  • Dust, fumes, chemicals
  • Mold, animals, smoke
  • Symptoms improve on weekends
  • Started after remodeling
  • Works around dust/fumes
  • Vapes daily
Match symptom timing to exposure timing. Exposure history changes the differential but does not replace current assessment.
  • Snoring
  • Witnessed apnea
  • Morning headache
  • Daytime sleepiness
  • PAP use/adherence
  • “Stops breathing in sleep”
  • “Falls asleep during the day”
  • “Stopped using CPAP”
Recognize the sleep signal, then route detailed interpretation to the sleep lessons.
  • Walking distance
  • Stairs
  • ADLs
  • Exertional symptoms
  • Change from usual
  • “Used to walk a block”
  • “Now stops after 20 feet”
  • “Needs help dressing”
Baseline → current ability → degree of change. Functional decline is often the fastest way to uncover severity.
  • Medication frequency
  • Oxygen use
  • PAP use
  • Airway clearance
  • Reason therapy is missed
  • Controller used only PRN
  • CPAP stopped due to leak
  • Oxygen not used outside home
Ask why. A barrier may explain “nonadherence” and change what should happen next.
  • Caregiver support
  • Equipment access
  • Medication affordability
  • Transportation
  • Follow-up access
  • Cannot refill inhaler
  • No ride to appointments
  • No help managing equipment
A good treatment plan must be usable. Identify barriers before assuming the patient simply will not follow it.
Choose the next question

Which question gives the most useful new information?

Avoid collecting data just because it is available.

Patient statement: “I have COPD, so I’m always short of breath.”
PA-01 → PA-02 bridge

Read it first. Ask when it is missing or unclear.

The chart and interview are complementary, not competing sources.

Fast rule

If the answer is already clearly documented and current, use it. If it is missing, vague, outdated, or conflicting, clarify with the patient or another reliable source.

Chart: “Home oxygen 2 L/min.”
Patient: “I’ve been turning it up to 5 L/min every afternoon because I get winded.”
Integrated interview

Which finding changes the picture most?

Use the history to identify the meaningful change, not merely the longest list of facts.

A 67-year-old with chronic lung disease reports:
  • Usually walks from the bedroom to the kitchen without stopping.
  • For 2 days, must stop halfway because of dyspnea.
  • Rescue inhaler now used every 2–3 hours instead of once or twice per day.
  • Productive cough has increased from baseline.
  • No recent change in occupation or home environment.
