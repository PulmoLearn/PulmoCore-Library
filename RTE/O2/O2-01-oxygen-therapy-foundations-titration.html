Oxygen Therapy Foundations & Titration

Treat oxygen like a medication: identify the need, choose a target, titrate to physiologic response, reassess, and avoid unnecessary excess.

What should the respiratory therapist do next?

Use the patient’s oxygenation, symptoms, support level, target, and trend—not a single saturation number in isolation.

Core titration framework

Oxygen has a target—not a goal of “as high as possible”

The source review emphasizes using the lowest effective oxygen dose, correcting hypoxemia, and titrating down once the patient is stable.

1. Identify the needUse SpO₂, PaO₂ when available, work of breathing, mental status, color/perfusion, heart rate, and the overall clinical picture.
2. Verify the targetUse the ordered or protocol-defined oxygen goal. Some patient groups require a narrower target; those disease-specific rules are taught in later lessons.
3. Titrate to effectIf oxygenation is below target, increase support according to the device, protocol, and clinical urgency. If above target and stable, reduce excess oxygen rather than leaving it unnecessarily high.
4. Reassess after every changeCheck oxygenation, respiratory effort, mental status, heart rate, tolerance, and whether the device is functioning as intended.
Exam move

Do not chase a perfect saturation. Correct hypoxemia, achieve the ordered target, then avoid more oxygen than the patient needs.

What tells you oxygen is needed—or needs adjustment?

  • Cyanosis

  • Confusion

  • Lethargy or sleepiness
Do not wait for every sign to appear. Assess the whole patient and verify that the oxygen measurement itself is reliable.
  • Verify the reading
  • Check the oxygen source/device
  • Confirm current flow/FiO₂
  • Assess work of breathing
  • Increase oxygen according to the device/protocol and urgency
  • Reassess the response promptly

  • Patient is clinically stable
  • Oxygenation remains above the ordered target
  • Reduce oxygen in controlled steps
  • Reassess after each change
  • Avoid abrupt removal in a patient still dependent on support
Oxygen should be kept at the lowest therapeutic dose that achieves the clinical goal.
  • Baseline SpO₂ and symptoms
  • Activity level or exercise test conditions
  • Current device and flow/FiO₂
  • Lowest SpO₂ during activity
  • Dyspnea/work of breathing
  • Recovery after exercise
  • Response to oxygen adjustment
An oxygen requirement can change with activity. Titrate and reassess under the condition in which desaturation occurs.
  • Oxygen toxicity with prolonged high FiO₂ exposure
  • Absorption atelectasis with rapid/high oxygen exposure
  • Fire risk
  • No smoking/open flame
  • Keep ignition sources away
  • Teach patient/family oxygen-safety rules

respiratory-driven protocol

Apply the protocol instead of treating the number in isolation

Protocol: Adjust oxygen to keep SpO₂ within the prescribed patient-specific target; reassess after changes and reduce oxygen when the patient is stable above target.

Patient: Stable, no distress, SpO₂ persistently above target on the current setting.
minimize hypoxemia

Correct the immediate problem, then reassess

Scenario: A patient develops acute respiratory distress with a verified SpO₂ well below the ordered target.
Integrated RTE-style titration

Choose the safest oxygen decision

Patient:
  • Oxygen target is documented in the respiratory protocol
  • Current SpO₂ is above that target on supplemental oxygen
  • Respiratory rate and work of breathing are improved
  • Mental status is normal
  • No recent desaturation with routine activity
