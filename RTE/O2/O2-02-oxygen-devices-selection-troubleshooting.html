Low-flow vs high-flow

“Low-flow” does not mean a low number on the flowmeter

The classification depends on whether the system meets the patient’s inspiratory flow demand.

Low-flow systemDoes not meet the patient’s full inspiratory demand. Room air is entrained, so delivered FiO₂ is variable and changes with breathing pattern.
High-flow systemMeets or exceeds inspiratory demand and provides a more stable, predictable FiO₂. A Venturi device can be high-flow even when its oxygen input is only a few L/min because entrained air raises total flow.
RTE trap

A nasal cannula at 6 L/min is still a low-flow device. Device classification is about meeting inspiratory demand—not the number printed on the flowmeter.

  • Run at least 5 L/min
  • Too little flow allows exhaled CO₂ to accumulate in mask dead space
  • Verify adequate flow

If a simple mask is running at 3 L/min, fix the flow/device problem before interpreting treatment failure.
  • About 40–70% FiO₂ in the source lesson
  • 10–15 L/min
  • Reservoir bag remains inflated during inspiration
  • If bag collapses, increase flow
  • Verify the bag is patent
  • Confirm the correct valve configuration
Partial rebreather = reservoir bag without the one-way valve arrangement of a nonrebreather.
  • High-concentration low-flow reservoir system
  • Source lesson: ≈60–80%+ FiO₂ at 10–15+ L/min
  • Reservoir should remain inflated during inspiration
  • Verify oxygen source and flow
  • Check one-way valves
  • Keep reservoir inflated
  • Improve mask seal
A collapsing reservoir bag means the patient is drawing gas faster than the bag is being refilled—correct the flow/setup immediately.
  • High-flow air-entrainment device
  • Delivers a precise FiO₂ determined by jet/entrainment geometry
  • Changing oxygen input changes total flow, not the selected FiO₂ when used correctly
  • Do not block entrainment ports
  • Use the correct adapter/setting
  • Verify adequate total flow for patient demand
If precise controlled FiO₂ is needed, the Venturi principle is the key advantage.
  • Interface positioned over tracheostomy
  • Tubing connected and patent
  • Flow adequate
  • Humidification appropriate for the artificial airway
  • Dislodged interface
  • Kinked tubing
  • Source failure
  • Secretion obstruction may mimic “oxygen failure”
Detailed tracheostomy care and humidification are taught in the airway/humidification pathway; here the focus is confirming the oxygen interface is actually delivering gas to the airway.
  • Thorpe tube must be upright
  • Read flow at the center of the float ball
  • Bourdon gauge can function in different orientations but back-pressure may create a false-high reading
  • Hissing suggests leak/connection/O-ring problem
  • No flow: check source pressure → control → regulator → tubing → interface
  • If wall gas fails, move dependent patient to a reliable backup source
A blender combines compressed air and oxygen to provide a selected FiO₂. Verify both gas sources are connected and available before trusting the blended output.
