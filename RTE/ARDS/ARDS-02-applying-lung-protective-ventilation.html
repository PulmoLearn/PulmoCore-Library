Applying Lung-Protective Ventilation in ARDS

Mechanical ventilation can support an injured lung and can also stretch it too far. In acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), set a small tidal volume (VT) from predicted body weight (PBW), which is based on height and sex, and check the end-inspiratory plateau pressure. Reassess oxygenation, ventilation, pH, and the patient’s condition after each adjustment.

How do we protect the injured lung?

Protect the lung during support

PBW → VT → plateau → reassess

Base VT on predicted body weight

PBW reflects expected lung size from height and sex; actual body weight is not the dosing weight for VT. A common starting target is about 6 mL/kg PBW, within a guideline range of 4–8 mL/kg PBW.

Limit lung-distending pressure

Measure plateau pressure with an inspiratory hold when the patient is passive. Aim below 30 cm H₂O. A high plateau pressure can signal excessive lung stretch; check VT, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), compliance, and technique.

Accept some carbon dioxide with limits

A smaller VT may raise arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO₂). Permissive hypercapnia avoids unsafe larger breaths, but pH, patient response, and contraindications matter. Reassess significant acidosis rather than simply accepting any value.

Recheck oxygenation and ventilation

Review oxygen saturation, arterial blood gas, plateau pressure, delivered VT, respiratory rate, PEEP, and patient-ventilator interaction after changes. Adjust the care plan if oxygenation, pressure, or acid-base status remains unsafe.

Core rule: use 4–8 mL/kg PBW and keep plateau pressure below 30 cm H₂O. Start near 6 mL/kg and adjust to safe pressure, oxygenation, ventilation, and clinical response.
Math cue: with PBW 70 kg, 6 mL/kg gives VT 420 mL. The 4–8 mL/kg range is 280–560 mL; actual scale weight does not change these numbers.
Lung-protective decisions

Review each concept

Lung size tracks body height and sex more closely than current scale weight. Calculate PBW from the accepted height-based equation; using actual weight in obesity can deliver an overly large VT.
A typical starting point is 6 mL/kg PBW within the 4–8 mL/kg guideline range. For PBW 60 kg, 6 mL/kg gives 360 mL. Check the delivered volume, not only the set value.
An inspiratory hold with no flow estimates pressure at the end of inspiration when the patient is passive. The lung-protective target is plateau pressure below 30 cm H₂O.
When plateau pressure exceeds the limit, review measurement quality, VT, PEEP, and compliance. A smaller VT within the protective range may lower stretch; reassess gases and oxygenation after the change.
Peak pressure includes resistance in the airway and circuit. If peak rises but plateau remains acceptable, check secretions, tube kinks, bronchospasm, and flow before assuming lung stretch alone.
Lower VT can reduce carbon dioxide clearance. A moderate rise may be accepted to protect the lung if pH and clinical status remain acceptable under the care plan. Severe acidosis needs prompt review.
If carbon dioxide rises and pH falls, review respiratory rate and ventilator timing within safe limits and check for air trapping. Raising VT above the protective plan is not the automatic first response.
Fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) and PEEP can support oxygenation while VT remains protective. Reassess blood pressure, oxygen level, and lung response when PEEP changes.
Double triggering or strong patient effort can increase delivered volume or pressure even if the set VT looks protective. Check waveforms, comfort, synchrony, and actual delivered breaths.
Record PBW, set and delivered VT, plateau pressure, PEEP, FiO₂, gas results, and timing. A safer pressure with worsening pH or oxygenation requires another evaluation rather than an assumption of success.
