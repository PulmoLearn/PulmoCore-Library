Recognizing ARDS & Acute Lung Injury Physiology

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is rapid, widespread inflammatory lung injury after a trigger such as pneumonia, sepsis, or aspiration. Leaky lung vessels fill air spaces with fluid, making the lungs stiff and oxygen transfer poor. Read the timing, imaging, oxygenation, support level, and whether heart failure or fluid overload fully explains the findings. The arterial oxygen tension (PaO₂) divided by fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) helps classify severity on qualifying support such as positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP).

Does the pattern fit ARDS?

Apply the ARDS definition

Timing → opacities → oxygenation → cause

Find the acute injury

ARDS develops within about one week of a known insult or new respiratory symptoms. Pneumonia, sepsis, aspiration, trauma, and transfusion are possible triggers. Inflammation increases vessel leak into the air spaces.

Check bilateral lung findings

New bilateral opacities on chest radiograph or computed tomography support the pattern when not fully explained by effusions, collapse, or nodules. The global definition also allows lung ultrasound when performed by a trained operator.

Assess oxygenation and its cause

Divide arterial oxygen tension (PaO₂) by fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂). A ratio at or below 300 supports ARDS hypoxemia in the proper setting. Edema should not be primarily due to heart failure or fluid overload; both processes may coexist.

Use the applicable definition

The global definition includes nonintubated adults on high-flow nasal oxygen at 30 L/min or more and can use an oxygen saturation (SpO₂)/FiO₂ ratio at or below 315 if SpO₂ is 97% or less. For intubated severity, mild is ratio >200–300, moderate >100–200, severe ≤100 with positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) at least 5 cm H₂O.

Core rule: no single low oxygen value diagnoses ARDS. Match an acute trigger, bilateral findings, impaired oxygenation on qualifying support, and edema not primarily explained by fluid overload or heart failure.
Severity cue: PaO₂ 75 mm Hg on FiO₂ 0.50 gives a ratio of 150. In an intubated patient with PEEP at least 5 cm H₂O, that is moderate ARDS.
Acute diffuse lung injury

Review each concept

ARDS can follow pneumonia, sepsis, aspiration, major trauma, or transfusion. New or worsening hypoxemic failure within one week of the insult or symptom onset fits the acute timing criterion.
Inflammation damages the alveolar-capillary barrier, letting fluid and proteins enter air spaces. The resulting edema is not primarily a heart-pump or fluid-overload problem.
Fluid-filled or collapsed air spaces can receive blood but little fresh air. This shunt contributes to low oxygen that may respond poorly to more oxygen alone.
Injured and fluid-filled lung units expand less easily. Lower compliance means stiffer lungs and more pressure may be needed for a given breath. Ventilation decisions belong in the next lesson.
Bilateral opacities may be seen on radiograph or computed tomography. Trained lung ultrasound can also support the global definition. An isolated unilateral opacity alone does not satisfy the bilateral criterion.
PaO₂/FiO₂ uses FiO₂ as a decimal: PaO₂ 80 on FiO₂ 0.40 gives 200. Always note support and measurement timing, not just the number.
The global definition can include patients on high-flow nasal oxygen at least 30 L/min. A PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio ≤300 or SpO₂/FiO₂ ratio ≤315 can identify hypoxemia, but SpO₂ must be ≤97% for that saturation-based ratio.
With PEEP of at least 5 cm H₂O, a PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio >200–300 is mild, >100–200 moderate, and ≤100 severe. Report the ratio and support when giving a severity category.
Heart failure and fluid overload can produce bilateral opacities. Assess fluid balance, cardiac findings, imaging, and the insult; ARDS remains possible if these do not fully account for the edema.
Communicate the trigger, onset, bilateral imaging, oxygenation ratio, level of support, and alternate explanations. Reassess severity as oxygen and ventilation settings change.

Calculate before you classify

  • Chest radiograph shows new bilateral opacities; cardiac and fluid findings do not fully explain them
  • The patient is now intubated with PEEP 8 cm H₂O
  • Arterial PaO₂ is 72 mm Hg on FiO₂ 0.60
  • The patient’s PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio is 120
