Prone Positioning & Refractory Hypoxemia

In acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), turning an intubated patient face down can spread ventilation more evenly and improve blood-flow matching. Consider prone positioning when oxygen remains poor after lung-protective settings and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) have been reviewed. Use a trained team, protect the airway and pressure points, and reassess the response. The arterial oxygen tension (PaO₂) divided by inspired oxygen fraction (FiO₂) gauges severity.

When and how should we prone?

Prone with a safety plan

Identify → prepare → turn → monitor

Decide who may benefit

For intubated ARDS with a low arterial oxygen tension to inspired oxygen ratio (PaO₂/FiO₂) after low tidal volume and PEEP optimization, prone positioning can improve oxygenation. European guidance highlights a ratio below 150; U.S. guidance strongly recommends prone positioning for severe ARDS.

Use a coordinated long session

Proning is a team procedure. Secure the endotracheal tube and lines, plan the turn, protect eyes and skin, and arrange safe access to the airway. U.S. guidance recommends more than 12 hours per day in severe ARDS; European guidance favors 16-hour sessions when criteria are met.

Reassess benefit and harm

Compare oxygen saturation, arterial gas and FiO₂, ventilator pressures, blood pressure, tube position, skin, and eye protection before and after turning. Continue lung-protective ventilation while prone; do not judge success from one saturation value.

Separate position from pressure maneuvers

Proning may reopen dependent regions through positioning. A recruitment maneuver instead briefly raises ventilator pressure to open lung units. Current guidance advises against prolonged high-pressure recruitment maneuvers in moderate to severe ARDS because harm can outweigh benefit.

Core rule: optimize protective ventilation, identify persistent hypoxemia, use a trained team for a prolonged prone session when indicated, and reassess oxygenation and complications throughout.
Guideline cue: prone positioning is a position-based strategy. Do not treat it as permission to apply a prolonged high-pressure recruitment maneuver, which current ARDS guidance recommends against.
Position and response

Review each concept

Oxygen remains low despite appropriately adjusted inspired oxygen, PEEP, and lung-protective ventilation. Recheck the airway, equipment, and reversible causes before calling it refractory.
PaO₂/FiO₂ uses FiO₂ as a decimal. PaO₂ 70 mm Hg on FiO₂ 0.70 gives a ratio of 100. State the PEEP and timing when interpreting severity and considering proning.
Positioning can improve ventilation in previously compressed back regions, distribute lung stress more evenly, and improve ventilation-to-blood-flow matching. These changes may improve oxygenation.
Evidence-based proning is a sustained intervention, not a brief turn to test the oxygen number. Follow the team protocol for timing; severe ARDS guidance calls for more than 12 hours per day, often about 16 hours.
A trained team assigns roles, secures tube and lines, confirms the ventilator circuit and suction access, and watches for accidental extubation or line loss during the turn.
Pad pressure points, protect the face and eyes, manage moisture, and reposition the head and limbs according to protocol. Document skin and eye checks during the session.
Turning and PEEP may affect blood pressure and venous return. Check pressure, pulse, perfusion, and medication or support needs before, during, and after positioning.
Compare SpO₂, PaO₂/FiO₂, FiO₂ need, ventilator pressures, and gas results over time. The team also weighs airway safety, skin, and hemodynamic effects.
A ventilator recruitment maneuver uses a temporary pressure increase to open lung units. It is separate from prone positioning. Prolonged high-pressure maneuvers are discouraged in moderate to severe ARDS.
Maintain protective tidal volume and pressure limits while prone. If oxygenation remains poor or complications develop, reassess causes and escalate within the ARDS care pathway rather than repeating unsafe pressure maneuvers.

Persistent hypoxemia after optimization

An adult with ARDS remains severely hypoxemic despite protective ventilation:
  • PaO₂ is 68 mm Hg on FiO₂ 0.80, giving a ratio of 85
  • PEEP is 12 cm H₂O; tidal volume and plateau pressure are in a protective range
  • The endotracheal tube and lines can be secured; the trained team is available
  • Blood pressure is supported and stable enough for a planned turn
