Chest-Wall, Obesity & Other Extrapulmonary Restriction

A low lung volume can result from limited expansion of the chest, abdomen, or breathing muscles even when the lung tissue is not the main problem. Read the history, examination, lung volumes, imaging, and ventilation data together. Kyphoscoliosis and severe obesity can increase breathing work and cause sleep or daytime hypoventilation.

Where is the restriction?

Interpret the patient record

Mechanics → volumes → ventilation

Identify the outside-lung limit

Kyphoscoliosis limits thoracic cage motion. Severe obesity can load the chest wall and diaphragm, especially when lying down. Neuromuscular weakness can also limit expansion. Examine body shape, posture, muscle function, and imaging.

Confirm a restrictive pattern

A low forced vital capacity (FVC) with a preserved forced expiratory volume in one second to FVC ratio suggests restriction but does not prove it. A low total lung capacity (TLC) confirms a restrictive ventilatory impairment.

Look for hypoventilation

Review sleep symptoms, morning headache, daytime sleepiness, and arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO₂). Obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS) means obesity, sleep-disordered breathing, and awake PaCO₂ at least 45 mm Hg after excluding other causes.

Interpret gas transfer with care

Diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) may be low with reduced lung volume even when lung tissue is relatively preserved. Read its components, test quality, imaging, and context; no single DLCO result proves the cause.

Core rule: use low TLC to confirm restriction; use history and imaging to locate the cause; use carbon dioxide and sleep data to check whether the patient also hypoventilates.
Chart cue: obesity alone does not establish OHS. Awake hypercapnia requires confirmation and evaluation for other causes; a single low FVC does not prove a low TLC.
Outside-lung restriction

Review each concept

A marked sideways curve with abnormal forward-backward spine curvature can restrict thoracic cage movement. Look for reduced volumes, exertional limitation, sleep-related hypoventilation, and later daytime hypercapnia.
Excess chest and abdominal load reduces resting lung volumes and may increase breathing work, especially when supine. The degree of mechanical restriction varies; body size alone does not diagnose a ventilatory disorder.
A low FVC with a normal or high expiratory ratio suggests restriction but can also reflect air trapping, poor effort, or other causes. Obtain TLC when feasible to confirm a restrictive ventilatory impairment.
TLC is the amount of air in the lungs after the largest inhalation. A measured value below the lower limit of normal confirms restriction; then use imaging, examination, strength, and history to find its source.
Low DLCO can result from reduced accessible lung volume or diseased gas-exchange tissue. A relatively preserved transfer coefficient with low accessible alveolar volume can suggest incomplete expansion, but it is not a stand-alone diagnosis.
OHS includes a body mass index of at least 30 kg/m², sleep-disordered breathing, and awake PaCO₂ of at least 45 mm Hg after other causes of hypoventilation are excluded. Obstructive sleep apnea alone is not enough.
When OHS suspicion is high, measure awake PaCO₂ with an arterial blood gas. For lower suspicion in an obese patient with sleep-disordered breathing, serum bicarbonate below 27 mmol/L can help exclude OHS; a higher value calls for PaCO₂ testing.
Orthopnea, morning headache, poor sleep, and daytime drowsiness may suggest hypoventilation in chest-wall or obesity-related disease. Evaluate sleep and ventilation rather than using resting pulse oximetry alone.
Chronic hypercapnic failure from chest-wall restriction may warrant noninvasive ventilation after full assessment. In stable OHS with severe obstructive sleep apnea, continuous positive airway pressure is often the first treatment; reassess response.
Compare TLC, vital capacity, symptoms, sleep data, carbon dioxide, and functional change. New drowsiness or rising PaCO₂ calls for prompt evaluation even when resting oxygen saturation seems acceptable.

Low volumes with a chest-wall clue

An adult with severe kyphoscoliosis reports morning headaches and increasing fatigue:
  • TLC is below the lower limit of normal; FVC is low with a preserved expiratory ratio
  • Chest imaging shows a marked spine and rib-cage deformity without diffuse interstitial change
  • Awake PaCO₂ is 51 mm Hg; resting oxygen saturation is 94%
  • Overnight monitoring suggests sleep-related hypoventilation
