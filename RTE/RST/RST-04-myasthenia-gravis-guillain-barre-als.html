Myasthenia Gravis, Guillain–Barré & ALS

These disorders can all weaken breathing and cough, but their history and pattern differ. Myasthenia gravis (MG) causes fluctuating, often fatigable weakness. Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) usually develops over days to weeks and may disturb heart rate or blood pressure. Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) usually progresses over months. Read the chart, examine the trend, and identify threats to ventilation, cough, and swallowing.

Which pattern fits the chart?

Interpret the patient record

Tempo → pattern → respiratory risk

MG: fluctuating, fatigable weakness

Look for drooping eyelids, double vision, nasal speech, chewing or swallowing difficulty, and weakness that worsens with use. Respiratory and bulbar decline can signal myasthenic crisis. Ask what changed since the last assessment.

GBS: rapid weakness and autonomic change

Look for weakness that spreads over days, reduced or absent reflexes, and possible numbness or tingling. Track neck strength, vital capacity (VC), cough, swallowing, heart rhythm, and blood pressure. A patient may worsen quickly.

ALS: progressive motor loss

Look for months of worsening weakness, muscle wasting or twitching, stiff movement, and speech or swallow change. Sensory loss is usually not a leading feature. Check for orthopnea, morning headache, weak cough, and sleep-related hypoventilation.

Compare function, not labels alone

Gather onset, examination, prior tests, current vital capacity, inspiratory strength, cough, bulbar symptoms, carbon dioxide when indicated, and serial change. A normal resting saturation cannot exclude early ventilatory failure.

Core rule: recognize the likely pattern, then decide whether the patient is losing ventilation, cough effectiveness, or airway protection. Escalate a fast decline even if oxygen saturation is still normal.
Chart cue: a vital capacity (VC) that falls over hours matters more than a single stable-looking oxygen reading. Compare repeatable measures with the patient’s speech, swallow, work of breathing, and cough.
Three disease patterns

Review each concept

MG commonly causes fluctuating weakness that worsens with repeated use. Drooping eyelids, double vision, nasal speech, and tired chewing can accompany respiratory muscle weakness. A diagnosis in the history helps, but current function sets urgency.
Rapidly worsening weakness with poor breathing, weak cough, choking, or pooled secretions may be a crisis. Review serial vital capacity and inspiratory strength along with clinical signs; arrange immediate airway-capable assessment.
GBS commonly causes fairly rapid, often ascending weakness with reduced reflexes after an infection. Sensory symptoms can occur. Facial, neck, breathing, and swallowing weakness can develop during the acute course.
GBS can affect automatic control of heart rate and blood pressure. New rhythm changes, swings in blood pressure, or marked pulse changes add urgency to respiratory and neurologic monitoring.
ALS usually causes steadily progressive motor weakness over months. Muscle wasting or twitching and signs of increased muscle tone can coexist. Prominent sensory loss suggests looking for another or additional cause.
Orthopnea, disrupted sleep, morning headache, and daytime sleepiness can point to nocturnal hypoventilation. Check respiratory function, carbon dioxide when indicated, and the adequacy of cough and secretion clearance.
Slurred or nasal speech, repeated choking, trouble swallowing, drooling, and weak cough can signal poor airway protection in any of these diseases. These signs call for direct assessment alongside volume and pressure tests.
Trend VC, maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP), cough quality, and other available measures using the same technique when possible. A falling VC or a less negative MIP indicates worsening strength; interpret with the whole patient.
Pulse oximetry reflects oxygenation and may remain normal while respiratory muscles weaken. New shallow breathing, sleep symptoms, rising carbon dioxide, or a poor cough can reveal problems sooner.
Use onset, tempo, reflex and sensory findings, fatigability, bulbar signs, respiratory measures, and autonomic data to describe the likely pattern. Communicate uncertainty and urgent change rather than relying on one clue.
Integrated RTE-style data case

Fast weakness and a dangerous trend

An adult presents one week after a stomach illness:
  • Leg weakness moved upward over three days; reflexes are reduced
  • VC falls from 26 to 17 mL/kg over six hours, with a weaker cough
  • Speech becomes nasal; pulse swings between 55 and 125/min
  • Resting oxygen saturation remains 97% on room air
