Restrictive Physiology: Intrinsic vs Extrinsic

Restriction means the lungs cannot expand to their expected total size. A measured low total lung capacity confirms it. The next question is where the limit comes from: diseased lung tissue itself or a problem outside the lung, such as chest-wall mechanics, obesity, or weak breathing muscles. Combine lung volumes, gas transfer, imaging, and the patient story.

Restriction localization framework

FVC clue → TLC proof → source of limitation

Confirm true restriction

Spirometry may show a low forced vital capacity (FVC) with a normal or high forced expiratory volume in one second / FVC ratio (FEV1/FVC). That pattern suggests restriction but cannot prove it. Total lung capacity (TLC) below the lower limit of normal on measured lung volumes confirms restriction.

Look inside the lung

Intrinsic or parenchymal disease can make lung tissue stiff or reduce functioning air sacs. Imaging may show interstitial changes, and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO), corrected and interpreted appropriately, is often low. These are clues, not a single diagnostic signature.

Look beyond the lung tissue

Chest-wall limitation, obesity, pleural disease, or neuromuscular weakness can prevent normal expansion while lung tissue is comparatively preserved. A relatively normal lung image, weakness or deformity on exam, and a preserved DLCO can support an extrinsic source.

Integrate the full record

Review maneuver quality, hemoglobin, imaging, body habitus, muscle strength, cough, and symptoms. DLCO can be abnormal for other reasons and may fall with reduced accessible lung volume; never assign etiology from DLCO alone.

Core rule: low FVC is a clue, not proof of restriction. Confirm low TLC first; then use gas transfer, imaging, mechanics, and history to distinguish lung-tissue disease from an extrinsic expansion problem.
Chart cue: an obstructed patient can also have low FVC from air trapping while TLC is normal or high. A true mixed pattern needs measured low TLC plus a low FEV1/FVC.
Finding the source of low volume

Review each concept

Forced vital capacity is the total volume forced out after a full inhale. A low result can suggest restriction, but poor effort, early stopping, or gas trapping can also make it small.
Total lung capacity (TLC) measures gas at full inflation. A TLC below the lower limit of normal confirms a restrictive ventilatory defect; interpret a reliable reference range and test quality.
A normal or high FEV1/FVC is common in uncomplicated restriction because FEV1 and FVC fall together. A low ratio points to obstruction and requires further lung-volume interpretation if FVC is also low.
Inflamed or scarred parenchyma can make lungs stiff and lower expansion. Interstitial imaging changes, crackles, exertional desaturation, and reduced gas transfer can support this source.
Chest-wall deformity, pleural restriction, obesity, or breathing muscle weakness can reduce lung expansion without primary interstitial disease. The exact lung-volume pattern varies with the cause.
Diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) tests transfer of gas from alveoli to blood. A low value can fit parenchymal disease but also anemia, vascular disease, emphysema, or low sampled lung volume; review hemoglobin and the full pattern.
Interstitial reticulation or scarring can support intrinsic lung disease. A relatively clear lung image with severe scoliosis or weak breathing muscles makes an extrinsic explanation more plausible, but imaging alone is not a complete test.
Weak inspiratory muscles may lower vital capacity; a weak cough may impair secretion clearance. Compare upright and supine vital capacity or inspiratory pressure when clinically indicated rather than relying only on a resting saturation.
Obesity often reduces expiratory reserve volume and resting end-expiratory volume; a clearly low TLC is more likely with marked obesity or other contributors. Do not label every low FVC in obesity confirmed restriction.
If FVC is low but TLC is normal, do not call confirmed restriction. If TLC is low, examine DLCO, imaging, strength, pleura, and clinical history before deciding whether the cause is intrinsic, extrinsic, or mixed.

A low TLC, but clear lung tissue

An adult has exertional dyspnea and marked thoracic scoliosis:
  • Quality spirometry shows low FVC and an FEV1/FVC above its lower limit
  • Measured TLC is below its lower limit of normal
  • Hemoglobin-adjusted DLCO is within its reference range
  • CT shows chest-wall deformity without an interstitial pattern
