Interstitial & Parenchymal Restrictive Disease

Interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) can inflame or scar the lung tissue around air sacs. Stiffer lungs take more effort to inflate, and damaged gas-transfer surfaces may lower oxygen during activity. Put symptoms, measured lung volumes, diffusing capacity, imaging, and exercise oxygenation together; then look at how those findings change over time.

Parenchymal assessment framework

Symptoms → volumes → images → trajectory

Read the clinical pattern

Progressive exertional breathlessness, dry cough, and fine inspiratory crackles may point toward ILD, but these findings are not specific. Ask about work and home exposures, medications, connective-tissue symptoms, and competing cardiac or pulmonary problems.

Confirm and characterize physiology

A low measured total lung capacity (TLC) confirms restriction. Forced vital capacity (FVC) may also be low, often with a normal or high forced expiratory volume in one second / FVC ratio (FEV1/FVC). A low diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) supports impaired gas transfer but has other causes.

Pair imaging and oxygen data

High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) may show reticulation, ground-glass change, or fibrosis; the pattern helps specialists narrow the cause. Pulse oximetry at rest and during a supervised walk can expose exertional hypoxemia despite a normal resting reading.

Judge trajectory and support needs

Compare serial symptoms, FVC, DLCO, imaging, and walk oxygenation. Worsening across these domains supports progression and prompts specialist review. Confirm oxygen needs with appropriate testing; oxygen and rehabilitation address functional consequences while disease-specific decisions depend on the diagnosis.

Core rule: a low TLC establishes restriction; reduced DLCO, interstitial findings on HRCT, and exertional desaturation together favor intrinsic lung-tissue disease. Each result needs its own quality check and clinical context.
Chart cue: a resting oxygen saturation within the usual range does not exclude a significant drop with walking. Repeat comparable measurements and distinguish chronic progression from an acute new event.
Inside the lung tissue

Review each concept

The parenchyma includes alveoli and nearby support tissue. Inflammation or fibrosis can make lung tissue stiff and reduce the area or efficiency available for oxygen transfer.
Compliance describes how much volume changes for a given change in pressure. A stiff lung has lower compliance: a deeper breath requires more pressure and effort, so patients often breathe faster and shallower.
Ask about occupational dust, birds or mold, medications, and autoimmune symptoms, along with prior imaging and diagnoses. ILD describes a group of diseases, not one cause with one treatment.
Spirometry may reveal a low FVC with a normal or high FEV1/FVC. Measured TLC below its reference limit confirms restriction; FVC alone cannot separate true restriction from poor effort or gas trapping.
DLCO estimates transfer of test gas from alveoli to blood. It can fall in parenchymal disease but also with anemia, pulmonary vascular disease, or emphysema; compare hemoglobin and imaging.
Fine reticular lines, ground-glass change, and honeycombing are different imaging clues. A pattern may guide diagnosis, but the scan should be read with exposures, physiology, and specialist assessment.
In ILD, resting saturation may be acceptable while walking reveals hypoxemia. Document the activity, oxygen device or room air, starting value, low value, symptoms, and recovery in a supervised assessment.
Severe chronic resting or severe exertional hypoxemia may support supplemental oxygen under clinical guidance. Match the prescription to tested conditions, titrate and reassess, and teach safe device use.
A lower FVC or DLCO, more breathlessness, increased fibrotic imaging change, or worse exercise oxygenation can indicate progression. Consider test variation and another cause of sudden decline before assigning a trend.
A new sharp fall in oxygenation may reflect an acute ILD event, infection, heart failure, or pulmonary embolism. Provide appropriate oxygen, assess urgently, and avoid assuming it is only the usual chronic restriction.
Integrated RTE-style chart case

Normal at rest; limited on the walk

An adult reports worsening exertional breathlessness and a dry cough:
  • Quality lung volumes show TLC below the lower reference limit; FEV1/FVC is normal
  • Hemoglobin-adjusted DLCO is low; HRCT shows new bilateral reticular changes
  • Room-air saturation is 95% at rest and falls to 86% on a supervised walk
  • Earlier FVC and walk results were better
