Neuromuscular Respiratory Failure Assessment

When breathing muscles weaken, a patient may lose the ability to take a deep breath, ventilate through sleep, cough up mucus, or protect the airway. Pulse oximetry can look reassuring early. Gather repeatable measures of vital capacity, inspiratory and expiratory strength, and cough; then interpret their trend with bulbar function, secretions, and the patient’s breathing effort.

Is the breathing pump tiring?

Respiratory muscle assessment

VC → MIP → MEP and cough → airway

Measure the inspiratory pump

Vital capacity (VC) measures the largest breath the patient can move. Maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP, also called negative inspiratory force) measures strength pulling inward against an occluded airway. Use coached repeat efforts and record position, units, and trends.

Measure expiration and cough

Maximum expiratory pressure (MEP) measures strength pushing outward. Peak cough flow (PCF) captures how effectively a cough moves air. Weak expiration or bulbar problems can reduce secretion clearance even when resting oxygen saturation is normal.

Check the technique and the airway

A poor lip seal, leak, fatigue, or inconsistent effort can falsely lower pressure readings. A mask or alternative measure may help when facial weakness prevents a seal. Check speech, swallowing, choking, drooling, and ability to manage secretions.

Interpret serial change

A declining VC, a MIP moving toward zero, a falling MEP or PCF, or rising carbon dioxide with shallow breathing suggests ventilatory pump failure. Escalate urgently when rapid decline, weak cough, bulbar weakness, or airway compromise appears; do not wait for desaturation.

Core rule: no single number captures ventilation, cough, and airway protection. Compare quality VC, MIP, MEP, and cough results with prior values and the patient’s bulbar function and clinical trend.
Chart cue: an MIP of −25 cm H2O is weaker than −60 cm H2O because the pressure is closer to zero. A normal saturation does not rule out early hypoventilation or an ineffective cough.
The breathing pump

Review each concept

Coach a full inhale followed by a complete exhale to measure VC; forced or slow methods may be used. Record liters or milliliters per kilogram, patient position, cooperation, and quality, then compare serial values.
For MIP, have the patient exhale toward residual volume and then inhale as hard as possible against an occluded measurement device, following local protocol. Check the seal and repeat efforts for a reliable result.
For MEP, begin near full inspiration and ask for a forceful expiration against the measuring device. Weak expiratory muscles may limit cough; report pressure units and repeatable efforts.
Maximum voluntary ventilation (MVV) measures the amount moved during a short period of rapid deep breathing, projected to one minute. It reflects overall performance and effort and is not an isolated test of one muscle.
MIP is usually reported as a negative pressure. Moving from −60 to −25 cm H2O means less inspiratory strength, even though the printed number seems larger mathematically.
PCF estimates how strongly the patient can expel air with a cough. Low or declining flow, retained mucus, or repeated need for suction suggests risk of poor clearance and a need for cough-support planning.
Slurred or nasal speech, difficulty swallowing, choking, pooled secretions, and weak voice can indicate impaired upper-airway function. Even a patient who can generate some inspiratory pressure may be unable to protect the airway.
Air leaking around the mouthpiece or through the nose can make a pressure or volume result falsely low. Reposition the device, use a nose clip or mask when suitable, repeat the maneuver, and document the limitation.
Shallow breaths, morning headache, sleep disruption, new drowsiness, or rising carbon dioxide can indicate hypoventilation. Pulse oximetry measures oxygenation; it does not measure carbon dioxide clearance.
A rapid fall in VC together with weaker MIP and MEP, poor cough, or worsening bulbar symptoms calls for urgent team assessment and a ventilatory and airway plan before collapse.
Integrated RTE-style data case

Saturation looks normal, strength does not

An adult with evolving neuromuscular weakness is assessed four hours apart:
  • Quality VC falls from 24 to 14 mL/kg; MIP changes from −52 to −26 cm H2O
  • MEP falls from 48 to 29 cm H2O and the cough becomes ineffective
  • Speech is newly nasal, swallowing is difficult, and secretions pool
  • Resting saturation is 96% on room air
