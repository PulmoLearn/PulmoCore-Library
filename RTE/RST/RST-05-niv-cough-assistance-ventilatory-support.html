NIV, Cough Assistance & Ventilatory Support

In neuromuscular disease, ventilation, cough, and airway protection may fail at different times. Noninvasive ventilation (NIV) can support weak breathing in selected patients. A cough assistance plan helps move secretions. Follow the patient’s response and recommend a change when support is no longer enough.

Socratic support-decision string

Which support fits the need?

Match care to the failure

Ventilate → clear cough → protect airway

Support ventilation

For stable chronic hypoventilation from neuromuscular weakness, consider NIV after reviewing symptoms, breathing tests, carbon dioxide, comfort, and goals of care. Select a plan that assists breaths and reassess sleep and daytime function.

Move secretions

Check cough strength, peak cough flow, mucus retention, and ability to clear the upper airway. Assisted cough may use breath stacking, manual assistance, or mechanical insufflation–exsufflation (MI-E), which gives a breath and then rapidly helps pull air out.

Protect the airway

Check swallowing, choking, pooled secretions, alertness, and aspiration. Suction and secretion management may be needed. A mask does not protect the airway; severe bulbar dysfunction or failed NIV calls for prompt airway-capable evaluation.

Change the plan with the trend

Recheck breathing effort, carbon dioxide when available, cough and secretion movement, patient comfort, and alertness. Recommend stronger support or urgent invasive ventilation evaluation when ventilation fails or secretions and airway protection cannot be managed.

Core rule: recommend a care plan for each function. NIV assists ventilation; cough assistance moves mucus; suction and airway assessment address secretions and aspiration risk. Reassess whether each choice works.
Chart cue: stable oxygen saturation can coexist with rising carbon dioxide and a weak cough. A mask trial is unsafe as a delay when the patient cannot protect the airway or is rapidly failing.
Respiratory support decisions

Review each concept

In a patient with neuromuscular disease and chronic respiratory failure, NIV can reduce the work of breathing and improve ventilation. Look for orthopnea, sleep symptoms, morning headache, reduced breathing tests, or elevated carbon dioxide.
Check mask tolerance, breathing effort, leak, alertness, carbon dioxide or blood gas when indicated, and clinical response. A reassuring oxygen number by itself does not show adequate ventilation.
In Guillain–Barré syndrome or myasthenic crisis, rapid weakness, worsening bulbar signs, or falling vital capacity requires close, airway-capable care. Do not use a prolonged NIV trial to postpone needed intubation.
A weak cough or falling peak cough flow calls for a clearance plan. Breath stacking increases inspired volume; manually assisted cough adds timed external pressure; MI-E alternates positive and negative pressure to help move mucus.
After cough assistance, reassess audible secretions, cough strength, suction yield, breathing comfort, and retained mucus. If clearance remains poor, reassess technique and recommend a different or stronger approach.
Choking, drooling, nasal speech, and pooled secretions point to impaired swallow or airway control. NIV does not seal the airway against aspiration. Consider suction, team assessment, and an airway plan.
Thick mucus and excess saliva can disrupt NIV and cough care. Review hydration, humidification, positioning, suction access, and a team plan for saliva management as appropriate for the patient.
Inspiratory muscle training uses repeated inhalation against resistance in selected, stable patients. It is a planned rehabilitation option after assessment; it is not rescue treatment for rapidly declining ventilation or an unsafe airway.
In amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, repeat respiratory and cough assessments support early discussion of NIV, cough assistance, secretion care, patient preferences, and possible invasive ventilation if noninvasive support later fails.
If cough support helps but carbon dioxide rises, reassess ventilatory support. If ventilation improves but aspiration and secretion pooling worsen, address airway protection. Report the response and recommend the next action.

The mask helps, but the airway worsens

An adult with progressive neuromuscular weakness uses NIV overnight:
  • Morning headache improves and carbon dioxide decreases with NIV
  • Cough is weaker, mucus remains despite assisted cough, and suction is frequent
  • Swallowing worsens; saliva pools and the patient chokes repeatedly
  • Resting oxygen saturation is 96% on room air
