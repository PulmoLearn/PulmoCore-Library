Carbon Monoxide & Cyanide Toxicity

Carbon monoxide and cyanide can cause severe tissue hypoxia while familiar oxygen values look reassuring. Carbon monoxide reduces hemoglobin oxygen transport, so conventional pulse oximetry and arterial oxygen pressure may appear normal. Cyanide prevents cells from using oxygen and can cause sudden collapse with marked lactic acidosis. Use exposure history, clinical findings, and multiple-wavelength hemoximetry (CO-oximetry) together.

Toxic hypoxia framework

Suspect → interpret → treat → escalate

Suspect the exposure

Consider carbon monoxide with fire smoke, indoor generators, heaters, engines, charcoal, or multiple people with headache, nausea, dizziness, confusion, syncope, or chest pain. Enclosed fires can produce both carbon monoxide and cyanide.

Interpret the right measurements

Arterial oxygen pressure measures dissolved oxygen, not usable hemoglobin capacity. Conventional pulse oximetry cannot reliably distinguish oxyhemoglobin from carboxyhemoglobin. Use blood CO-oximetry to measure carboxyhemoglobin.

Treat before confirmation

Remove the patient from exposure, give 100% oxygen immediately, support ventilation and circulation, and obtain blood for CO-oximetry and lactate. Do not titrate oxygen downward because the pulse-oximeter value looks normal.

Escalate severe toxicity

Consult poison control or medical toxicology. Seek hyperbaric consultation for severe carbon monoxide findings. Give hydroxocobalamin promptly when life-threatening cyanide poisoning is strongly suspected; do not wait for a delayed cyanide level.

Core hemoximetry rule: normal arterial oxygen pressure (PaO2) or pulse-oximeter saturation (SpO2) does not exclude carbon monoxide poisoning. PaO2 reflects dissolved oxygen, and standard pulse oximetry can misclassify carboxyhemoglobin.
Interpret carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) in context: the level may fall after time away from exposure or after oxygen begins, and it does not always match clinical severity. Use the exposure history, neurologic and cardiac findings, acidosis, and treatment already given.
Measurement, treatment & escalation

Review each concept

Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless. Common sources include fire smoke, indoor generators, faulty heaters, engines, and charcoal used in enclosed spaces. Clusters of similar symptoms in people sharing a location are an important clue.
Carbon monoxide forms carboxyhemoglobin, reducing oxygen-carrying capacity and impairing oxygen release to tissues. Neurologic and cardiac tissues are especially vulnerable, causing headache, confusion, syncope, seizure, chest pain, ischemia, or coma.
PaO2 measures oxygen dissolved in plasma and can remain normal. Standard two-wavelength pulse oximetry may display carboxyhemoglobin as oxyhemoglobin. Neither result reliably shows usable oxygen delivery during carbon monoxide poisoning.
A laboratory CO-oximeter uses multiple wavelengths to measure carboxyhemoglobin and other hemoglobin species. Venous or arterial blood can be used. Interpret the result with exposure timing, oxygen treatment, symptoms, and smoking history.
Administer 100% oxygen as soon as carbon monoxide exposure is suspected and continue while evaluation proceeds. Protect the airway and ventilate when needed. A reassuring conventional saturation is not a reason to reduce oxygen.
Seek hyperbaric oxygen consultation for serious neurologic findings, loss of consciousness, cardiac involvement, severe acidosis, or markedly elevated carboxyhemoglobin. Pregnancy supports a lower threshold because fetal hemoglobin clears carbon monoxide slowly.
Burning plastics and other nitrogen-containing materials can release cyanide in enclosed fires. Cyanide blocks mitochondrial oxygen use, producing abrupt altered mental status, seizures, severe lactic acidosis, hypotension, cardiovascular collapse, or arrest.
Cyanide testing is rarely available fast enough to guide resuscitation. When life-threatening cyanide poisoning is strongly suspected, administer hydroxocobalamin according to protocol while supporting airway, breathing, and circulation.
Hydroxocobalamin commonly causes deep red skin, plasma, and urine discoloration. It may interfere with color-based laboratory tests and some hemodialysis blood-leak detectors. Communicate administration to the laboratory and receiving team.
Trend neurologic status, electrocardiogram, cardiac biomarkers when indicated, acid-base status, lactate, perfusion, carboxyhemoglobin, and response to oxygen or antidote. Arrange follow-up because delayed neurologic problems can occur after carbon monoxide exposure.

Recognize two forms of toxic hypoxia

Adult rescued from an enclosed structure fire:
  • Confusion, hypotension, and soot around the mouth
  • SpO2 is 99% on a nonrebreather mask
  • PaO2 is 180 mm Hg while receiving oxygen
  • Carboxyhemoglobin is 22%
  • Lactate is 12 mmol/L with severe metabolic acidosis
