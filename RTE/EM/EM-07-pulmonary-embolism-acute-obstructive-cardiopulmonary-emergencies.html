Pulmonary Embolism & Acute Obstructive Cardiopulmonary Emergencies

A pulmonary embolism (PE) is a clot that blocks blood flow through lung arteries. Air may reach lung units that receive little blood, raising dead-space ventilation; oxygenation can fall while the right ventricle strains against the obstruction. Use clinical probability to guide testing in stable patients. Sudden hypotension, shock, or collapse demands immediate cardiopulmonary support and escalation.

Which path fits this patient?

Pulmonary embolism framework

Suspect → image → support → reassess

Recognize the pattern

Sudden shortness of breath, pleuritic chest pain, fast breathing, fast heart rate, low oxygen, fainting, or unexplained hypotension may suggest PE. Look for recent surgery, immobility, cancer, pregnancy, estrogen exposure, or leg symptoms. No single finding proves PE.

Understand the mismatch

A blocked pulmonary artery leaves some lung units ventilated but poorly perfused: dead-space ventilation increases. Blood is diverted to other regions, and gas exchange can worsen. A large clot raises right-ventricular pressure and can reduce blood flow to the left heart.

Choose a diagnostic path

In a stable patient with low or intermediate clinical probability, a D-dimer test can help rule out PE when negative under an accepted pathway. High probability or a positive D-dimer calls for imaging. Computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is standard; a ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) scan is an option when CTPA is unsuitable.

Respond to instability

Give oxygen when needed, monitor closely, and activate urgent medical and PE response help for hypotension, shock, or collapse. Bedside echocardiography can reveal right-ventricular strain and help urgent assessment if transport is unsafe, but it does not confirm or exclude PE on its own. Prepare for provider-directed treatment and advanced support.

Core imaging rule: select testing according to clinical probability and patient stability. Do not rely on a D-dimer alone when PE probability is high.
Emergency cue: sudden hypotension or worsening oxygenation with suspected PE calls for immediate oxygenation, monitoring, and team escalation while the diagnostic and treatment plan proceeds.
Clues, imaging & emergency care

Review each concept

Recent surgery, immobility, cancer, pregnancy, estrogen exposure, or a prior clot can raise PE probability. Sudden unexplained dyspnea, pleuritic pain, tachycardia, syncope, or hypoxemia should trigger assessment.
An embolus blocks perfusion to some ventilated alveoli. Those breaths move air but exchange little gas in the blocked region. Overall ventilation-perfusion matching becomes less efficient, and oxygenation may worsen.
A large obstruction raises resistance in lung arteries. The right ventricle must pump against a sudden high load and may enlarge or fail. Blood pressure can fall as less blood reaches the left heart.
Combine symptoms, vital signs, risk factors, and a validated clinical decision pathway. Testing depends on whether probability is low, intermediate, or high, and whether the patient can safely undergo imaging.
A negative D-dimer under an accepted pathway can help rule out PE in a stable low- or intermediate-probability patient. A positive result is nonspecific and usually calls for imaging. High clinical probability should proceed to imaging without using D-dimer to rule out PE.
Computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) directly images pulmonary arteries and is the standard diagnostic scan when indicated and feasible. It can show clots and signs of right-ventricular enlargement.
A ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) scan compares where air goes with where blood goes. It is an alternative when iodinated contrast or another factor makes CTPA unsuitable. Its interpretation depends on clinical context and image quality.
Echocardiography can identify right-ventricular strain during urgent evaluation when shock makes transport unsafe. Right-heart strain has other causes, so bedside echo findings alone do not establish PE in most patients.
For suspected PE with shock, supply oxygen as needed, monitor perfusion, activate an emergency or PE response team, and prepare for provider-directed treatment. If cardiac arrest occurs, follow resuscitation protocols while reversible causes are addressed.
Trend mental status, blood pressure, pulse, respiratory effort, oxygenation, and response to support. A previously stable patient who develops hypotension or syncope needs immediate reassessment and escalation.

Recommend the next diagnostic step

Adult two weeks after major surgery:
  • Sudden shortness of breath and pleuritic pain
  • Heart rate 118 per minute; oxygen saturation 90% on room air
  • Blood pressure 124/76 mm Hg, alert and able to lie flat
