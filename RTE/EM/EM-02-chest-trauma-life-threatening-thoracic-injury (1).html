Chest Trauma & Life-Threatening Thoracic Injury

Chest trauma can impair ventilation, collapse a lung, fill the pleural space with blood, or obstruct circulation within minutes. Expose and compare both sides of the chest, connect physical findings to the injury pattern, and treat immediate threats as they are found. An unstable patient with clinical signs of tension pneumothorax needs decompression before confirmatory imaging.

Which chest injury comes first?

Thoracic trauma framework

Inspect → compare → stabilize → reassess

Inspect and palpate

Expose the chest while preventing heat loss. Look for penetrating wounds, bruising, deformity, paradoxical motion, asymmetry, and respiratory effort. Palpate for tenderness, instability, crepitus, and subcutaneous air.

Compare both sides

Assess chest expansion, breath sounds, percussion, oxygenation, ventilation, pulse, blood pressure, and neck veins. Unilateral findings help separate pneumothorax, hemothorax, airway placement problems, and lung injury.

Stabilize immediate threats

Provide oxygen and ventilatory support as needed. Seal an open chest wound with a vented seal or three-sided dressing per protocol, decompress suspected tension pneumothorax, and prepare for tube thoracostomy.

Reassess the whole system

Repeat the chest exam after airway placement, positive-pressure ventilation, decompression, chest-tube insertion, transport, or deterioration. Confirm that the intervention improved air entry, ventilation, oxygenation, and circulation.

Core chest-trauma rule: tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis in an unstable patient. Do not delay emergency decompression for a chest radiograph or computed tomography scan.
Do not anchor on the first explanation: sudden unilateral breath-sound loss after intubation may reflect mainstem intubation, tube displacement, mucus obstruction, hemothorax, or pneumothorax. Hypotension, rising airway pressure, and rapid deterioration increase concern for tension physiology.
Recognition, priority & respiratory support

Review each concept

Suspect tension pneumothorax with rapid respiratory and circulatory decline, unilateral absent or markedly reduced breath sounds, increasing distress, hypotension, and possible neck-vein distention. Tracheal deviation is a late sign. Decompress immediately and follow with definitive pleural drainage.
A penetrating chest wound may pull air through the chest wall and impair ventilation. Give oxygen, apply a vented chest seal or a three-sided occlusive dressing according to protocol, monitor closely for tension physiology, and prepare for a chest tube placed away from the wound.
A stable traumatic pneumothorax may be small or visible only on computed tomography. Observation and repeat imaging may be appropriate in selected stable patients, but positive-pressure ventilation and clinical deterioration increase concern for expansion or tension.
Blood in the pleural space causes reduced breath sounds, impaired ventilation, and shock when blood loss is large. Percussion is more likely dull than hyperresonant. Support oxygenation and circulation, prepare for pleural drainage, and escalate urgently for heavy or ongoing blood loss.
Flail chest occurs when a chest-wall segment moves paradoxically because multiple adjacent ribs are fractured in more than one place. Provide oxygen, strong pain control, and pulmonary hygiene. Use ventilatory support for respiratory failure, not solely to stabilize the moving segment.
Blunt injury can cause alveolar bleeding and edema without an external wound. Hypoxemia, crackles, and patchy opacities may worsen over the first 24 to 48 hours. Provide oxygen, analgesia, careful fluid management, and ventilatory support when needed.
A large persistent air leak, extensive subcutaneous emphysema, pneumomediastinum, or failure of the lung to re-expand after chest-tube placement should raise concern for major airway disruption. Maintain oxygenation, obtain airway and surgical help, and anticipate bronchoscopy.
Diaphragm rupture can allow abdominal organs into the chest and compress the lung. Clues include bowel sounds in the chest, an elevated or unclear hemidiaphragm, or a gastric tube seen above the diaphragm. Stabilize breathing and arrange urgent surgical evaluation.
Bedside ultrasound and portable chest radiography can rapidly support the trauma assessment. Computed tomography detects smaller and more complex injuries in stable patients. Imaging helps define injury but must not delay treatment of a clinically apparent life threat.
After oxygen, assisted ventilation, wound sealing, decompression, or chest-tube placement, repeat the respiratory and circulatory exam. Track chest rise, bilateral air entry, airway pressure, oxygenation, carbon dioxide, pulse, blood pressure, and drainage-system function.

Treat before the image

Intubated adult after a high-speed collision:
  • Sudden severe hypoxemia and hypotension
  • Markedly increased peak airway pressure
  • Absent breath sounds on the left
  • Distended neck veins and rapidly worsening perfusion
  • Trachea appears shifted toward the right
