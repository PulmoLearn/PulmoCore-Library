Burns, Smoke Exposure & Respiratory Failure

After a fire, respiratory danger can come from a swelling upper airway, soot and inflammation in the lower airways, or toxic gases that disrupt oxygen delivery and use. Assess exposure and airway changes early. Give high-concentration oxygen when smoke inhalation is suspected, prepare airway support when obstruction is developing, and keep reassessing even if the first examination seems reassuring.

Burn and smoke emergency framework

Assess → oxygenate → protect airway → reassess

Assess exposure and the airway

A fire in an enclosed space raises concern for both inhalation injury and toxic gases. Check voice, swallowing, stridor, work of breathing, facial and oral burns, soot in the mouth, and mental status. Progressive hoarseness, stridor, or swelling needs immediate expert airway assessment.

Address hidden toxins

Give high-concentration oxygen for suspected smoke exposure. Carbon monoxide (CO) can impair oxygen transport while a standard pulse oximeter looks reassuring. Obtain blood carboxyhemoglobin by laboratory co-oximetry. Severe confusion, shock, or marked lactate elevation after an enclosed-space fire also raises concern for cyanide toxicity.

Plan respiratory support

Prepare controlled early intubation when airway obstruction is developing; a swollen airway may become harder to secure. If breathing is inadequate, support ventilation with appropriate equipment and experienced help. Lower-airway smoke injury may evolve over hours despite an initially mild examination.

Monitor and coordinate

Reassess airway sounds, chest movement, work of breathing, oxygenation, blood gas data, and perfusion. Bronchoscopy may help clinicians assess inhalation injury after the airway is stabilized. Coordinate urgent burn-center evaluation and provider-directed toxic-gas treatment when indicated.

Core emergency rule: treat the airway and oxygenation risks visible now while preparing for swelling, lower-airway injury, and toxic-gas effects that may appear later.
Interpretation warning: a normal-looking standard oxygen saturation does not exclude CO poisoning. Do not use pulse oximetry alone to reassure a patient exposed to smoke in an enclosed space.
Airway, toxins & evolving injury

Review each concept

An enclosed-space fire makes inhaled smoke and toxic-gas exposure more likely. Ask about time inside, loss of consciousness, other trauma, and any prior airway or breathing symptoms.
New hoarseness, stridor, difficulty swallowing, oral swelling, or increasing work of breathing suggests a narrowing airway. Facial burns and soot increase concern, but the airway decision depends on the whole picture and its trend.
Provide high-concentration oxygen when smoke exposure or carbon monoxide poisoning is suspected while evaluating ventilation and airway protection. Oxygen speeds CO elimination but does not replace airway support when breathing is ineffective.
A conventional pulse oximeter cannot reliably distinguish oxygenated hemoglobin from carboxyhemoglobin. An apparently normal saturation after smoke exposure does not exclude impaired oxygen transport from carbon monoxide.
Blood testing with laboratory co-oximetry measures carboxyhemoglobin after suspected carbon monoxide exposure. Interpret results alongside exposure history, symptoms, and how long the patient has received oxygen.
Enclosed-space smoke exposure with collapse, severe confusion, shock, or marked lactic acidosis raises concern for cyanide. Activate poison-center or toxicology help and prepare for provider-directed hydroxocobalamin when strongly suspected; do not delay oxygen and resuscitation.
Progressive voice change, stridor, oral swelling, or respiratory distress can precede complete obstruction. Call an experienced airway team and prepare controlled intubation before swelling makes the airway difficult to secure.
Soot and chemical irritants can injure bronchial lining, increase secretions, and worsen air movement and gas exchange over time. Repeat respiratory examinations and monitor for increasing support needs.
Clinicians may use bronchoscopy to examine airway injury and guide further care after immediate oxygenation and airway needs are addressed. It should not delay emergency airway protection when obstruction is progressing.
Trend voice, stridor, breathing effort, oxygenation, ventilation, blood pressure, mental status, and responses to treatment. Communicate fire setting, exposure time, oxygen given, CO testing, airway findings, and changes to the receiving burn or emergency team.
