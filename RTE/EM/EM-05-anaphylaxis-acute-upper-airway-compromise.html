Anaphylaxis & Acute Upper-Airway Compromise

Anaphylaxis is a rapidly developing reaction that can affect breathing and circulation. After a likely trigger, throat swelling or stridor, wheeze, hypotension, and gastrointestinal symptoms can signal danger. A rash may be absent. Give intramuscular epinephrine promptly, activate emergency help, support oxygenation and circulation, and prepare for a difficult airway if swelling progresses.

What must happen first?

Anaphylaxis emergency framework

Recognize → treat → support → reassess

Recognize the pattern

Symptoms may follow foods, medications, insect stings, or another exposure. Look for rapid throat or tongue swelling, voice change, stridor, wheeze, breathing difficulty, hypotension, collapse, hives, flushing, vomiting, or abdominal cramps. Skin findings are not required.

Give the first-line drug

Administer intramuscular epinephrine into the outer mid-thigh immediately when anaphylaxis is suspected. Activate emergency response and remove an ongoing trigger when possible. Repeat intramuscular epinephrine per protocol if symptoms persist or recur.

Support airway and circulation

Give supplemental oxygen, monitor closely, assist ventilation if needed, and prepare intravenous fluids for hypotension. Progressive hoarseness, stridor, tongue swelling, or poor air movement calls for experienced airway help and a rescue plan.

Reassess and observe

Check voice, swelling, air movement, wheeze, blood pressure, mental status, and response after treatment. Inhaled bronchodilators can help persistent lower-airway wheeze. Antihistamines may ease itching, but they do not replace epinephrine.

Core emergency rule: intramuscular epinephrine is the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis. Do not delay it for antihistamines, steroids, imaging, or laboratory tests.
Airway warning: stridor suggests upper-airway narrowing; wheeze suggests lower-airway narrowing. Both can occur together, and rapidly increasing swelling can make later intubation difficult.
Recognition, treatment & reassessment

Review each concept

A new medication, food, sting, or other exposure followed by rapid breathing or circulatory symptoms raises concern for anaphylaxis. The exact trigger may remain uncertain during initial treatment.
Anaphylaxis can present with sudden bronchospasm, laryngeal involvement, or hypotension after a likely allergen even when no rash is visible. Do not wait for a second organ system when a life-threatening pattern is already present.
Stridor is a harsh sound from narrowed upper airways, often most audible during inspiration. Wheeze usually comes from lower-airway narrowing and is often most noticeable during exhalation. Listen for both and assess air movement.
Intramuscular epinephrine into the outer mid-thigh treats airway edema, bronchospasm, and vasodilation. Use the appropriate dose or auto-injector for patient size under local protocol. Do not substitute a bronchodilator or antihistamine.
Persistent or recurring breathing or circulatory compromise may require another intramuscular epinephrine dose according to protocol. Recheck the airway, breath sounds, oxygenation, pulse, and blood pressure after each dose.
Provide oxygen according to severity, monitor work of breathing, and assist ventilation if breathing becomes ineffective. If stridor, swelling, or decreasing air movement progresses, involve an experienced airway clinician early.
Widespread vasodilation and fluid leakage can cause severe hypotension. Keep the patient in an appropriate position, give epinephrine, establish intravenous access, and administer fluids as indicated while monitoring perfusion.
Inhaled bronchodilators may help persistent wheeze after epinephrine. Antihistamines can help itching and hives. Neither replaces epinephrine for airway swelling or shock; corticosteroids should not delay immediate treatment.
New hoarseness, stridor, tongue swelling, drooling, or rapidly changing voice suggests worsening upper-airway compromise. Call for expert airway and surgical backup while oxygenation remains possible; prepare rescue equipment before obstruction worsens.
Symptoms can persist or return after initial improvement. Continue monitored observation appropriate to severity, response, and local protocol; arrange emergency evaluation and a clear handoff that includes exposure, doses, times, and response.

Prioritize the first intervention

Adult shortly after receiving an antibiotic:
  • New hoarse voice and inspiratory stridor
  • Diffuse wheeze and labored breathing
  • Blood pressure 78/42 mm Hg and increasing confusion
  • Raised red skin patches are visible on the trunk
