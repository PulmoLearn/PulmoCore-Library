Initial Respiratory Assessment in High-Risk Emergencies

A high-risk emergency demands a rapid primary survey, not a complete diagnosis. Start with scene safety and a first impression, then identify immediate airway and breathing threats. Treat the first life threat found, call for needed help, and reassess after every intervention. Focused history, monitoring, laboratory results, and imaging refine care but should not delay support for an obstructed airway or ineffective breathing.

What requires action first?

Rapid respiratory primary survey

Recognize → assess → intervene → reassess

Recognize immediate danger

Confirm scene safety, use appropriate protective equipment, assess responsiveness, and form a quick visual impression. Activate emergency support early when the patient is unresponsive, unstable, or likely to need advanced airway management.

Assess airway patency

Listen for speech, airflow, stridor, snoring, or gurgling. Look for blood, vomit, secretions, swelling, trauma, or a foreign body. An airway that cannot be maintained takes priority over a detailed history.

Assess breathing effectiveness

Check rate, depth, effort, chest rise, symmetry, breath sounds, skin color, mental status, oxygen saturation, and exhaled carbon dioxide when available. A normal saturation value does not prove that ventilation is adequate.

Intervene and reassess

Open and clear the airway, provide oxygen, assist ventilation, or escalate airway support as indicated. After each action, reassess chest rise, air entry, oxygenation, carbon dioxide, circulation, and mental status.

Core emergency rule: treat the first life threat found. Do not wait for the full history, laboratory results, or imaging before correcting airway obstruction or ineffective breathing.
Monitor the patient, not one number: poor perfusion, motion, dyshemoglobins, and delayed response can limit pulse oximetry. Pair monitor data with breathing effort, chest rise, air movement, color, and mental status.
Assessment, action & reassessment

Review each concept

Confirm the scene is safe and use appropriate protective equipment. Note responsiveness, position, skin color, visible effort, ability to speak, and major bleeding. These first seconds help determine whether to activate more help immediately.
Normal speech suggests airflow through the upper airway. Stridor, snoring, gurgling, weak speech, silence, paradoxical movement, or visible material may signal obstruction. Open the airway and remove or suction accessible material as indicated.
Rate alone is not enough. Assess depth, pattern, effort, symmetry, chest rise, air entry, breath sounds, color, and mental status. Slow shallow breaths, minimal chest rise, or declining alertness can signal respiratory failure even before saturation falls.
Pulse oximetry estimates oxygen saturation; it does not measure carbon dioxide removal. Evaluate ventilation with respiratory depth, chest rise, mental status, exhaled carbon dioxide, and blood gas data when available.
Position and open the airway, suction blood or secretions, and use an airway adjunct when indicated and not contraindicated. With suspected cervical injury, begin with a jaw-thrust maneuver while maintaining stabilization, but do not allow airway compromise to persist.
A patient with a pulse but absent or inadequate breathing needs assisted ventilation. Use a properly sized bag-mask device with oxygen, maintain a seal and open airway, deliver only enough volume for visible chest rise, and avoid excessive ventilation.
Call for advanced airway help when basic maneuvers cannot maintain patency, bag-mask ventilation remains ineffective, protective reflexes are lost, or rapid deterioration is likely. Continue effective oxygenation and ventilation during preparation.
Assess pulse, perfusion, blood pressure, major bleeding, and level of consciousness while respiratory care continues. If an unresponsive adult has no normal breathing and no definite pulse within 10 seconds, begin cardiopulmonary resuscitation.
Review the event, symptoms, medications, exposures, medical history, monitoring trends, laboratory results, and imaging that are immediately available. Use them to refine the cause and next action after urgent airway and breathing threats are addressed.
Repeat the primary survey after every intervention and whenever the condition changes. Report the original threat, action taken, response, current respiratory status, relevant data, and the support or equipment still needed.
Integrated RTE-style emergency case

Choose the first respiratory sequence

Adult found collapsed after an unknown event:
  • Responds only to painful stimulation
  • Pulse is present at 72/min
  • Breathing is shallow and irregular at 5/min
  • Gurgling secretions are heard in the upper airway
  • Chest rise is minimal and oxygen saturation is falling
