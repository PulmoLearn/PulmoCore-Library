Airway Trauma & Inhalation Injury

Facial injury, neck trauma, and smoke exposure can turn a manageable airway into a difficult or impossible airway as bleeding and edema increase. Look beyond oxygen saturation: assess the mechanism, voice, mouth and neck, airflow, secretions, swelling, and trend. When obstruction is developing, mobilize an experienced airway team and protect the airway before anatomy becomes severely distorted.

Difficult-airway recognition framework

Recognize → trend → prepare → protect

Recognize high-risk mechanisms

Closed-space fire, blast, facial or neck burns, blunt or penetrating facial injury, expanding neck swelling, and direct laryngeal trauma should raise concern. Note cervical-spine precautions and possible toxic exposure.

Trend the airway exam

Repeat assessment of voice, stridor, drooling, swallowing, oral opening, soot, edema, bleeding, secretions, facial distortion, neck crepitus, air movement, respiratory effort, and mental status. Change over time matters.

Prepare multiple pathways

Call the most experienced airway clinician early. Prepare suction, oxygenation and ventilation equipment, video or flexible visualization, a supraglottic rescue device when appropriate, and emergency invasive-airway backup.

Protect and verify

When progressive obstruction is likely, secure the airway before severe edema removes safe options. Confirm placement with continuous waveform capnography, assess bilateral air entry and depth, secure the tube, and reassess frequently.

Core airway-trauma rule: a patient who can be oxygenated now may still be losing the airway. Progressive hoarseness, stridor, swelling, or distorted anatomy supports early controlled airway protection.
One clue is not the whole decision: singed nasal hair or facial soot alone does not automatically require intubation. Combine the mechanism with symptoms, oral and neck findings, respiratory status, expected course, and ability to monitor closely.
Recognition, timing & airway safety

Review each concept

An enclosed-space fire raises concern for inhalation injury and toxic gases. Facial impact, blast, penetrating neck trauma, prolonged entrapment, and circumferential face or neck burns can predict a difficult airway even before severe symptoms appear.
Progressive hoarseness, stridor, drooling, dysphagia, oral burns, tongue or facial swelling, soot in the mouth, carbonaceous secretions, respiratory distress, and altered mental status increase concern for airway compromise.
Thermal injury and tissue trauma can produce increasing edema over time, and fluid resuscitation may make swelling more apparent. Delaying airway protection can turn a controlled intubation into a failed airway emergency.
Blood, vomit, loose teeth, tissue disruption, and swelling can block visualization and ventilation. Use aggressive suction and cervical-spine precautions as indicated. Avoid blind nasal instrumentation when midface or skull-base injury is suspected.
An expanding neck hematoma, voice change, stridor, hemoptysis, subcutaneous air, crepitus, or air bubbling through a neck wound suggests laryngeal or tracheal injury. Minimize repeated manipulation and obtain expert airway and surgical support.
State the primary technique, how oxygenation will continue, when an attempt will stop, the rescue ventilation device, and the emergency invasive-airway plan. Bring skilled help and needed equipment before medications remove spontaneous breathing.
Early controlled intubation is favored when obstruction is present or likely to progress, ventilation or oxygenation is failing, protective reflexes are lost, transport will be prolonged, or facial and neck anatomy is rapidly worsening.
Smoke and chemical particles can injure the tracheobronchial tree, causing bronchospasm, inflammation, edema, and airway casts. Bronchoscopy can help define lower-airway injury and guide pulmonary hygiene after the airway is stabilized.
A normal pulse-oximeter value does not rule out a threatened upper airway or toxic smoke exposure. Give high-concentration oxygen after significant smoke exposure while airway assessment and appropriate toxic-exposure testing continue.
Confirm the endotracheal tube with continuous waveform capnography, bilateral air entry, chest movement, depth marking, and imaging when appropriate. Secure the tube carefully and reassess after swelling, procedures, movement, and transport.

Protect the airway before it closes

Adult rescued from an enclosed house fire:
  • Facial and neck burns with soot in the mouth
  • Voice has become progressively hoarse
  • Increasing tongue and oropharyngeal swelling
  • New inspiratory stridor and difficulty swallowing
  • Oxygen saturation is 98% on a nonrebreather mask
