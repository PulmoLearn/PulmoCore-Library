Overdose, Sedative/Opioid Respiratory Depression & Antidotes

Opioids and other sedatives can slow breathing until oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises. Assess responsiveness, airway, breathing, and pulse. Call for help, open the airway, and provide effective ventilation when breathing is inadequate. Give naloxone for suspected opioid effects while ventilation continues; consider flumazenil only in carefully selected benzodiazepine cases.

How do you restore breathing?

Overdose emergency framework

Assess → ventilate → reverse → reassess

Assess the emergency

Check responsiveness, breathing quality, and pulse while activating emergency help. Slow, shallow, or absent breathing with reduced alertness is dangerous even before oxygen saturation falls. Look for medication containers, recent procedures, and other exposure clues.

Support breathing now

Open the airway, give oxygen as needed, and provide bag-mask ventilation if breathing is ineffective. If there is no definite pulse, begin cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) with breaths and follow the arrest algorithm. Do not pause basic resuscitation to search for an antidote.

Match the reversal agent

Naloxone reverses opioid effects and may be given for suspected opioid-related respiratory depression. Flumazenil reverses benzodiazepine effects in selected low-risk patients, such as some monitored procedural-sedation cases. It does not reverse opioids or alcohol.

Monitor and escalate

Recheck breathing rate and depth, chest rise, oxygenation, carbon dioxide monitoring when available, mental status, and pulse. Naloxone can wear off before an opioid does; repeat doses or an infusion may be needed under protocol. Prepare advanced airway support if ventilation remains inadequate.

Core emergency rule: protect oxygen delivery and ventilation first. Give an indicated reversal agent without delaying airway support or CPR.
Flumazenil caution: chronic benzodiazepine use, seizure risk, or a mixed/unknown overdose can make flumazenil dangerous. Seek toxicology or poison-center advice and follow local protocols; supportive ventilation remains essential.
Recognition, support & antidotes

Review each concept

Count breaths and assess depth, chest rise, airway sounds, and responsiveness. A slow rate with shallow breaths means poor ventilation and carbon dioxide removal. Pulse oximetry may lag, especially when supplemental oxygen is being given.
An unresponsive patient with a definite pulse but ineffective breathing needs assisted ventilation and prompt emergency support. If there is no definite pulse, start CPR with breaths and follow the cardiac arrest algorithm.
Reposition the airway and deliver effective bag-mask breaths when spontaneous ventilation is inadequate. Watch for chest rise and reassess air movement. Oxygen alone does not remove retained carbon dioxide when the patient is barely breathing.
Reduced alertness with slow or absent breathing after possible opioid exposure supports an opioid toxidrome. Small pupils can be a clue but are neither required nor enough by themselves to diagnose overdose.
Give naloxone promptly by an available route under protocol when opioid-related respiratory depression is suspected. Continue ventilation while waiting for response. The immediate treatment goal is adequate breathing and airway protection.
Opioids may be taken with benzodiazepines, alcohol, or other sedatives. Naloxone reverses only the opioid portion. If breathing remains inadequate after naloxone, keep ventilating and evaluate other causes rather than assuming the patient is safe.
Flumazenil can reverse benzodiazepine sedation in carefully selected patients with a known exposure and low seizure risk, such as some procedural-sedation events. It is not a routine treatment for an unknown overdose and does not replace airway support.
Flumazenil can trigger seizures or dangerous rhythms in higher-risk patients, especially with chronic benzodiazepine dependence or certain mixed overdoses. When the history is unclear, seek expert advice and prioritize supportive care.
Naloxone may wear off before the opioid. After initial improvement, continue monitoring respiratory rate, depth, mental status, oxygenation, and carbon dioxide when available. Give further naloxone or an infusion per protocol if depression recurs.
Record suspected substances, timing, initial breathing and pulse, ventilation provided, naloxone dose and route, response, and any recurrence. Arrange monitored emergency care; a brief awakening alone does not establish safety.

Prioritize respiratory support

Adult found unresponsive beside a prescription opioid bottle:
  • Breathing four shallow breaths per minute
  • Pulse 68 per minute; oxygen saturation falling
  • Minimal chest rise and no evidence of trauma
  • A family member reports possible use of a sleep medicine
