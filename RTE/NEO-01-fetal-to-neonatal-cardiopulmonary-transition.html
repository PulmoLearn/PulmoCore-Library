Fetal-to-Neonatal Cardiopulmonary Transition

Before birth, the placenta performs gas exchange and the fluid-filled lungs have high pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR). Fetal shunts route blood around the lungs and much of the liver. At birth, effective lung aeration and rising oxygen lower PVR, while cord clamping removes the low-resistance placenta and raises systemic vascular resistance (SVR). Those pressure changes redirect blood through the lungs and promote functional closure of the fetal shunts.

Transition framework

Three fetal shunts, two major resistance changes

Ductus venosus

Routes oxygenated blood from the umbilical vein toward the inferior vena cava, bypassing much of the liver.

Foramen ovale

Routes blood from the right atrium to the left atrium, helping deliver the best-oxygenated blood toward the left ventricle, ascending aorta, heart, and brain.

Ductus arteriosus

Routes much of right-ventricular output from the pulmonary artery into the aorta because fetal PVR is high.

At birth

Lung aeration lowers PVR; cord clamping raises SVR. Increased pulmonary blood flow raises left-atrial pressure and promotes functional closure of the foramen ovale.

Best-oxygenated fetal blood pathway:
Placenta → umbilical vein → ductus venosus → IVC → right atrium → foramen ovale → left atrium → left ventricle → ascending aorta.
Exam logic: if the lungs do not aerate effectively after birth, PVR may remain high, pulmonary blood flow remains low, left-atrial pressure may fail to rise appropriately, and fetal right-to-left shunting can persist.
Fetal circulation & transition

Review each concept

Before birth, the placenta—not the fetal lungs—provides gas exchange. Oxygenated blood returns to the fetus through the umbilical vein, while the umbilical arteries carry lower-oxygen blood back toward the placenta.
The ductus venosus connects the umbilical venous pathway toward the inferior vena cava and bypasses much of the hepatic circulation, helping preserve relatively oxygen-rich placental blood for the central circulation.
Preferential streaming directs much of the best-oxygenated blood across the foramen ovale from the right atrium to the left atrium, then through the left ventricle and ascending aorta toward the heart and brain.
Because fetal pulmonary vascular resistance is high, much of right-ventricular output bypasses the lungs through the ductus arteriosus from pulmonary artery to aorta.
Your PulmoLearn source identifies fluid-filled, unexpanded lungs and the relatively low-oxygen intrauterine environment as major reasons fetal PVR remains high. Low pulmonary blood flow is therefore normal fetal physiology.
Effective first breaths expand alveoli and increase oxygen tension. Lung expansion and oxygenation cause pulmonary vasodilation, so PVR falls and pulmonary blood flow rises.
Clamping the umbilical cord removes the low-resistance placental circulation. Your AHRC question bank explicitly teaches that this raises systemic vascular resistance.
As PVR falls, pulmonary blood flow and pulmonary venous return increase. Left-atrial pressure rises above right-atrial pressure, pressing the foramen ovale flap closed functionally.
Your PulmoLearn transition slide links higher oxygen tension and reduced prostaglandin effect with ductal constriction after birth. The important reasoning is that the newborn circulation no longer needs the fetal bypass pathway once pulmonary blood flow rises.
If lung aeration is ineffective, PVR may remain high. That limits pulmonary blood flow, reduces pulmonary venous return and left-atrial pressure, and can preserve fetal pressure relationships with persistent right-to-left shunting and hypoxemia.
For NEO-01, use gestational age, perinatal history, fetal maturity clues, and delivery context to anticipate transition risk. Prematurity, pulmonary hypoplasia, poor lung expansion, or conditions that impair normal fall in PVR change how confidently you expect spontaneous transition to succeed.
