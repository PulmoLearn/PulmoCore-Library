Sedation, Analgesia, Paralysis & Reversal Agents

Separate pain control, sedation, anesthesia, and neuromuscular blockade; recognize commonly tested agents and reversals; and respond safely when medication-related hypoventilation or airway compromise develops during critical care or moderate sedation.

2027 RTEIII.E.3 Pharmacologic recommendationsShared: III.H.8 Moderate sedation
AnalgesiaReduces pain.
SedationReduces anxiety/agitation or induces sleep.
ParalysisStops skeletal-muscle movement—not awareness or pain.
ReversalNaloxone • flumazenil • NMBA reversal agents.

Medication-role framework

Four categories the exam can deliberately blur

CategoryExamples supported by source setCore meaning / respiratory implication
Sedatives / hypnoticsMidazolam (Versed), lorazepam (Ativan), diazepam (Valium)Reduce anxiety/agitation or induce sleep. Benzodiazepines can depress ventilation; flumazenil is the source reversal agent.
Anesthetic agentsPropofol, ketamine, etomidateUsed for anesthesia/procedural or critical-care applications in the source set. Propofol is specifically associated with hypotension; these agents can affect airway reflexes and ventilatory drive.
AnalgesicsMorphine, fentanyl, other opioidsReduce pain. Opioids can depress respiratory drive; naloxone is the source reversal agent.
Neuromuscular blockersVecuronium, rocuronium, succinylcholineCause skeletal-muscle paralysis. They do not provide sedation or analgesia and remove spontaneous respiratory effort.
Critical rule

Paralysis ≠ sedation. The source review explicitly states that a patient can be awake but paralyzed. Sedation and analgesia must be addressed before/while neuromuscular blockade is used according to the clinical plan.

Source purpose: reduce anxiety/agitation and induce sleep/sedation.
Source reversal: flumazenil.
Respiratory concern: sedatives can reduce respiratory drive, mental status, airway tone, and spontaneous interaction with the ventilator.
Analgesia means reducing the perception of pain.
Source adverse effect: respiratory depression.
Source reversal: naloxone (Narcan) reverses opioid-related ventilatory depression.
Nondepolarizing: vecuronium, rocuronium; source mechanism = prevent acetylcholine from attaching to its receptor.
Depolarizing: succinylcholine; source identifies it as rapid-onset paralysis for rapid-sequence intubation.
Respiratory implication: NMBAs remove spontaneous effort and can mask examination findings. Ensure adequate ventilatory support and objective monitoring.
The source review lists neostigmine and pyridostigmine as reversal agents for nondepolarizing blockade.
The quiz specifically tests neostigmine as a reversal drug for nondepolarizing NMBAs.
Source monitoring: respiratory rate/depth, SpO₂, ETCO₂ when available, heart rate/rhythm, blood pressure, level of consciousness, airway patency, and patient response.
Supplemental oxygen may delay desaturation even while ventilation becomes dangerously inadequate.
Early clues: bradypnea, shallow breathing, rising ETCO₂, reduced capnogram frequency, somnolence, and loss of airway tone.
The source bronchoscopy guide directs the team to stop/reduce sedating medication as directed, stimulate/reposition, open the airway, provide oxygen, and support ventilation.
Summon assistance and prepare reversal or advanced airway management when indicated.
Do not wait for SpO₂ to fall when RR, ETCO₂, consciousness, or airway tone already show ventilatory failure.
The source weaning material says drugs suppressing ventilatory drive should be discontinued/reduced when evaluating spontaneous breathing readiness.
A separate course question links prolonged high-dose benzodiazepine use with long-term delirium and spontaneous-awakening trials.
Exam move: medication effect is part of the respiratory assessment—do not diagnose ventilatory failure or failed liberation without considering sedation/paralysis.
