Neonatal Resuscitation: Ventilation First

Most newborn bradycardia during resuscitation is driven by inadequate ventilation. After initial stabilization, begin positive-pressure ventilation (PPV) for apnea, gasping, or heart rate below 100/min. The best sign that PPV is working is a rising heart rate. If the chest does not move or the heart rate does not rise, correct ventilation before escalating.

Ventilation is the priority

Resuscitation framework

Establish effective lung inflation before escalating

1. Initial stabilization

Provide warmth, position the airway, dry and stimulate, and suction the mouth/nose only when secretions are obstructing breathing or ventilation. Routine suction should not delay ventilation.

2. Start PPV when indicated

Begin PPV when the newborn remains apneic, is gasping, or has heart rate below 100/min after initial steps. Your current PulmoLearn source uses a ventilation rate of 30–60 inflations/min.

3. Ask: is ventilation effective?

A rising heart rate is the most important sign. Gentle bilateral chest movement supports lung inflation. Persistent bradycardia should make you suspect ineffective ventilation until proven otherwise.

4. Correct before compressing

If the chest does not move or the heart rate does not improve, use MR SOPA to correct mask ventilation. Chest compressions belong only after effective ventilation has been established and severe bradycardia persists.

MR SOPA:
Mask adjustment → Reposition airway → Suction if obstruction suspected → Open mouth → Pressure increase → Alternative airway.

Ventilation-first decisions

Review each concept

Warm the infant, position the airway, dry and stimulate, and remove secretions only when they obstruct breathing or ventilation. If apnea, gasping, or bradycardia persists, move promptly to assisted ventilation rather than repeating stimulation.
Your current PulmoLearn neonatal resuscitation lesson states that PPV is indicated after initial steps when the infant is apneic, gasping, or the heart rate is below 100/min.
The current PulmoLearn source cites the 2025 guideline as permitting 30–60 inflations per minute. Adjust pressure to produce gentle visible chest movement and—most importantly—a rising heart rate. Do not chase a specific pressure number when lung inflation is clearly inadequate or excessive.
Visible chest movement helps confirm lung inflation, but your source repeatedly identifies a rising heart rate as the most important physiologic response to effective neonatal ventilation.
Mask adjustment → Reposition airway → Suction mouth/nose if obstruction is suspected → Open mouth → Pressure increase in appropriate increments → Alternative airway. The goal is not memorizing letters; it is achieving effective lung inflation.
Place the pulse-oximeter sensor on the right hand or wrist for a preductal measurement. Use the trend and clinical response to guide oxygen titration. Do not assume that every newborn should immediately receive a fixed high oxygen concentration.
Your scenario source states that ECG provides the fastest reliable heart-rate assessment during advanced neonatal resuscitation and is recommended before chest compressions. Clinical heart-rate assessment still begins immediately while monitoring is applied.
An endotracheal tube is especially appropriate when mask ventilation remains ineffective, prolonged ventilation is expected, airway obstruction requires access below the cords, or chest compressions are needed. A laryngeal mask is an alternative when face-mask ventilation is unsuccessful and intubation is not feasible or unsuccessful in an appropriately sized infant.
The NBRC outline specifically includes self-inflating, flow-inflating, and T-piece resuscitators under resuscitation equipment. Before delivery, confirm that the selected device, gas source, mask/interface, suction, oxygen-blending/monitoring equipment, and backup airway equipment are assembled and functional. Your PulmoLearn source specifically defines a T-piece resuscitator as a device that can deliver controlled inspiratory pressure and PEEP.
If there is no chest movement, do not immediately assume the infant needs compressions. Check mask seal/position, airway position, obstruction, mouth position, delivered pressure, gas/device function, and whether an alternative airway is needed. The device must actually deliver effective ventilation.
If heart rate remains below 60/min after at least 30 seconds of effective ventilation with visible chest movement, escalation to coordinated chest compressions is indicated. NEO-04 owns the 3:1 compression sequence, medications, access, and advanced-resuscitation details.
