Respiratory Distress Syndrome & Surfactant Deficiency

Newborn respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) is primarily a disease of prematurity. Immature type II pneumocytes produce too little surfactant, increasing alveolar surface tension. Alveoli collapse at end expiration, compliance and functional residual capacity fall, work of breathing rises, and gas exchange worsens. Early noninvasive distending pressure, selective surfactant, and careful escalation are the core treatment decisions.

RDS treatment framework

Recruit the lung, replace the missing surfactant, reassess early

Recognize classic RDS

Prematurity plus early progressive distress, tachypnea, grunting, flaring, retractions, increasing oxygen need, and a low-volume diffuse ground-glass/reticulogranular chest x-ray with air bronchograms strongly supports RDS.

Start with noninvasive support when feasible

Your PulmoLearn source favors keeping spontaneously breathing preterm infants on CPAP or another noninvasive strategy when feasible. CPAP/PEEP helps maintain functional residual capacity and limits repeated alveolar collapse.

Consider selective early surfactant

If oxygen need and distress rise despite effective CPAP, reassess interface/device function and discuss selective surfactant rather than waiting for severe failure. Surfactant directly addresses the underlying deficiency.

Escalate when support is failing

Persistent hypoxemia, rising PaCO₂, respiratory acidosis, apnea, bradycardia, severe work of breathing, or CPAP failure support escalation toward invasive ventilation and broader evaluation.

Board logic: RDS is a distending-pressure + surfactant-deficiency problem. Oxygen alone does not correct alveolar collapse.

Pathophysiology, support & surfactant

Review each concept

Immature type II pneumocytes do not produce enough surfactant. Surface tension rises, alveoli become unstable and collapse at end expiration, compliance falls, functional residual capacity falls, and the infant must generate more pressure to reopen collapsed alveoli.
Atelectasis produces low-volume, poorly ventilated lung units and V/Q mismatch. Early RDS commonly produces hypoxemia. As fatigue or failure develops, PaCO₂ may rise and respiratory acidosis may appear.
High-yield findings in your source include prematurity, early tachypnea, grunting, nasal flaring, retractions, cyanosis, reduced aeration, and increasing oxygen requirement. Apnea or bradycardia suggests more severe disease or fatigue.
Your course repeatedly describes RDS as low lung volumes with diffuse reticulogranular/ground-glass opacity and air bronchograms. This differs from the hyperinflation, perihilar streaking, and fissural fluid pattern emphasized for TTN.
The RT treatment frame in your source calls RDS a distending-pressure problem. CPAP or PEEP helps preserve functional residual capacity, reduce repeated end-expiratory alveolar collapse, improve oxygenation, and reduce work of breathing when spontaneous breathing is adequate.
Rising FiO₂ requirement despite adequate CPAP, persistent severe retractions, refractory hypoxemia, rising PaCO₂, worsening respiratory acidosis, apnea, or bradycardia should trigger immediate reassessment. Verify interface and equipment first, then recommend surfactant and/or higher respiratory support as appropriate.
Bronchodilators do not correct surfactant deficiency. When the RDS pattern and support needs indicate surfactant therapy, recommend/prepare for surfactant according to the neonatal protocol and provider order because it addresses the underlying alveolar-instability mechanism.
The registry maps this lesson to endotracheal instillation. Your PulmoLearn source requires the RT to assist with surfactant preparation/delivery, ensure surfactant-delivery and airway equipment are ready, and monitor the infant closely during and after administration. Exact product dose and technique are not specified in the retrieved source set.
Your source specifically directs monitoring for post-dose changes in compliance, oxygenation, ventilation, and pressure needs. A rapidly improving lung may need prompt adjustment of oxygen or ventilatory pressure to avoid excessive support.
Invasive support becomes appropriate when noninvasive support is failing or the infant develops persistent hypoxemia, rising PaCO₂/acidosis, apnea, bradycardia, worsening fatigue, or other respiratory-failure features. Use lung-protective ventilation and reassess frequently.
Do not leave the plan unchanged while support requirements rise. Reassess gestational age, work of breathing, oxygen requirement, CPAP effectiveness, blood gas trend, chest imaging, apnea/bradycardia, and response to prior interventions; then recommend the next level of support or surfactant discussion.

Recommend the next change before severe failure

27-week infant on CPAP:
  • Persistent grunting and severe retractions
  • FiO₂ requirement has risen to 0.50 to maintain the target SpO₂ range
  • CXR shows low volumes, diffuse granular/ground-glass opacities, and air bronchograms
  • CPAP interface and device function have been verified
