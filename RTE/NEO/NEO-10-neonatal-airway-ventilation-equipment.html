Neonatal Airway, Ventilation & Equipment

Neonatal respiratory support is a complete system: infant → airway or interface → circuit → device → monitoring. Because neonatal tidal volumes are small, small airway-position changes, leaks, condensate, poor humidification, or added apparatus dead space can have outsized effects. Before increasing support, verify that the airway and equipment are actually delivering what you think they are delivering.

System framework

Patient → airway/interface → circuit → device/data

Neonatal ETT

Use gestational age and weight with the unit-approved neonatal airway reference for size. Track lip/gum depth, but do not use depth alone to prove correct placement.

Verification

Use exhaled CO₂, chest movement, bilateral breath sounds, physiologic response, pressure/volume behavior, and radiograph. Recheck after retaping, repositioning, transport, procedures, or unexplained change.

Circuit & interface

Check seal, patency, connections, condensate, humidification, sensors, alarms, and unnecessary apparatus dead space.

Ventilator strategy

Choose support based on oxygenation, lung volume, ventilation, respiratory drive, airway protection, and disease physiology—not device familiarity.

Core troubleshooting rule: correct the system first, then change settings. Low pressure + low exhaled VT suggests disconnection/leak/airway displacement; high pressure suggests obstruction, secretions, worse compliance, pneumothorax, or interaction problems.
Source boundary: your neonatal competency source intentionally does not teach one universal ETT-size or depth formula. Use gestational age/weight plus the unit-approved neonatal airway reference.
Airway, circuits & ventilation

Review each concept

The source explicitly rejects one universal formula. Select neonatal ETT size from gestational age and weight using the approved neonatal airway chart for the clinical setting.
Lip/gum markings help trend position. The goal is mid-trachea with the head neutral and the tip above the carina, but an external depth number alone does not confirm correct placement.
Use exhaled CO₂, visible chest rise, bilateral breath sounds, physiologic improvement, pressure/volume behavior, and radiograph. Recheck after retaping, repositioning, transport, procedures, or unexplained changes.
A neonatal airway is short and small, so modest movement can create major displacement. Secure carefully while protecting fragile skin; unexplained deterioration after movement should prompt an airway-position check.
Your competency material specifically says to reduce circuit dead space. Added connectors or unnecessary apparatus consume a larger fraction of each neonatal breath and can worsen effective CO₂ clearance.
Heated humidification helps prevent drying, thick secretions, and increased airway resistance. Check humidifier function, temperature, condensate, and secretion burden when support appears ineffective.
Verify interface seal, delivered pressure, circuit, humidification, backup breaths when applicable, alarms, nasal obstruction, gastric distention, skin injury, and synchrony. A leak or poor fit can make therapy look inadequate.
FiO₂ changes inspired oxygen. PEEP supports end-expiratory lung volume. PIP/inspiratory pressure drives VT in pressure ventilation. Rate changes mandatory breath frequency/minute ventilation; inspiratory time affects filling and mean airway pressure.
RDS needs recruitment/PEEP while limiting excessive pressure/volume. MAS needs time to fill and exhale; excessive rate worsens air trapping. Apnea/relatively normal lungs need reliable backup ventilation with the minimum effective support.
Missed efforts can reflect insensitive triggering, weak drive, obstruction, or leak. Auto-triggering can reflect excessive sensitivity, leak, condensate, or circuit movement. Compare visible effort with waveform/trigger behavior.
On HFOV, mean airway pressure is the primary recruitment/oxygenation control. Amplitude is a major determinant of oscillatory tidal volume and CO₂ clearance. If oxygenation is acceptable but PaCO₂ rises, separate the ventilation problem from the recruitment problem before increasing MAP.
Use the lowest effective support while monitoring exhaled VT, pressures, compliance, chest movement, gases, oxygen need, and imaging. Prevent volutrauma, barotrauma, atelectrauma, excess dead space, airway injury, unnecessary disconnections, and unplanned extubation.
