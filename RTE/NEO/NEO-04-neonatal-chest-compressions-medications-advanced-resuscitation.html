Neonatal Chest Compressions, Medications & Advanced Resuscitation

Chest compressions are not the first response to neonatal bradycardia. They begin only when the heart rate remains below 60/min despite at least 30 seconds of ventilation that visibly inflates the lungs. Once compressions begin, coordinate a 3:1 compression-to-ventilation rhythm, use an advanced airway and 100% oxygen, reassess after about 60 seconds, and escalate to epinephrine when severe bradycardia persists.

Escalate in sequence

Advanced resuscitation framework

Ventilate → compress → reassess → medicate

1. Confirm effective ventilation

Do not begin compressions for a heart rate below 60/min until the newborn has received at least 30 seconds of ventilation that visibly inflates the lungs. If ventilation is ineffective, return to ventilation corrective steps.

2. Begin coordinated compressions

Use an advanced airway, 100% oxygen, and a 3:1 compression-to-ventilation ratio. Your source teaches the two-thumb encircling-hands technique over the lower third of the sternum.

3. Reassess after about 60 seconds

Continue coordinated compressions and ventilation for approximately 60 seconds, then reassess heart rate. Improvement changes the next step; persistent severe bradycardia requires escalation.

4. Epinephrine / access / volume

If heart rate remains below 60/min after coordinated compressions and ventilation, prepare intravascular epinephrine. Your source identifies the umbilical venous catheter (UVC) as the preferred delivery-room route. Consider volume expansion when blood loss or shock is suspected.

Priority sequence: ineffective ventilation is fixed before compressions; persistent bradycardia after effective ventilation triggers compressions; persistent bradycardia after coordinated compressions/ventilation triggers epinephrine.

Advanced resuscitation decisions

Review each concept

Your PulmoLearn source is explicit: start chest compressions when heart rate remains below 60/min despite at least 30 seconds of ventilation that visibly inflates the lungs. This prevents premature escalation when the real problem is still inadequate ventilation.
An advanced airway improves the reliability of ventilation while the team performs coordinated compressions. Your source identifies chest compressions as an indication for endotracheal intubation when feasible.
Your current neonatal resuscitation source directs the team to use 100% oxygen once coordinated chest compressions begin. This is a specific advanced-resuscitation step, not the same as routine oxygen management during initial PPV.
Coordinate three compressions followed by one ventilation. The purpose is synchronized cardiopulmonary support in a newborn whose severe bradycardia has persisted despite effective ventilation.
Your PulmoLearn source teaches the two-thumb encircling-hands technique over the lower third of the sternum. Technique and ventilation should remain coordinated rather than becoming two independent tasks.
After about 60 seconds of coordinated compressions and ventilation, reassess heart rate. The next action depends on the response. Do not continue the same sequence indefinitely without checking whether circulation is improving.
If the heart rate remains below 60/min after coordinated compressions and ventilation, your source directs the team to give epinephrine through an intravascular route. This is an escalation after ventilation and compressions have both been made effective.
Your source identifies an umbilical venous catheter as the preferred route for intravascular epinephrine during delivery-room resuscitation. The key exam concept is route/access priority, not a device-specific insertion procedure in this lesson.
Do not give volume simply because the heart rate is low. Your source reserves volume expansion for suspected blood loss or shock and lists isotonic crystalloid or O-negative blood as appropriate options depending on the clinical situation.
Your faculty source directs immediate communication for persistent apnea, persistent bradycardia, absent chest movement, or poor response to interventions. Advanced resuscitation depends on clear roles, closed-loop communication, and rapid preparation of airway, vascular, medication, and monitoring resources.
Recovery does not end the event. Continue monitoring heart rate, breathing, oxygenation, temperature, perfusion, glucose/metabolic stability, and neurologic status; document the intervention sequence and response, communicate the course during handoff, and debrief the team.
