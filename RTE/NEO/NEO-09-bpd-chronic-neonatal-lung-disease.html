BPD & Chronic Neonatal Lung Disease

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is chronic neonatal lung disease that develops in the setting of extreme prematurity and disrupted lung development. Your PulmoLearn sources frame the injury as multifactorial: prematurity plus inflammation, oxygen exposure, pressure/volume injury, infection, and prolonged respiratory support can impair normal alveolar and pulmonary vascular development. The RT’s job is to determine how well the infant is compensating now—not simply recognize the history.

BPD condition-determination framework

Trend the chronic respiratory burden across systems

How did the lung get here?

Extreme prematurity interrupts normal lung growth. Inflammation, oxygen exposure, pressure/volume injury, infection, and prolonged support add cumulative injury. Your source describes disrupted alveolar and pulmonary vascular development rather than a single-cause disease.

How hard is the infant working now?

Trend oxygen requirement, ordered saturation goals, work of breathing, respiratory support, blood-gas/CO₂ pattern, apnea/bradycardia, fatigue, and exacerbations. A change in trajectory matters more than one isolated number.

Is growth/feeding destabilizing the lung?

Breathing consumes energy. Poor growth reduces reserve. Feeding intolerance and aspiration risk can worsen respiratory stability and increase support needs.

Is a complication emerging?

Increasing oxygen need, rising PaCO₂, fatigue, poor feeding, recurrent illness, or pulmonary-hypertension clues should trigger broader reassessment rather than simply increasing support.

Key exam principle: chronic oxygen dependence is only one piece of BPD. Determine the infant’s condition from respiratory trends, growth, feeding/aspiration, systemic stability, and pulmonary vascular risk together.
Scope boundary: the retrieved PulmoLearn sources do not provide a single diagnostic-age or oxygen-duration threshold for defining BPD severity in this lesson set. NEO-09 therefore focuses on the source-supported disease pattern and clinical condition determination rather than inventing a numeric classification.
Chronic lung injury & ongoing assessment

Review each concept

Your competency notes state that BPD risk reflects extreme prematurity plus inflammation, oxygen exposure, pressure/volume injury, infection, and prolonged support. The prevention principle is cumulative: use the least injurious effective respiratory support and pursue liberation when safe.
The PulmoLearn Chronic Lung Disease of Infancy lesson specifically links BPD with alveolar simplification and pulmonary vascular remodeling. The practical implication is reduced respiratory reserve and ongoing risk for oxygen dependence and pulmonary hypertension.
Monitor oxygen requirement and ordered saturation goals, work of breathing, exacerbations, and ongoing support needs. Chronic care is not a one-time “home oxygen” decision; trends show whether the infant is stable, improving, or losing reserve.
Your BPD source lists rising PaCO₂ among deterioration clues. Interpret it with pH, respiratory effort, fatigue, oxygen requirement, support level, and the broader clinical trajectory rather than treating the number in isolation.
Your source includes oxygen, humidification, HFNC, CPAP, mechanical ventilation, and chronic respiratory technology as possible support concepts. Match the support to the infant’s current need while minimizing unnecessary oxygen, pressure, and volume injury.
The chronic-care notes emphasize that growth and nutrition directly affect respiratory stability. Increased work of breathing consumes energy; poor growth leaves less reserve for illness, feeding, and respiratory effort.
Feeding fatigue, desaturation, and aspiration risk can destabilize chronic lung disease. A worsening respiratory pattern around feeds should not be interpreted as “just BPD” without considering feeding mechanics and aspiration.
Pulmonary vascular remodeling and chronic lung disease increase pulmonary-hypertension risk. Your sources repeatedly include pulmonary hypertension surveillance as part of chronic BPD monitoring and follow-up.
High-yield worsening clues in your source include increasing oxygen requirement, rising PaCO₂, apnea, bradycardia, increased work of breathing, fatigue, poor feeding, recurrent illness, and pulmonary-hypertension clues. A sudden deterioration also requires immediate troubleshooting for airway/equipment problems or pneumothorax rather than assuming gradual BPD progression.
Your advanced neonatal notes connect excess oxygen, unnecessary pressure/volume, unstable support, infection, and prolonged invasive ventilation with chronic injury risk. Avoiding volutrauma/barotrauma and unnecessary oxygen while maintaining adequate recruitment and gas exchange is part of BPD prevention.
Coordinate oxygen/respiratory monitoring with growth and nutrition, aspiration assessment, pulmonary hypertension surveillance, developmental follow-up, immunization, hearing/vision care, and specialty appointments. Chronic respiratory stability depends on more than the lungs alone.
Review the course of prematurity and respiratory support, then integrate current SpO₂/oxygen requirement, work of breathing, blood gases, support level, growth, feeding tolerance, aspiration risk, exacerbations, and pulmonary-hypertension clues. The question is: Is this infant chronically stable, slowly improving, or deteriorating?
