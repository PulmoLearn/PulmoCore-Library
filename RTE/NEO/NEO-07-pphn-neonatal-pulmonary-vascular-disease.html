PPHN & Neonatal Pulmonary Vascular Disease

Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN) is a failure of the normal post-birth fall in pulmonary vascular resistance. Pulmonary blood flow stays limited and fetal-style right-to-left shunting may continue across the ductus arteriosus and/or foramen ovale. The result is severe, often labile hypoxemia that can be worse than the lung disease alone would predict.

Socratic PPHN recognition string

Is the hypoxemia explained by lung disease alone?

Condition-determination framework

Recognize persistent fetal-style circulation after birth

Mechanism

Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) stays abnormally high after birth. Instead of sending increasing blood flow through the lungs, the circulation continues to favor fetal-style right-to-left shunting.

Oxygenation clue

Hypoxemia may be severe or fluctuate rapidly and can be out of proportion to the apparent lung disease. Handling or agitation may worsen oxygenation because pulmonary vascular tone can change abruptly.

Pre/postductal clue

Preductal saturation higher than postductal saturation supports right-to-left ductal shunting. However, your source explicitly warns that the absence of a differential does not exclude PPHN.

Confirmation

Echocardiography evaluates pulmonary hypertension, shunt direction, ventricular function, and structural heart disease. It is essential because critical congenital heart disease can also cause severe neonatal hypoxemia.

Best reasoning sequence: verify oxygenation → compare pre/postductal SpO₂ → assess lung disease and recruitment → review hemodynamics/ABG → evaluate shunt physiology → confirm with echocardiography.
Source boundary: the PulmoLearn sources describe a “meaningful” pre/postductal difference but do not provide one universal numeric cutoff in the retrieved NEO-07 material. This lesson therefore teaches pattern recognition rather than inventing a saturation-gap threshold.
PPHN recognition & pulmonary vascular physiology

Review each concept

Normally, lung expansion and increasing oxygen lower PVR after birth, pulmonary blood flow rises, and fetal shunts functionally close. In PPHN, pulmonary vascular relaxation fails or is inadequate, so PVR remains high and fetal pressure relationships persist.
When right-sided pressures remain high, blood may move right-to-left across the ductus arteriosus and/or foramen ovale. That blood enters the systemic circulation without adequate pulmonary oxygenation, producing shunt-type hypoxemia.
Preductal SpO₂ is obtained from the right hand/wrist. Postductal SpO₂ is measured from a lower extremity. A higher preductal than postductal saturation supports right-to-left ductal shunting because deoxygenated blood enters the descending aorta through the ductus arteriosus.
Your source explicitly states that the absence of a pre/postductal difference does not exclude PPHN. Shunting may occur mainly at the foramen ovale, may vary over time, or may not create a large ductal differential at the moment it is measured.
Rapid changes in oxygen saturation with handling or agitation are a classic pulmonary-vascular clue in your course. Pulmonary vasoconstriction can worsen abruptly, decreasing pulmonary blood flow and increasing right-to-left shunting.
If oxygenation is much worse than the degree of parenchymal disease on imaging would predict, consider vascular shunt physiology or structural heart disease rather than assuming that alveolar disease alone explains the problem.
Your sources specifically associate PPHN with severe meconium aspiration, pneumonia, RDS, and pulmonary hypoplasia. Determine and treat the underlying lung problem while also recognizing the pulmonary vascular component.
Your neonatal source identifies echocardiography as the test that confirms pulmonary hypertension, evaluates shunt direction and ventricular function, and helps exclude congenital structural heart disease.
Your source emphasizes correcting contributors that increase PVR: optimize lung recruitment and ventilation, correct hypoxemia and acidosis, maintain temperature and glucose, support systemic blood pressure/perfusion, and minimize unnecessary agitation.
The competency source identifies inhaled nitric oxide as the specialty gas used when pulmonary hypertension physiology is present. It selectively dilates the pulmonary vasculature, helping reduce PVR and right-to-left shunting when the lungs are adequately recruited and the underlying problem is being treated.
After iNO begins, assess oxygenation response and the overall hemodynamic/respiratory picture. Failure to improve may suggest inadequate lung recruitment or structural disease. Your source specifically warns that abrupt iNO discontinuation can cause rebound pulmonary hypertension, so weaning should be gradual and monitored.
Use the history and underlying disease, degree and variability of hypoxemia, pre/postductal saturation pattern, blood gas trend, chest imaging, perfusion/hemodynamics, response to lung recruitment, and echocardiographic findings together. PPHN is not diagnosed from one low SpO₂ value.
