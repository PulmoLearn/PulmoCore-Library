TTN, Meconium Aspiration & Neonatal Pneumonia

Neonatal respiratory distress is a syndrome, not a diagnosis. Use the same comparison every time: history → mechanism → bedside pattern → oxygenation/ventilation → imaging → course. TTN points toward retained fetal lung fluid, MAS toward meconium exposure with obstruction/inflammatory injury, and pneumonia toward infection risk plus respiratory and systemic illness.

Primary: I.A.8 Determination of a patient’s condition
TTNTerm/late-preterm, often C-section without labor; retained fluid; milder early tachypnea; hyperinflation/perihilar streaking/fluid in fissures.
MASMeconium exposure; airway obstruction + inflammation; coarse/patchy disease, possible hyperinflation, PPHN and air-leak risk.
PneumoniaMaternal infection/prolonged rupture/systemic illness; infectious-inflammatory parenchymal disease; integrate labs, imaging, perfusion, temperature and course.
Socratic differential string

Which diagnosis fits the whole pattern?

Differential framework

Separate the three disorders by mechanism and context

TTN: retained fetal lung fluid

Typical context is term or late-preterm birth shortly after delivery, especially scheduled cesarean delivery without labor. Expect tachypnea with generally milder distress and a course that should improve rather than progressively worsen.

MAS: obstruction + inflammatory injury

Meconium-stained fluid plus respiratory distress supports MAS. Partial airway obstruction can create air trapping/hyperinflation; inflammatory and surfactant effects add atelectasis and V/Q mismatch.

Pneumonia: infection/inflammation

Maternal infection, chorioamnionitis, prolonged rupture of membranes, temperature instability, poor perfusion, systemic illness, and respiratory distress shift concern toward neonatal pneumonia/sepsis rather than uncomplicated TTN.

Trend matters: uncomplicated TTN should move toward improvement. A rising FiO₂ requirement, worsening acidosis, poor perfusion, temperature instability, apnea, severe retractions, or failure to improve should make you reopen the differential.
High-yield discriminators

Review each clue

Your TTN source emphasizes term/late-preterm birth, cesarean delivery without labor, and maternal diabetes as useful context. Delayed fetal lung-fluid clearance lowers compliance, causes mild V/Q mismatch, and produces tachypnea and increased work of breathing soon after birth.
The course’s high-yield TTN x-ray pattern is hyperinflation with perihilar streaking and fluid in the fissures. This contrasts with the low-volume diffuse ground-glass pattern of RDS and the coarse/patchy hyperinflated pattern that may occur with MAS.
TTN is generally milder and should improve over a short course. Supportive oxygen and monitoring may be enough; CPAP can be anticipated when tachypnea, grunting, or work of breathing persists despite low-flow oxygen. Rising support needs or failure to improve argue against simply labeling the infant “uncomplicated TTN.”
Immediately connect meconium-stained amniotic fluid—especially in a term/post-term or fetal-stress context—with MAS risk when respiratory distress is present after birth.
Meconium can obstruct airways, irritate lung tissue, inactivate surfactant, and trigger pulmonary vasoconstriction. Partial “ball-valve” obstruction can allow air in but limit exhalation, causing air trapping, hyperinflation, and increased pneumothorax risk, while complete obstruction can contribute to atelectasis.
Your source emphasizes tachypnea, grunting, retractions, cyanosis, coarse breath sounds, low SpO₂, and patchy/coarse infiltrates with possible hyperinflation. Severe cases can have refractory hypoxemia, acidosis, PPHN, air leak, apnea, or shock.
Maternal infection/chorioamnionitis and prolonged rupture of membranes are key risk clues in your neonatal competency notes. Respiratory distress plus temperature instability, poor perfusion, or other systemic illness findings should increase concern for neonatal pneumonia/sepsis.
Your source intentionally teaches pneumonia as an integrated pattern: use maternal/delivery history, respiratory distress, systemic illness, labs, imaging, oxygen need, blood-gas trend, perfusion, temperature, and clinical course together. The retrieved source does not give one unique pneumonia x-ray pattern that safely separates every case from RDS or TTN.
TTN: retained-fluid context + milder early tachypnea + hyperinflation/perihilar streaking/fissural fluid + improvement. MAS: meconium exposure + coarse/patchy disease ± hyperinflation + air-trapping/PPHN/air-leak risk. Pneumonia: infection risk + respiratory distress + systemic illness pattern + supportive labs/imaging/course.
Escalation triggers in your sources include rising FiO₂ need, severe retractions, apnea, poor perfusion, temperature instability, pre/postductal saturation difference, worsening acidosis, refractory hypoxemia, or failure to improve. Sudden asymmetric deterioration should also trigger evaluation for an air leak rather than being attributed to gradual disease progression.
The RTE task is not simply recalling disease definitions. Review gestational age, delivery mode, meconium exposure, maternal infection/rupture history, timing of symptoms, work of breathing, oxygen requirement, SpO₂ trend, blood gas, imaging, perfusion, temperature, and trajectory—then identify the most likely condition and competing diagnosis.
Integrated RTE-style differential case

Use the history and trajectory to challenge the first diagnosis

Term infant after scheduled cesarean delivery without labor:
  • Tachypnea and mild retractions begin shortly after birth
  • CXR: hyperinflation, perihilar streaking, fluid in fissures
  • Initial oxygen need is modest
  • Six hours later: FiO₂ need is rising, perfusion is worsening, and temperature is unstable
