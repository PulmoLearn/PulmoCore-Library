Apnea of Prematurity & Neonatal Respiratory Control

Apnea of prematurity reflects immature respiratory control in a premature infant and may appear as central, obstructive, or mixed pauses. Your course source defines neonatal apnea as a breathing pause lasting more than 20 seconds, often with bradycardia, cyanosis, or pallor. The RT must verify the event on continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring, distinguish the pattern, evaluate whether a secondary problem is contributing, and reassess the response to caffeine or respiratory support.

Socratic event string

Is this apnea—and what kind?

Apnea-monitoring framework

Verify the event before treating the label

1. Confirm the event

Use continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring to identify the pause and correlate it with heart-rate and oxygenation change. Do not treat a monitor alarm as a diagnosis without checking the infant and the monitoring signal.

2. Classify the pattern

Central apnea has absent respiratory effort. Obstructive apnea has persistent effort with impaired airflow. Mixed apnea contains both components.

3. Assess severity

Look at duration, recurrence, associated bradycardia, cyanosis/pallor or desaturation, recovery, need for stimulation, and whether the infant can sustain spontaneous breathing between events.

4. Ask why now?

Prematurity may explain recurrent apnea, but a new or worsening pattern should prompt reassessment for secondary illness, recent sedation, respiratory deterioration, or other multisystem problems rather than being automatically attributed to immature control.

Board logic: monitor → verify → classify → evaluate cause → treat → reassess the apnea burden and physiologic response.
Source boundary: the retrieved sources support caffeine/xanthine therapy, noninvasive respiratory support, continuous monitoring, and evaluation of secondary causes, but they do not provide a caffeine dose or a complete medication protocol. This lesson therefore teaches indication/response rather than dosing.
Respiratory control, monitoring & treatment

Review each concept

Your neonatal source states that apnea of prematurity results from immature respiratory control and may be central, obstructive, or mixed. It is most strongly associated with prematurity rather than being a normal finding in a mature infant.
The retrieved AHRC 262 assignment defines neonatal apnea as a pause in breathing lasting more than 20 seconds, often accompanied by bradycardia, cyanosis, or pallor. Use this course definition for this review lesson.
In central apnea, respiratory effort stops during the event because the infant does not generate a breath. This matches the registry focus on distinguishing central apnea from other neonatal events.
In obstructive apnea, respiratory effort persists but airflow is blocked. The key distinction is that the infant is attempting to breathe even though effective airflow is not occurring.
Mixed apnea contains both central and obstructive components in one event. Your PulmoLearn neonatal disease source explicitly teaches that apnea of prematurity can be central, obstructive, or mixed.
Use continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring and correlate the respiratory pause with heart-rate and oxygenation change. Review the event duration, frequency, bradycardia/desaturation pattern, recovery, and whether stimulation or respiratory support was required.
A respiratory pause plus bradycardia or desaturation is more clinically important than an isolated unverified alarm. Determine whether the pattern is recurrent, whether respiratory effort is absent or persistent, and whether the event burden is increasing over time.
Your neonatal curriculum repeatedly treats worsening apnea as a reason to reassess the whole infant. Recent sedation can depress respiratory drive; infection or multisystem deterioration may increase respiratory support needs; and NEC may present with abdominal distention, apnea, worsening acidosis, and ventilation difficulty.
The registry specifically requires caffeine therapy in this lesson, and your neonatal competency material identifies caffeine support as part of managing premature infants with weak respiratory drive/apnea burden. Caffeine is used to support respiratory drive; the retrieved sources do not provide a dosing protocol, so dose calculations are intentionally not taught here.
Your AHRC source lists noninvasive ventilation among treatments for neonatal apnea. If events persist despite stimulation/medical therapy or the infant cannot maintain gas exchange, noninvasive support may be required and should be reassessed for effectiveness.
Your advanced neonatal competency guide says low ventilator settings alone do not prove extubation readiness. Assess respiratory drive and recent apnea burden, including recent sedation and caffeine therapy, before deciding the infant can sustain spontaneous breathing after extubation.
After caffeine or respiratory-support changes, reassess event frequency, duration, bradycardia/desaturation, need for stimulation, blood-gas/oxygenation trend when indicated, and whether spontaneous respiratory drive is becoming more reliable.
Integrated RTE-style apnea case

Do not anchor on prematurity when the pattern changes

29-week infant with previously stable apnea of prematurity:
  • Now has a sudden increase in apnea events with recurrent bradycardia
  • Recent caffeine therapy is documented
  • Abdomen is newly distended
  • Acidosis is worsening
  • Ventilation support requirement is increasing
