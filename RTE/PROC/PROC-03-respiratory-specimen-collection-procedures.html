Respiratory Specimen Collection Procedures

A useful respiratory result starts with the right source and a clean collection. Expectorated or induced sputum, endotracheal aspirate (ETA), blind mini-bronchoalveolar lavage (mini-BAL), bronchoscopic bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), and biopsy answer different questions. Choose a safe route, check sample quality, and send the correctly labeled specimen for the ordered test.

Which sample will answer the question?

Specimen-selection framework

Question → route → quality → handling

Noninvasive or airway sample

A patient who can cough may provide expectorated sputum. If unable, ordered sputum induction stimulates a deep cough. An intubated patient can provide an ETA through the artificial airway; ETA is generally the initial sampling approach in suspected ventilator-associated pneumonia.

Sampling farther down

Mini-BAL uses a catheter for blind distal airway sampling without visual selection of a segment. Bronchoscopic BAL places a scope in a chosen segment, instills saline, and retrieves fluid. A visible lesion or tissue question may require biopsy rather than fluid alone.

Quality before interpretation

Deep-cough sputum is different from saliva. Many squamous epithelial cells on Gram stain can suggest oral contamination; ask whether the sample meets lab criteria. A positive airway culture can reflect colonization, so interpret it with symptoms, imaging, and other findings.

Safe collection and handoff

Sputum induction can provoke bronchospasm and generate aerosols. Assess breathing and infection precautions before starting. Use the correct sterile container and test order, record patient and site or source, close the container, use biohazard transport, and send promptly per lab policy.

Core rule: the specimen must match the suspected site and requested test. A deeper sample is not automatically better if its procedural risk outweighs the likely benefit.
Team cue: confirm the order, patient condition, isolation plan, technique, collection site, and lab requirements. Never guess a missing label or substitute saliva for lower-airway sputum.
Sampling and specimen quality

Review each concept

Coach a deep cough into a sterile container. Sputum comes from the lower airways; a mostly watery mouth sample may not answer a lower-respiratory question. Follow local rejection criteria.
When sputum cannot be produced, a protocol may use nebulized hypertonic saline to trigger cough. Check baseline breathing and oxygenation, bronchospasm risk, and ordered bronchodilator support; monitor during collection.
Induction can generate infectious aerosols. For suspected pulmonary tuberculosis (TB), use the specified airborne-infection isolation space and protective equipment; check local infection-control rules for the suspected pathogen.
An ETA collects secretions through an endotracheal tube. For suspected ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), noninvasive ETA with semiquantitative culture is generally preferred to routine invasive sampling.
A mini-BAL catheter passes through the artificial airway to obtain a distal fluid sample without a bronchoscope selecting the exact segment. Follow the device protocol and monitor ventilation, oxygenation, and tolerance.
For BAL, the provider wedges a bronchoscope in the chosen segment, instills sterile saline, and collects returned fluid. Confirm the site and requested microbiology or cell tests before preparing containers.
A biopsy collects tissue for pathology and sometimes organism testing. It has distinct bleeding and, for some approaches, pneumothorax risks; fluid from BAL cannot replace a requested tissue diagnosis.
Note amount, color, odor, and appearance, but none identifies an organism alone. Gram stain with many squamous epithelial cells suggests contamination from the mouth; use lab criteria and clinical context.
A positive ETA or BAL culture does not automatically prove pneumonia: colonization is possible. Review fever, secretions, oxygen needs, imaging, collection quality, and the ordered test with the team.
Identify the patient and exact source or segment, collection time, and tests ordered. Seal the container, follow biohazard bag and PPE rules, and transport promptly under lab instructions; resolve labels before dispatch.

The first specimen misses the mark

  • Gram stain shows many squamous epithelial cells; the lab flags poor quality
