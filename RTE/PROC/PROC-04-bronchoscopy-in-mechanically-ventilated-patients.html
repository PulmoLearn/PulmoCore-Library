Bronchoscopy in Mechanically Ventilated Patients

Passing a bronchoscope through an endotracheal tube (ETT) leaves less room for air to move. Resistance rises; delivered tidal volume (VT), peak inspiratory pressure (PIP), oxygenation, and exhalation may change. The respiratory therapist prepares the circuit and ventilator, watches the patient and waveforms, and helps the team pause or adjust when ventilation is threatened.

Ventilated-bronchoscopy framework

Fit → closed circuit → waveforms → reassessment

Plan the fit

Confirm the ETT inner diameter and scope outer diameter. A scope that nearly fills the tube markedly restricts flow; guidance commonly calls for about 2 mm of clearance. Check airway security, cuff, baseline settings, and whether the planned procedure is tolerable.

Build a closed path

Use the compatible swivel or access adapter with a seal around the scope. Check tight connections, circuit integrity, oxygen source, ventilator alarms, and suction readiness. A loose adapter can lose tidal volume and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP).

Interpret the ventilator

With the scope in the ETT, higher resistance can raise PIP; a high-pressure limit may shorten delivery and reduce exhaled VT. In pressure control, flow and delivered VT may fall. Slower exhalation can cause air trapping and intrinsic PEEP; compare both patient and waveforms.

Support and reassess

Coordinate oxygen and ventilator changes with the provider and local protocol; preserve lung-protective goals. Watch oxygen saturation, exhaled VT, pressure, flow returning to zero, hemodynamics, and tolerance. Pause sampling and withdraw the scope when ventilation or oxygenation becomes unsafe.

Core rule: a rising peak pressure with falling delivered volume during scope passage is a ventilation problem until checked, not a reason to silence an alarm.
Team cue: make an explicit pause plan before insertion. If oxygenation or ventilation deteriorates, tell the provider, pause or withdraw the scope, support ventilation, and reassess tube, adapter, secretions, and settings before resuming.
Device and bedside checks

Review each concept

Compare the ETT inner diameter with bronchoscope outer diameter before setup. About 2 mm of clearance is commonly recommended; a snug scope can severely reduce the remaining path for airflow.
Clarify whether the provider will inspect the airway, remove a plug, or perform bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL). Plan sampling tools and labels, but also determine if the patient can tolerate scope time and suction.
A compatible sealed swivel adapter allows scope entry while limiting circuit disconnection. Verify correct assembly and secure fit. A damaged seal or loose connector may cause a leak and loss of PEEP.
Before insertion, note oxygen saturation, exhaled VT, PIP, plateau pressure if obtained, PEEP, fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), patient synchrony, and blood pressure. Compare the same measurements during and after the procedure.
The scope raises airway resistance and often PIP. PIP alone does not show alveolar pressure; compare with plateau pressure when appropriate. Do not simply raise the pressure alarm without assessing volume delivery and risk.
In volume-targeted ventilation, a pressure limit may cut a breath short. In pressure-targeted ventilation, the narrowed tube can lower delivered VT. In both cases, follow exhaled VT and patient status rather than relying only on the set value.
The bronchoscope also obstructs expiratory flow. Flow that does not return toward zero before the next breath can suggest air trapping and intrinsic PEEP. Ask for a pause or scope withdrawal if ventilation becomes unsafe.
Coordinate the oxygen plan with the team before insertion; some patients need a temporary increase in FiO2. Watch continuous pulse oximetry and clinical signs, and reassess after suction or BAL because oxygenation can worsen.
For falling exhaled VT, desaturation, or high pressure, alert the provider and pause. Withdraw the scope as needed, assess patient and circuit, restore effective ventilation, and identify the cause before continuing.
Remove the scope, check the adapter and circuit, restore the planned ventilator settings and alarms, confirm VT, pressures, oxygenation, and ETT position, and document patient response and any specimen handoff.
Integrated RTE-style procedure case

Pressure rises; volume falls

During BAL through an ETT, the ventilator reaches its high-pressure limit:
  • PIP rises after scope insertion, and exhaled VT falls
  • Oxygen saturation begins to drop
  • The scope nearly fills the ETT, and expiration is prolonged
