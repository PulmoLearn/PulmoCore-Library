Flexible, Rigid & Advanced Bronchoscopy Procedures

Bronchoscopy methods serve different targets. A flexible scope can inspect and sample airways. A rigid scope can help manage selected large central obstructions. Endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) guides a needle toward tissue beside the airway, while navigation helps reach some peripheral lung targets. Choose the method that fits the question and support the provider before, during, and after the procedure.

Which technique reaches the target?

Procedure-selection framework

Target → access route → sample or treatment → safety

Flexible versus rigid

A flexible bronchoscope bends through branching airways for inspection, lavage, and sampling. Rigid bronchoscopy provides a larger working channel and airway control for selected large foreign bodies, central obstruction, or heavy bleeding, often with deeper anesthesia and a specialized team.

Choose the sample

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) collects fluid from a selected lung region. Endobronchial biopsy samples a visible airway lesion. Transbronchial sampling reaches tissue beyond the airway wall; choice and bleeding or pneumothorax risk depend on the target.

EBUS and needle aspiration

Linear EBUS shows structures adjacent to the airway in real time. EBUS-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (TBNA) can sample mediastinal or hilar lymph nodes and some nearby masses for diagnosis or staging. Label each sampled site accurately.

Navigation to peripheral lesions

Electromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy (ENB) and related navigation tools use an image-based route to help a bronchoscope reach some peripheral nodules. Navigation aids access but does not guarantee a diagnostic specimen; the team may use other imaging or sampling methods.

Core rule: locate the target before choosing a scope. A central airway blockage, lymph node outside the airway, and peripheral lung nodule call for different tools and planning.
Team cue: confirm consent, imaging, oxygenation, bleeding risk, device size, sedation or anesthesia plan, labeled specimens, and rescue support. After sampling, reassess oxygenation and look for bleeding or pneumothorax when relevant.
Matching scope to target

Review each concept

A flexible scope bends into smaller bronchi for inspection and selected sampling or treatment. It still narrows the airway and can impair ventilation, especially through a narrow endotracheal tube.
A rigid scope can allow larger tools and airway control for certain large central foreign bodies, severe central obstruction, or significant airway bleeding. A trained team and anesthesia support are needed.
The provider instills sterile saline into a targeted segment and collects returned fluid. BAL is not a needle sample of a lymph node; verify specimen destination and site label.
Forceps through a scope can sample a visible endobronchial lesion. A biopsy differs from inspection or lavage and adds bleeding risk; tissue beyond the wall can also risk pneumothorax.
Linear EBUS combines a scope with ultrasound to view tissue outside the airway. It helps select a reachable lymph node or nearby mass for real-time needle sampling.
During EBUS-TBNA, the provider passes a needle through the airway wall into the target. Record node station or site and handle samples for the ordered tests.
A mediastinal or hilar node may need tissue to diagnose disease and inform lung-cancer staging. Imaging alone may not provide the same pathologic answer.
Navigation bronchoscopy uses a planned route from CT and tracking tools to approach a distant nodule. Small or hard-to-reach lesions may still yield a nondiagnostic sample.
Confirm appropriate scope, ultrasound or navigation system, needle or biopsy tools, image plan, specimen containers, oxygen and ventilation support, monitoring, and assigned procedural roles.
Track breathing, oxygen saturation, bleeding, and chest symptoms after needle or transbronchial biopsy. Escalate suspected pneumothorax or instability and follow the team’s imaging plan.
