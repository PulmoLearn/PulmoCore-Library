Bronchoscopy Complications & Postprocedure Assessment

Bronchoscopy does not end when the scope comes out. Hypoxemia, bronchospasm, airway bleeding, pneumothorax, and sedation-related breathing problems may appear during the procedure or recovery. Compare with baseline, recognize the pattern, stop or pause for serious deterioration, support breathing, and communicate a focused reassessment to the team.

Complication-response framework

Trend → likely cause → immediate action → recovery

Airway and oxygenation

Watch oxygen saturation, breathing effort, breath sounds, and ventilation. Falling oxygen may follow suction, lavage, or obstruction. New wheeze suggests bronchospasm; a quiet or tiring patient is not necessarily improving.

Bleeding and chest injury

Small blood-streaked sputum can occur after sampling, but persistent or brisk bleeding needs immediate escalation. New pleuritic pain, unilateral decreased breath sounds, or desaturation after transbronchial biopsy raises concern for pneumothorax.

Sedation and recovery

Moderate sedation requires ongoing attention to responsiveness, airway patency, breathing rate and depth, oxygenation, blood pressure, and recovery. Oxygen saturation can look acceptable while supplemental oxygen masks hypoventilation; use ventilation monitoring when indicated.

Stop, reassess, communicate

For severe adverse events, pause or terminate the procedure and help restore airway and breathing. Record the timing and trend, notify the provider, follow the rescue plan, and reassess before any return to baseline care or discharge.

Core rule: sustained desaturation, ineffective breathing, significant bleeding, hemodynamic instability, or suspected pneumothorax is a reason to stop or pause and escalate—not to continue a routine specimen collection.
Team cue: reassess symptoms, consciousness, vital signs, oxygen and ventilation, breath sounds, and procedure-specific risks. Use the provider’s imaging plan after biopsy or if pneumothorax is suspected; do not assume every patient needs routine imaging.
Complication recognition and recovery

Review each concept

A falling oxygen saturation during bronchoscopy may reflect suction, lavage, scope obstruction, sedation, or the patient’s illness. Pause the maneuver, assess airway and ventilation, provide ordered oxygen support, and notify the provider.
New wheeze with increased work of breathing and impaired air movement can signal bronchospasm. Alert the provider, pause airway stimulation, support oxygenation, and prepare prescribed bronchodilator or rescue measures.
Blood-streaked sputum can follow biopsy, but ongoing, brisk, or worsening bleeding can threaten airway patency. Escalate promptly and assist with airway and bleeding-control plans rather than treating all hemoptysis as minor.
New chest pain, dyspnea, one-sided reduced breath sounds, or low oxygen after a transbronchial biopsy warrants urgent assessment for pneumothorax. Follow the provider’s imaging and treatment plan; an unstable patient needs immediate response.
A patient who is difficult to arouse, breathing slowly or shallowly, or obstructing the airway may have sedation-related respiratory depression. Assess ventilation and airway patency, summon the responsible team, and follow the rescue protocol.
Trend respiratory rate and depth, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, heart rate, mental status, and discomfort. Ventilation monitoring such as capnography may help when indicated; oxygen saturation alone cannot exclude hypoventilation.
A low-grade fever can occur after lavage or bronchoscopy. Persistent or high fever, instability, or other new symptoms require evaluation; neither assume infection from a transient fever nor dismiss a worsening patient.
A severe or rapidly worsening complication warrants stopping or pausing the procedure and alerting the provider while supporting airway, breathing, and circulation. Resume only after the team reassesses risks and benefits.
Topical throat anesthesia and sedation can blunt protective reflexes. Follow facility instructions for return of swallowing and gag function and sufficient alertness before food or drink; do not use an arbitrary clock alone.
Compare findings with baseline, confirm oxygen and device needs, document specimens and complications, and communicate follow-up for bleeding, chest symptoms, fever, or worsening breathlessness before discharge or handoff.
