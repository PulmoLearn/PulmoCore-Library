Hospital-Acquired, Ventilator-Associated & Aspiration Infection

Preventing infection requires attention to the airway, oral care, aspiration risk, and time on a ventilator. Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) describe clinical infections. A ventilator-associated event (VAE) is a surveillance pattern of worsening oxygenation that can have several causes. Read the timeline and follow the prevention protocol.

Which prevention step matters now?

Prevent and interpret events

Timeline → prevention → surveillance

Classify the timeline

HAP starts at least 48 hours after hospital admission and was not developing at admission. VAP occurs more than 48 hours after endotracheal intubation. A new opacity and clinical signs still need assessment to support pneumonia.

Reduce aspiration and exposure

Use hand hygiene, appropriate head-of-bed elevation, oral care with toothbrushing, safe feeding, and swallowing assessment when needed. Maintain an artificial airway and suction secretions with clean technique under local protocol.

Reduce avoidable ventilator days

Use sedation-minimizing protocols or spontaneous awakening trials and assess readiness for spontaneous breathing trials when safe. The team determines whether extubation criteria are met; avoid unnecessary ventilator time.

Understand the VAE signal

Adult VAE surveillance begins with sustained worsening oxygenation after stable or improving ventilator settings. It may reflect infection, pulmonary edema, atelectasis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, or another cause. Investigate clinically; VAE is not a synonym for VAP.

Core rule: perform prevention steps every day, reassess readiness to reduce avoidable ventilation, and investigate deterioration. A VAE flags a change for surveillance; clinical pneumonia needs its own evaluation.
Protocol cue: elevate the head of the bed when appropriate, provide toothbrushing-based oral care, and change ventilator circuits when soiled, malfunctioning, or required by the manufacturer—not simply on a routine schedule.
Prevention and surveillance

Review each concept

A new pneumonia beginning at least 48 hours after hospital admission, with no evidence it was developing at admission, fits the HAP timeline. In the clinical HAP category, the patient is not on a ventilator.
Pneumonia occurring more than 48 hours after endotracheal intubation fits the VAP timeline. New respiratory findings call for clinical evaluation; a culture or fever alone does not establish the diagnosis.
Adult VAE surveillance tracks sustained increases in ventilator oxygen or pressure needs after a period of stability. Pulmonary edema, atelectasis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and pneumonia may all cause worsening.
Perform hand hygiene before and after airway contact. Follow local clean or sterile suction policy, protect equipment from contamination, and handle secretions and respiratory devices safely.
Head-of-bed elevation, commonly 30°–45° when safe, helps reduce aspiration risk. Recheck positioning after transport and procedures and follow individualized contraindications.
Scheduled oral care with toothbrushing reduces oral debris. Current adult prevention guidance does not advise routine chlorhexidine for VAP prevention; follow the current local protocol and patient needs.
Change a ventilator circuit when it is visibly soiled, malfunctioning, or directed by manufacturer instructions. Routine scheduled replacement offers no prevention benefit and adds handling.
A sedation protocol or spontaneous awakening trial, paired with an appropriate spontaneous breathing trial, can help shorten unnecessary ventilation. Assess safety and coordinate with the team each day.
Dysphagia, reduced alertness, vomiting, or trouble managing secretions raises aspiration risk. Use swallowing evaluation, positioning, oral care, and safe feeding practices matched to the patient.
Rising oxygen or positive end-expiratory pressure needs after stability warrant assessment of infection, edema, atelectasis, equipment, and other causes. Do not call every VAE pneumonia.

Worsening settings do not name the cause

An adult is mechanically ventilated on hospital day five:
  • Ventilator oxygen needs were stable for two days, then rise and remain higher for two days
  • The head of the bed was left flat after a procedure; oral care was missed
  • Chest imaging now shows new opacities; fluid balance is strongly positive
  • No source of the worsening has yet been established
