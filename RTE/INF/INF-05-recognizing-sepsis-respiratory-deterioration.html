Recognizing Sepsis & Respiratory Deterioration

Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction from the body’s response to infection. A patient with pneumonia may develop poor perfusion while oxygen needs rise. Review the trend in blood pressure, breathing, alertness, urine output, lactate, and respiratory support. Recognize a dangerous pattern early and call for urgent team evaluation.

Read the clinical trend

Infection → organ function → urgency

Find the infection and organ change

Review suspected infection along with new confusion, low blood pressure, less urine, worsening gas exchange, or other organ dysfunction. Fever and fast heart rate may support concern, but they do not establish sepsis alone.

Read perfusion as a pattern

Compare blood pressure and mean arterial pressure (MAP), skin perfusion, alertness, and urine output. A falling pressure or new confusion during infection is urgent even before a lactate result returns.

Interpret lactate with context

Lactate may rise with poor perfusion, increased stress, or other causes. An elevated or rising value strengthens concern when paired with the clinical picture; a single value does not diagnose sepsis or prove the reason for shock.

Connect respiratory deterioration

Track respiratory rate, effort, oxygen saturation, delivered oxygen, arterial blood gas, and support needs. Worsening pneumonia can impair oxygenation; a tired patient may also lose ventilation. Escalate both the respiratory and perfusion threats.

Core rule: suspected infection plus new organ dysfunction deserves urgent assessment. Evaluate oxygenation, ventilation, and circulation together; do not wait for every test to return before escalating a deteriorating patient.
Chart cue: a lactate of 3 mmol/L raises concern in context but does not by itself meet septic shock criteria. Persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors with lactate above 2 mmol/L after adequate fluids meets the Sepsis-3 shock construct.
Infection and organ function

Review each concept

Sepsis means life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated response to infection. Look beyond fever or a white blood cell count for new problems in breathing, perfusion, thinking, or kidney function.
Pneumonia may be the suspected source, but other infections and noninfectious causes can coexist. Note cultures, imaging, symptoms, antimicrobial timing, and the response to treatment without delaying escalation.
A fast respiratory rate, falling blood pressure, rising pulse, or change in temperature can signal worsening illness. Compare with earlier values and the patient’s usual state.
MAP summarizes average arterial pressure. A low MAP adds concern for poor organ perfusion; check mental status, skin findings, urine output, and repeated pressure rather than trusting one isolated reading.
A high or rising lactate may reflect stress or impaired perfusion. Interpret with pressure, perfusion, liver function, medications, and trend. A normal value does not make an obviously deteriorating patient safe.
New confusion and less urine may indicate organ dysfunction during infection. Look for other explanations as the team evaluates, but report the new findings promptly.
A growing oxygen requirement, lower saturation, or worse arterial oxygen despite more support indicates respiratory deterioration. Record the device and inspired oxygen level when comparing measurements.
Faster breathing may be early compensation. Later, a quieter patient with shallow breaths, rising carbon dioxide, or falling alertness may be failing. Reassess the airway and ventilation urgently.
Persistent low pressure, altered perfusion, and high lactate during infection are concerning for septic shock. Formal Sepsis-3 criteria use vasopressors to maintain MAP at least 65 and lactate above 2 after adequate fluids.
Communicate suspected infection, vital-sign and oxygen trends, lactate, urine output, and mental status together. Request urgent sepsis and respiratory support evaluation under local response protocols.

Pneumonia and new organ dysfunction

An adult with pneumonia is reassessed two hours after admission:
  • Respiratory rate rises from 22 to 33/min; oxygen need rises from room air to 5 L/min
  • Blood pressure falls from 118/72 to 84/46 mm Hg; the patient is newly confused
  • Urine output falls; lactate rises from 1.8 to 3.6 mmol/L
  • Oxygen saturation is 89% despite added oxygen
