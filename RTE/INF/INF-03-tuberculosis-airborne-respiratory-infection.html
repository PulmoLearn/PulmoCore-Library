Tuberculosis & Airborne Respiratory Infection

Pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) can cause a lasting cough, fever, night sweats, weight loss, or coughing blood. A known exposure, imaging, and immune risk change concern. Suspected infectious TB calls for airborne precautions while the team evaluates active disease. A TB blood or skin test detects infection but cannot tell whether it is active; respiratory specimens are needed when pulmonary disease is suspected.

Which test should be recommended?

Recommend diagnostic procedures

Suspect → protect → image → sample

Recognize possible active disease

Ask about a lasting cough, coughing blood, fever, night sweats, weight loss, exposure, and immune suppression. Chest radiography may show abnormalities such as upper-lung infiltrates or cavities, but imaging alone cannot prove TB.

Start airborne protection early

For suspected pulmonary or laryngeal TB, use an airborne infection isolation room and a fit-tested N95 or higher respirator for staff. During necessary transport, have the patient wear a medical mask if tolerated and follow facility procedures.

Choose the right diagnostic tests

An interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) or tuberculin skin test (TST) detects infection, not active versus inactive disease. For suspected pulmonary TB, recommend sputum for acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear and culture, with a nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) on an initial specimen.

Interpret results together

NAAT can provide an early clue, while culture can confirm TB and guide drug susceptibility testing. A negative smear or negative NAAT does not exclude disease when suspicion remains. Follow public-health and isolation protocols for next steps.

Core rule: an infection test identifies immune response; imaging and respiratory specimens assess active pulmonary disease. Do not wait for an IGRA or TST result to protect others when infectious TB is suspected.
Procedure cue: sputum induction and bronchoscopy can generate aerosols. Coordinate the room, respirator protection, and specimen collection plan before the procedure.
TB assessment and testing

Review each concept

Ask about close contact with infectious TB, duration of cough, coughing blood, fever, night sweats, weight loss, prior TB care, and immune status. The full pattern guides urgency.
Place a patient with suspected infectious pulmonary or laryngeal TB in an airborne infection isolation room. Staff use a fit-tested N95 or higher respirator under the facility respiratory protection program.
A chest radiograph helps evaluate lung abnormalities and active-disease concern after a positive infection test or concerning symptoms. Cavities or infiltrates may raise suspicion, but imaging cannot confirm TB alone.
An IGRA measures the immune response to TB antigens. A positive result indicates likely infection, but it cannot separate inactive infection from active disease; assess symptoms and imaging next.
A TST also evaluates immune response to TB. Neither a positive nor a negative test alone confirms or rules out active pulmonary disease in a symptomatic patient.
When pulmonary TB is suspected, recommend sputum AFB smear and culture, usually with multiple specimens collected per local protocol. A deep-cough specimen is preferred over saliva.
Recommend NAAT on an initial respiratory specimen in suspected pulmonary TB. A positive result may provide early evidence of TB; a negative result cannot reliably exclude it when concern is high.
Mycobacterial culture confirms TB when positive and allows further organism and drug susceptibility assessment. Culture may take longer than rapid molecular testing.
Sputum induction and bronchoscopy can spread airborne particles. Coordinate airborne room use, staff respirators, transport, and room clearance under the facility infection-control plan.
A negative early smear or NAAT does not end the assessment if symptoms, exposure, and imaging remain concerning. Continue coordinated evaluation and follow criteria for ending isolation rather than improvising.
Integrated RTE-style diagnostic case

A negative rapid test is not the end

An adult with recent close TB exposure has three weeks of cough, night sweats, and weight loss:
  • Chest radiograph shows a new upper-lung cavity
  • Airborne precautions are in place
  • Initial sputum AFB smear and NAAT are negative
  • Culture remains pending; symptoms and imaging still raise concern
