Acute Hypoxemic, Hypercapnic & Mixed Respiratory Failure

Respiratory failure may involve oxygenation, ventilation, or both. An arterial blood gas (ABG) gives the arterial oxygen tension (PaO₂), arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO₂), and pH. Read these values with the inspired oxygen level, prior gases, breathing effort, mental status, and clinical course. A normal pulse oximeter reading cannot tell you whether carbon dioxide is rising.

Which type of failure is developing?

Classify the physiology

PaO₂ → PaCO₂ → pH → support

Hypoxemic: oxygen is too low

A PaO₂ below about 60 mm Hg on room air signals severe hypoxemia. Pneumonia, pulmonary edema, and acute respiratory distress syndrome can disrupt gas exchange. Check the inspired oxygen level because the same PaO₂ means something different on added oxygen.

Hypercapnic: ventilation is inadequate

A high PaCO₂ with pH below 7.35 supports acute hypercapnic respiratory failure. Causes include chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbation, weak breathing muscles, or depressed drive. A high PaCO₂ with near-normal pH may be chronic compensation; compare baseline.

Mixed: both processes occur

Low oxygen and high carbon dioxide with acidemia can occur together. Severe pneumonia with fatigue, COPD with infection, or combined pump and lung disease may cause a mixed picture. Reassess both oxygenation and ventilation.

Use the whole chart to act

Trend ABG, pulse oximetry, oxygen delivery, work of breathing, alertness, and the likely cause. Oxygen treats hypoxemia, while ventilatory support addresses inadequate carbon dioxide clearance; severe decline requires immediate escalation.

Core rule: low PaO₂ identifies an oxygenation problem; high PaCO₂ with acidemia identifies acute ventilatory failure. When both are present, address both and urgently reassess the cause and response.
ABG cue: PaCO₂ of 66 mm Hg with pH 7.26 is acute respiratory acidosis. PaCO₂ of 60 with pH 7.38 may reflect chronic compensation; review the patient’s prior values.
Oxygenation and ventilation

Review each concept

PaO₂ estimates the oxygen dissolved in arterial blood. Below about 60 mm Hg on room air indicates severe hypoxemia. On supplemental oxygen, interpret it with the fraction of inspired oxygen and clinical status.
PaCO₂ reflects ventilation. A high value means carbon dioxide is not being cleared adequately; check pH and prior results to judge whether this is acute, chronic, or acute on chronic.
When PaCO₂ rises and pH falls below 7.35, acute respiratory acidosis is present. A near-normal pH with a high PaCO₂ may indicate renal compensation over time; look at bicarbonate and baseline.
Pneumonia can fill or inflame alveoli and impair oxygen transfer. Pulmonary edema and acute respiratory distress syndrome are other causes. Support oxygenation while treating the underlying process.
Hypoventilation can follow airway obstruction, weak muscles, reduced respiratory drive, or severe fatigue. Oxygen saturation alone may look normal while PaCO₂ rises. Ventilatory support may be needed.
A PaO₂ of 52, PaCO₂ of 68, and pH 7.25 signals poor oxygenation plus acute carbon dioxide retention. Verify the inspired oxygen level and promptly evaluate the need for both oxygen and ventilation support.
The fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) is the share of inhaled gas that is oxygen. A PaO₂ of 65 mm Hg on room air and the same value on FiO₂ 0.60 do not represent equal oxygenation.
Peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) estimates blood oxygen saturation, not PaCO₂ or pH. Drowsiness, shallow breathing, or a weaker respiratory effort calls for ventilation assessment even when SpO₂ seems acceptable.
A focal infiltrate and low PaO₂ may point to pneumonia. Obstruction plus a rising PaCO₂ and low pH may signal COPD exacerbation. Weak inspiratory effort may indicate a failing breathing pump.
A falling PaO₂ despite more oxygen, rising PaCO₂, falling pH, or reduced alertness signals deterioration. Report the trend and obtain immediate airway and ventilatory evaluation when the patient is failing.

Oxygen is falling as fatigue develops

An adult with severe pneumonia becomes quieter and more drowsy despite added oxygen:
  • Earlier on room air: pH 7.46, PaCO₂ 32 mm Hg, PaO₂ 54 mm Hg
  • Now on FiO₂ 0.50: pH 7.25, PaCO₂ 68 mm Hg, PaO₂ 52 mm Hg
  • Breathing is shallow; air movement and alertness are reduced
