Spirometry Quality Before Interpretation

Do not diagnose from numbers you cannot trust. Before interpreting FEV₁, FVC, or the FEV₁/FVC ratio, verify that the patient performed an acceptable maneuver, repeated it consistently, and used a correctly prepared device with valid demographic and technical inputs.

Socratic quality string

Interpret it—or fix the test first?

Quality-first framework

Acceptability comes before repeatability; both come before interpretation

1. Verify patient/setup inputs.

Confirm the correct patient, demographics, indication, preparation/medication conditions, baseline status, and device readiness before trusting percent-predicted comparisons.

2. Judge each maneuver for acceptability.

Look for full inspiration, rapid forceful start, tight seal, sustained exhalation, and absence of major artifacts such as cough, leak, glottic closure, obstructed mouthpiece, or obvious hesitation.

3. Judge repeatability.

Similar FEV₁ and FVC values across acceptable efforts support reproducibility. Wide variation means technique/effort or another problem must be investigated before interpretation.

4. Decide the next action.

Accept and interpret, coach and repeat, correct the setup/input and regenerate, stop for safety, or recommend complete PFTs when valid spirometry needs further clarification.

Quality first. Interpretation second.

Your faculty guide explicitly warns that the most precise-looking number is not useful if the maneuver was invalid.

Quality decisions

Review each topic

An acceptable maneuver begins from full inspiration, has a rapid forceful start, maintains a tight seal, and continues long enough to obtain a valid endpoint. The therapist should observe the patient—not just the printed numbers.
Your source lists cough during the first second, glottic closure, leak, obstructed mouthpiece, poor seal, hesitation/slow start, extra breaths, incomplete inhalation, and early termination as important quality problems.
Stopping too soon commonly underestimates FVC and may leave the FEV₁/FVC ratio falsely preserved or elevated. Your faculty guide specifically warns that this can mimic restriction. Recoach and repeat before labeling disease.
Similar FEV₁ and FVC values across acceptable maneuvers support reproducibility. Wide variation should trigger evaluation of technique, effort, fatigue, pain, anxiety, understanding, or setup—not an immediate disease label.
Your source teaches: demonstrate the maneuver, use clear consistent commands, observe closely, and provide specific corrective feedback. “Biggest breath in—seal tightly—blast it out—keep going” maps directly to maximal inspiration, seal, explosive start, and sustained exhalation.
Your PulmoLearn source states that incorrect height, age, sex, units, reference settings, or medication/testing conditions can invalidate predicted comparisons. If an input is wrong, correct it and regenerate/review the report before interpreting percent-predicted values.
A low FVC caused by early termination should be repeated—not called restriction. If technically valid spirometry remains low or otherwise requires physiologic clarification, recommend complete PFTs such as lung-volume testing rather than overinterpreting spirometry alone.
A valid patient effort cannot compensate for inaccurate equipment. Verify correct setup, infection-control components, device readiness, and facility/manufacturer quality procedures. Detailed 3-L syringe verification and equipment QC live primarily in PFT-09.
The AHRC competency requires monitoring tolerance and applying stop criteria. New concerning symptoms such as chest pressure or presyncope should prompt stopping the test, assessing the patient, and escalating according to policy rather than forcing completion.

Portable spirometry:

  • FVC varies widely across attempts
  • Two efforts ended after only a short exhalation
