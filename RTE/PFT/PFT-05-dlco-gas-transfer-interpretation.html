DLCO & Gas-Transfer Interpretation

Diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) estimates how effectively gas moves across the alveolar-capillary interface into blood. Interpret the value by asking what could change membrane thickness, alveolar surface area, pulmonary capillary blood volume, hemoglobin availability, or capillary transit time.

DLCO framework

Interpret the physiology before the disease label

What DLCO measures

Your PulmoLearn source defines DLCO as an estimate of transfer across the alveolar-capillary interface. A small amount of carbon monoxide is used because CO binds strongly to hemoglobin.

What lowers DLCO

Your source explicitly lists emphysema with loss of alveolar surface area, anemia with low hemoglobin, and interstitial fibrosis with a thickened membrane as causes that usually decrease DLCO.

What raises DLCO

Your source explicitly lists polycythemia as a cause that usually increases DLCO because more hemoglobin is available for CO binding.

Why context matters

DLCO is influenced by membrane thickness, surface area, pulmonary capillary blood volume, hemoglobin, and capillary transit time. An abnormal DLCO therefore does not identify one disease by itself.

DLCO measures gas transfer—not airflow obstruction.

Your faculty guide calls this out as a common misconception. A patient can have normal airflow with impaired diffusion or obstructive spirometry with different DLCO patterns depending on the underlying disease.

Gas-transfer interpretation

Review each topic

The PulmoLearn foundations lesson states that DLCO uses a very small amount of carbon monoxide as a tracer because CO binds strongly to hemoglobin. The test estimates how much CO moves from alveoli into blood during a short breath-hold.
Your source specifically lists emphysema with loss of alveolar surface area as a cause that usually decreases DLCO. Less available exchange surface means less gas can transfer into pulmonary capillary blood.
Interstitial fibrosis is source-listed as a cause of decreased DLCO because membrane thickening slows gas transfer. During exercise, shortened pulmonary-capillary transit time can make this limitation more apparent.
The PulmoLearn sorting activity teaches anemia → lower DLCO and polycythemia → higher DLCO. This is why hemoglobin context matters before assuming an abnormal diffusion value reflects lung structure alone.
Your student and faculty notes explicitly include pulmonary capillary blood volume as a determinant of DLCO. The retrieved sources do not provide a separate disease-by-disease algorithm for this variable, so the lesson keeps the interpretation physiologic.
Your notes teach that shorter transit time during exercise is normally still sufficient for equilibration, but a thickened membrane or reduced surface area can expose diffusion limitation when blood moves more quickly through the capillary.
First identify airflow and lung-size physiology with spirometry and lung volumes. Then use DLCO to add the gas-transfer dimension. Your faculty guide recommends naming the physiologic property first: airflow, lung size, diffusion, or muscle performance.
This RTE review correct procedure technique or measurement when applicable. This lesson therefore does not invent timing or inspiratory-volume cutoffs.
A low DLCO should trigger a mechanism question: surface-area loss, membrane thickening, low hemoglobin, altered pulmonary capillary blood volume, or shortened transit time. A high value should likewise be interpreted in context; your source specifically supports polycythemia as one cause of increased DLCO.
