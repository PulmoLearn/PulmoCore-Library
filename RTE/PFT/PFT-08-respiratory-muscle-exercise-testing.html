Respiratory Muscle & Exercise Testing

Use the test that answers the clinical question. Maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP) evaluates inspiratory muscle strength, maximum expiratory pressure (MEP) evaluates expiratory muscle strength and cough-related reserve, maximum voluntary ventilation (MVV) estimates rapid ventilatory capacity and effort, and the 6-minute walk test (6MWT) shows how the patient performs during real-world exertion.

Which test answers the question?

Interpretation framework

Strength, capacity, or exercise response?

MIP / NIF

Maximum inspiratory pressure (also called negative inspiratory force in some clinical settings) evaluates inspiratory muscle strength. A weaker inspiratory pressure supports concern for reduced ventilatory pump reserve when the clinical context fits.

MEP

Maximum expiratory pressure evaluates expiratory muscle strength. Your PulmoLearn pediatric source specifically links MEP to cough effectiveness and secretion clearance.

MVV

Maximum voluntary ventilation estimates how much air a patient can move rapidly during a brief period of maximal breathing. Your faculty guide emphasizes that it reflects ventilatory capacity and effort and should not be interpreted in isolation.

6MWT

The lab protocol measures functional walking performance while tracking distance, SpO₂, heart rate, dyspnea, fatigue, symptoms, and the oxygen setup used during the test.

Normal SpO₂ does not prove normal ventilatory reserve.

Your neuromuscular teaching specifically warns that oxygen saturation may remain reassuring while VC, MIP/MEP, cough force, and CO₂ worsen.

Source boundary: the registry includes inspiratory muscle training (IMT) as a shared task, but the retrieved source set does not provide a specific training-load prescription. This lesson therefore uses MIP and clinical response to decide when respiratory-muscle weakness may justify an IMT evaluation and how to reassess response without inventing a percentage-load protocol.
Muscle & exercise-testing decisions

Review each topic

MIP answers a strength question, not an airflow-obstruction question. Use it when weakness, neuromuscular disease, difficulty sustaining ventilation, or reduced inspiratory reserve is suspected. Interpret serial values and the patient's clinical trajectory rather than a single isolated value when no source-specific cutoff is provided.
MEP estimates expiratory muscle strength. Your source explicitly states that it helps identify weakness that can impair cough. Pair a weak MEP with cough force, secretion burden, bulbar status, and clinical ability to clear secretions.
MVV estimates how much air a patient can move rapidly during a brief period of maximal breathing. It reflects integrated ventilatory capacity and effort, so poor cooperation or technique can lower it. Your faculty guide says not to interpret MVV in isolation.
The 2027 NBRC outline links mechanics of spontaneous ventilation with tidal volume, minute volume, maximal inspiratory pressure, and vital capacity. Use MIP together with VT/VE/VC and bedside work of breathing when judging ventilatory-pump reserve.
Your lab protocol requires chart review, baseline cardiopulmonary assessment and seated vitals, walking-stability assessment, a safe marked course, functioning pulse oximetry, emergency readiness, appropriate footwear/assistive devices, and the patient's current portable oxygen if indicated. Record baseline status, run the timer for 6 minutes, track distance, and update the patient each minute.
At the end of 6 minutes, your lab competency records SpO₂, heart rate, reported dyspnea and fatigue, distance walked, and the respiratory assessment. Interpret the pattern as functional exercise response: distance alone is not enough if the patient desaturates, becomes very symptomatic, or requires significant support.
The registry explicitly includes exertional oxygen response. Document whether the patient is on room air or an oxygen device/flow, then compare exertional SpO₂ and symptoms with baseline. A fall in oxygenation during exertion identifies a clinically important response that may justify further oxygen assessment or treatment review; use the dedicated oxygen lessons for device-specific titration rules.
Suspected muscle weakness → MIP/MEP (and related respiratory-muscle testing). Functional exertional limitation or exertional desaturation → 6MWT. Unexplained exercise limitation when resting studies do not explain symptoms may warrant more integrated exercise testing such as CPET, as your faculty guide notes.
If inspiratory weakness is present and an IMT program is being considered or performed, MIP provides a relevant strength measure to establish a baseline and follow response. Do not assume that low MIP alone identifies the cause of weakness; integrate technique, symptoms, neuromuscular status, VC, cough, and trends.
Your 6MWT competency requires pretest symptom/vital assessment, fall-risk review, an emergency plan, and observation for pain or deterioration. Patient safety outranks finishing the distance. Abnormal symptoms or instability require assessment and appropriate notification/escalation.
Integrated RTE-style case

Separate oxygenation from ventilatory-muscle failure

Patient with progressive neuromuscular weakness:
  • SpO₂ 96% at rest
  • Increasing fatigue and weak cough
  • VC is trending downward
  • MIP is becoming weaker over serial measurements
  • MEP is reduced
  • CO₂ is rising
