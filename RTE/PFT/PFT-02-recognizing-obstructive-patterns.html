Recognizing Obstructive Patterns

Obstruction means expiratory flow is limited. Start with a technically valid spirometry study, then compare FEV₁/FVC with the lower limit of normal (LLN) or its equivalent z-score. Use FEV₁, expiratory-flow shape, peak-flow information, symptoms, and bronchodilator data to describe the pattern and clinical context without letting one number do all the work.

Obstruction framework

Ratio first—then describe what the obstruction looks like

FEV₁/FVC below LLN

The registry makes LLN/z-score the primary discriminator for obstruction. LLN represents the lower boundary of the expected reference distribution; the GOLD source in your library notes that a z-score of −1.645 corresponds to the 5th percentile.

FEV₁ falls disproportionately

With airflow obstruction, the first-second volume is reduced more than the total forced vital capacity, lowering the ratio.

Expiratory limb becomes concave

Your PulmoLearn PFT source describes the obstructive flow-volume loop as having a scooped/concave expiratory limb.

Clinical context still matters

Asthma, COPD, and bronchiectasis can all produce obstruction. The spirometry pattern describes physiology; it does not independently identify the disease.

Do not use a fixed 0.70 ratio as the only universal rule in this lesson.

Your older PulmoLearn review content uses <0.70 as legacy/simple teaching language, while the current registry specifically directs PFT-02 to use LLN/z-score. GOLD also notes that a fixed ratio can classify some younger and older adults differently than LLN.

Obstructive-pattern decisions

Review each topic

The current PFT-02 registry says to identify obstruction using FEV₁/FVC relative to LLN/z-score. The GOLD source in your library explains that LLN classifies the lowest 5% of the healthy reference population as abnormal and that z = −1.645 corresponds to the 5th percentile. Reference equations matter because LLN and z-scores depend on the chosen reference set.
Your older PFT review content uses FEV₁/FVC <0.70 as a simple obstructive rule. Keep that recognizable for legacy exam language and COPD context, but the current registry's authoring direction is LLN/z-score rather than a fixed ratio as the primary universal discriminator.
Your PFT source describes FEV₁ as disproportionately reduced in obstruction. Use FEV₁ to understand the degree of expiratory-flow impairment in context, but do not diagnose obstruction from FEV₁ alone—the ratio is the key spirometric discriminator.
Your asthma/PFT sources note that FVC may remain normal or fall when air trapping limits complete exhalation. A low FVC with obstruction does not by itself prove restriction; PFT-03 and PFT-04 will address suspected restriction, TLC, air trapping, and mixed patterns.
The existing PulmoLearn glossary describes an obstructive flow-volume loop as having a concave or scooped expiratory limb. That visual clue supports the numeric pattern but should not replace quality review and ratio interpretation.
Peak expiratory flow (PEF) is effort-dependent and useful for asthma monitoring. Your PulmoLearn PFT review teaches personal-best zones: green 80–100%, yellow 50–79%, red <50%. It also explicitly states that PEF is not a substitute for spirometry diagnosis.
The registry requires bronchodilator data to be considered in recognizing obstruction. Improvement after bronchodilator supports reversibility in the proper context, but PFT-06 owns the detailed bronchodilator-response thresholds and current-versus-legacy interpretation.
The older PulmoLearn PFT content lists COPD, asthma, and bronchiectasis as examples associated with obstruction. Use history, symptoms, exposures, reversibility, imaging, and other data to determine the disease rather than labeling all obstruction as one diagnosis.
Do not call a low ratio obstructive until the maneuver is technically credible. PFT-01 remains the quality gate: poor start, leak, cough, incomplete inspiration, or other major artifacts can distort FEV₁, FVC, and the ratio.
Integrated RTE-style obstructive case

Recognize physiology without overcalling the diagnosis

Technically acceptable spirometry:
  • FEV₁/FVC is below the report's LLN
  • FEV₁ is reduced
  • FVC is mildly reduced
  • Expiratory limb is concave
  • PEF is below the patient's usual personal best
  • History includes episodic wheezing
