Flow-Volume Loops & Upper-Airway Obstruction

Flow-volume loops add shape to spirometry. Recognize the classic patterns: lower-airway obstruction produces a concave expiratory limb, restriction produces a smaller narrow loop, fixed upper-airway obstruction flattens both inspiratory and expiratory limbs, variable extrathoracic obstruction mainly flattens inspiration, and variable intrathoracic obstruction mainly flattens expiration.

Loop-pattern framework

Shape first, then location

Lower-airway obstruction

Concave or “scooped” expiratory limb. This supports expiratory airflow limitation such as asthma/COPD physiology.

Restriction

A smaller, relatively narrow loop caused by reduced lung volume; confirmation still depends on reduced TLC.

Fixed upper-airway obstruction

Both inspiratory and expiratory limbs flatten because the narrowed segment limits flow during both phases. Your mock-exam source describes a nearly round/box-like loop and gives laryngeal cancer as one example.

Variable obstruction

Extrathoracic lesions predominantly flatten inspiration; intrathoracic lesions predominantly flatten expiration.

Memory shortcut:
Fixed = both limbs. Extrathoracic = inspiration. Intrathoracic = expiration.

Loop interpretation

Review each pattern

A fixed lesion keeps a similar degree of narrowing during inspiration and expiration, so both limbs plateau/flatten. Your Lindsey Jones mock source describes a nearly round loop with few inflection points and identifies laryngeal cancer as a fixed-obstruction example. Tracheal stenosis and other fixed central lesions can produce the same general morphology.
The inspiratory limb flattens more than the expiratory limb. During forced inspiration, pressure within an extrathoracic airway can become more negative relative to surrounding pressure, favoring narrowing of a compliant lesion. Think: outside thorax → inspiration affected.
The expiratory limb flattens more than the inspiratory limb. During forced expiration, positive pleural pressure can narrow a compliant intrathoracic airway. Think: inside thorax → expiration affected.
Asthma/COPD-type obstruction typically produces a scooped or concave expiratory limb rather than a flat plateau. PFT-02 owns the numeric obstructive pattern; PFT-07 focuses on visual morphology.
The loop is reduced in overall volume/width because vital capacity is smaller, while the ratio may remain preserved. As PFT-03 emphasized, spirometry can suggest restriction but TLC confirms it.
Poor effort, incomplete inspiration, cough, leak, early termination, or inconsistent cooperation can distort a flow-volume loop. The peer-reviewed upper-airway review also warns that classic shapes depend on patient effort and may be obscured by coexisting lower-airway disease.
A suspicious loop should be integrated with symptoms such as stridor, voice change, prior airway surgery/intubation, neck mass, known stenosis, or other airway history before assigning a cause.
If the loop suggests upper-airway obstruction and the patient also has stridor, retractions, worsening work of breathing, or poor air movement, treat this as an airway-safety problem—not merely an interesting PFT finding. Maintain airway patency and escalate difficult-airway support as clinically required.
