Bronchodilator Response & Reversibility

Compare technically valid pre- and post-bronchodilator spirometry using the current response definition while still recognizing the older 12% + 200 mL rule as legacy exam language. A positive bronchodilator response supports reversible airflow physiology; it does not by itself diagnose asthma or exclude COPD.

Socratic reversibility string

Current rule, legacy rule, or overinterpretation?

Bronchodilator-response framework

Compare pre/post values correctly

Current ERS/ATS interpretation

A positive bronchodilator response is a change in FEV₁ or FVC greater than 10% of the predicted value.

Legacy threshold

The older 2005 rule defined significant response as an increase in FEV₁ and/or FVC of at least 12% from baseline and at least 200 mL. The registry specifically asks learners to recognize this as legacy exam language.

Quality still matters

Both pre- and post-bronchodilator studies must be technically acceptable and comparable. An apparent improvement caused by a poor baseline maneuver is not reliable reversibility.

Response is physiology, not diagnosis

Your asthma lesson links asthma with variable/reversible airflow obstruction, but bronchodilator responsiveness alone does not identify one disease and lack of a positive response does not by itself exclude asthma.

Current: (Post − Pre) ÷ Predicted × 100 > 10%   |   Legacy: ≥12% from baseline AND ≥200 mL
Watch the denominator.

The current rule expresses the change as a percentage of the predicted value—not as a percentage of the pre-bronchodilator value.

Reversibility decisions

Review each topic

Calculate the change in FEV₁ or FVC from pre- to post-bronchodilator, divide that change by the predicted value, and multiply by 100. A response greater than 10% of predicted in either FEV₁ or FVC meets the current criterion.
Older teaching used an increase in FEV₁ and/or FVC of at least 12% from the pre-bronchodilator value and at least 200 mL. Keep this recognizable because older review materials and exam language may still use it, but do not confuse it with the current calculation.
Predicted FEV₁ = 3.0 L. Pre = 1.8 L. Post = 2.15 L. Change = 0.35 L. Current response = 0.35 ÷ 3.0 × 100 = 11.7% of predicted, which exceeds the current 10% threshold.
Pre FEV₁ = 1.8 L; post = 2.05 L. Increase = 250 mL and 13.9% from baseline. This meets the older ≥12% AND ≥200 mL rule.
The current and legacy criteria both evaluate change in FEV₁ and/or FVC. Do not look only at FEV₁ if the clinically meaningful volume response occurs in FVC.
Your PulmoLearn asthma lesson teaches reduced FEV₁/FVC with significant bronchodilator reversibility as an important asthma clue and describes asthma as variable and often reversible. Keep the wording “supports reversible airflow obstruction” rather than “proves asthma.”
The GOLD material in your library discusses bronchodilator responsiveness in established COPD and its limitations. Therefore, do not use bronchodilator response as a simple asthma-versus-COPD switch.
PFT-01 remains the gatekeeper. Poor effort, leak, cough, early termination, or inconsistent coaching can create false differences between pre- and post-bronchodilator values.
Combine the response with baseline obstruction, symptoms, history, medication use, prior PFTs, and disease context. The response is one piece of evidence about reversibility, not an independent diagnosis.

Use the current denominator correctly

Technically valid spirometry:
  • Predicted FEV₁ = 3.20 L
  • Pre-BD FEV₁ = 1.90 L
  • Post-BD FEV₁ = 2.25 L
  • Absolute change = 0.35 L
