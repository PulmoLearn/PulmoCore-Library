Recognizing Pneumonia & Lower Respiratory Infection

Pneumonia is infection in the lung. A useful assessment combines new respiratory symptoms, vital signs, imaging, oxygen needs, and risk factors. Viral, bacterial, and aspiration patterns overlap, so one symptom or sputum color cannot name the cause. Read each result in context and check whether a sputum sample truly came from the lower airway.

Read the whole chart

Symptoms → imaging → risk → specimen

Recognize a lung infection pattern

Look for new cough, fever or chills, sputum, pleuritic chest pain, shortness of breath, fast breathing, or new oxygen need. Older adults may have less typical symptoms. Examine the full history and vital-sign trend.

Confirm the lung finding

New infiltrate or consolidation on chest imaging supports pneumonia when symptoms fit. Lung ultrasound can be an imaging option where trained expertise exists. Compare prior images and consider atelectasis, edema, or another cause of opacity.

Read the context

Community onset, recent viral illness, swallowing trouble or witnessed aspiration, immune status, and exposure history change the differential. Viral and bacterial infections may coexist; no single image shape or sputum color identifies the organism.

Interpret sputum carefully

A deep-cough specimen with many inflammatory cells and few squamous epithelial cells is more likely to represent the lower airway. Many epithelial cells suggest saliva contamination. Interpret Gram stain and culture with collection quality and symptoms.

Core rule: combine clinical findings with imaging to assess pneumonia. The cause remains a working judgment until the available tests and response provide enough support; ask whether each sputum result reflects a good specimen.
Chart cue: green sputum alone does not establish bacterial pneumonia. A poor sputum sample can grow mouth organisms and mislead the interpretation.
Infection assessment

Review each concept

Ask when cough, fever, sputum, dyspnea, and pleuritic pain began and whether they are getting worse. Compare temperature, respiratory rate, heart rate, mental status, and current oxygen need with baseline.
A new infiltrate or consolidation together with compatible symptoms supports pneumonia. A chest image may have competing explanations; review prior studies, the examination, and the clinical course.
Community-acquired pneumonia begins outside the hospital or is present on admission. Record exposures, chronic illness, recent illness, and severity. Hospital-acquired and ventilator-associated patterns are covered separately.
An acute viral syndrome, exposure, and a positive respiratory test can support viral infection. A positive viral test does not always rule out bacterial coinfection; interpret severity, imaging, and the patient’s course.
Fever, focal findings, and lobar consolidation can support bacterial pneumonia, but none is exclusive. A productive cough or colored sputum alone cannot identify a bacterial cause.
Dysphagia, impaired alertness, vomiting, poor cough, or a witnessed aspiration event raise concern. Sudden symptoms right after aspiration may reflect chemical lung injury; persistent or evolving infection requires reassessment.
A good expectorated specimen comes from a deep cough, not saliva. Many squamous epithelial cells suggest oral contamination; abundant inflammatory cells support lower-airway material. Use the lab’s quality assessment.
Gram stain shows cell types and bacterial forms quickly; culture may later identify organisms. A result from a contaminated sample may reflect mouth flora. Compare with clinical and imaging findings.
Increasing respiratory rate, new oxygen requirement, low blood pressure, confusion, or signs of respiratory distress call for prompt reassessment. Severity can matter more urgently than naming the organism.
Pneumonia is a clinical and imaging assessment. A positive test, a colored sputum sample, or one abnormal radiograph is not the whole story. Report uncertainty and the findings that change management.

New opacity, weak sample

  • Respiratory rate is 28/min; oxygen need is new
  • Chest imaging shows a new right lower-lung opacity
  • A sputum culture reports mixed oral flora; the sample has many squamous epithelial cells
