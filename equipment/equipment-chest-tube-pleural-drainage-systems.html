Chest Tube & Pleural Drainage Systems

Chest tubes are not placed by respiratory therapists in most settings, but RTs must recognize what the system is doing, monitor the patient’s cardiopulmonary status, protect the drainage system, identify dangerous equipment problems, and escalate quickly when the patient or system changes.

Why Chest Tubes Are Used

A chest tube removes air, blood, or other fluid from the pleural space or mediastinal space so the lung and/or thoracic structures can re-expand and remain stable. The drainage system also acts as a one-way valve so air or fluid can leave the chest without flowing back into the patient.

Air
Pneumothorax, tension pneumothorax, persistent air leak, post-procedure air accumulation.
Fluid/Blood
Pleural effusion, hemothorax, empyema, post-thoracic surgery drainage.
Post-op / Mediastinal
Thoracotomy or cardiac/thoracic surgery where mediastinal drainage must remain patent and monitored.
RT priority: Do not focus only on the drainage device. Always assess the patient first: respiratory distress, oxygenation, breath sounds, vital signs, pain, tube securement, and any new subcutaneous emphysema.

Setup & Safe Positioning

  1. Verify the provider order and prescribed suction level if suction is ordered.
  2. Confirm the drainage system is upright and below the level of the patient’s chest.
  3. Check all connections from patient to drainage system; secure connections per policy.
  4. Avoid dependent loops in tubing that trap fluid and increase resistance to drainage.
  5. Ensure tubing is not kinked, compressed, or under traction.
  6. Keep the system secure and out of walkways to prevent accidental dislodgement.
  7. Confirm emergency supplies are available at bedside.
Board-style safety trap: Do not clamp a chest tube routinely. Clamping can recreate a tension pneumothorax if air continues to accumulate. Clamp only for specific provider orders, facility protocols, or brief troubleshooting situations where trained staff are present.

Water Seal vs Suction

Water seal
The water seal is the one-way valve. Air leaves the patient and bubbles through water, but atmospheric air cannot travel backward into the pleural space.
Wet suction
Suction level is controlled by the height of the water column. Gentle bubbling usually indicates suction is active. Vigorous bubbling increases evaporation/noise but does not increase therapeutic suction.
Dry suction
Suction level is controlled by a regulator/dial inside the drainage system. The wall suction must still be adequate, but the device regulates the set negative pressure.
Tidaling: The water level in the water seal chamber may rise and fall with respirations. Tidaling often decreases as the lung re-expands. Sudden absent tidaling can also mean a kink, obstruction, dependent loop, or tube problem, so always assess the patient and tubing before assuming improvement.

Transport Considerations

Transport is a high-risk time for patients with chest tubes because tubing, suction, oxygen, and monitoring may be disrupted. The goal is to preserve drainage system function and identify patient deterioration quickly.

Before transport
Verify tube securement, dressing, drainage system position, suction order, oxygen supply, monitoring, and emergency dressing supplies.
During transport
Keep drainage system upright and below chest level. Avoid kinks/dependent loops. Do not let the system tip over or hang from tubing.
If suction is ordered
Clarify whether transport suction is required or if water seal is acceptable during transport per order/policy. Prepare portable suction when required.
High-risk changes
Increasing distress, falling SpO₂, absent breath sounds, sudden drainage change, new air leak, disconnection, or dislodgement require immediate escalation.

Documentation & Handoff

  • Patient status: SpO₂, oxygen device/settings, RR, work of breathing, breath sounds, pain, cough, and distress signs.
  • System status: water seal intact, suction level, wet/dry suction type, tidaling, bubbling/air leak pattern, system upright/below chest.
  • Drainage: total output, amount since last check, color/character, and any sudden changes.
  • Site/tubing: dressing intact, tube secure, no kinks/dependent loops, no new subcutaneous emphysema.
  • Interventions: oxygen changes, suction verification, connection fixes, provider notification, transport status, and patient response.

FAQ

Common Questions

Is bubbling always bad?

No. Intermittent bubbling may be seen with a pneumothorax as air exits the pleural space. New continuous bubbling, especially after movement or with patient deterioration, suggests an air leak and requires assessment.

Is absent tidaling always good?

No. Tidaling may decrease as the lung re-expands, but sudden absent tidaling may also mean a kink, clot, dependent loop, or obstruction. Assess the patient and system before assuming improvement.

Can the RT clamp a chest tube?

Routine clamping is avoided. It can trap air and contribute to tension pneumothorax. Follow facility policy and provider direction for any brief, specific clamping situation.

Summary & Safety Reminders

  • Patient first: If the patient is short of breath or unstable, support oxygenation and call for help before spending time troubleshooting the box.
  • Keep it low and upright: The drainage system should remain below chest level and upright.
  • Water seal matters: It is the one-way valve that protects the pleural space.
  • Do not routinely clamp: Clamping can recreate tension physiology.
  • New continuous bubbling = possible air leak: Assess the patient, insertion site, connections, and system.
  • Document trends: Drainage amount/character, bubbling, tidaling, suction level, patient response, and communications.
