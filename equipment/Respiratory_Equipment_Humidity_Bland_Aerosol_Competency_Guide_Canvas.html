Humidity & Bland Aerosol Therapy

Learn how to replace the upper airway's natural heat-and-moisture conditioning, distinguish humidity from aerosol, select active or passive humidification, recognize when an HME is unsafe, manage condensate, and troubleshoot bland aerosol and humidification systems.

Before you begin

Every normal breath is conditioned by the upper airway. Inspired gas is warmed, humidified, and filtered before it reaches the lower respiratory tract. When a patient breathes cold or dry gas, has an artificial airway, receives high minute ventilation, or depends on respiratory equipment that bypasses normal conditioning, the respiratory therapist may need to replace that function artificially.

This lesson contains one distinction that must remain clear from the beginning: humidity is invisible water vapor; aerosol is visible liquid droplets suspended in gas. They are not interchangeable. They use different equipment, create different physiologic effects, and are selected for different clinical problems.

For the 2027 Respiratory Therapy Examination (RTE), Breadth of Knowledge means knowing humidity physics, airway targets, devices, contraindications, setup rules, and troubleshooting. Depth of Clinical Judgment means recognizing whether a patient is inadequately humidified, whether a device is creating resistance or contamination risk, and what change should be made next.

Big idea: Follow the chain airway condition → humidity need → device → delivered heat/moisture or aerosol → secretion response → resistance/condensation → troubleshooting → reassessment.

Core vocabulary: humidity, aerosol, absolute humidity (AH), relative humidity (RH), body humidity, humidity deficit, isothermic saturation boundary (ISB), condensation, rainout, active humidifier, passive humidifier, bubble humidifier, passover humidifier, heated humidifier, heated-wire circuit, heat-moisture exchanger (HME), artificial nose, large-volume nebulizer (LVN), bland aerosol, aerosol collar, tracheostomy mask, T-piece, face tent, sterile water, isotonic saline, hypertonic saline, inspiratory demand, water trap.

Humidity and aerosol are different forms of water delivery

Humidity is water in the vapor state. The individual water molecules are dissolved in the gas and are invisible. Aerosol is made of actual liquid droplets or particles suspended in a gas and can appear as visible mist. Humidifiers add or recycle water vapor; nebulizers generate droplets.

Absolute humidity: how much water is actually present?

Absolute humidity (AH) is the actual mass of water vapor in a volume of gas, measured in milligrams per liter (mg/L). The course identifies fully conditioned gas at body temperature as 44 mg/L at 37°C and 100% RH.

Relative humidity: how full is the gas's capacity?

Relative humidity (RH) is the percentage of the gas's water-holding capacity that is currently filled. Temperature changes capacity. If AH stays constant and the gas is heated, capacity increases and RH falls. If the gas cools, capacity decreases and RH rises. When saturated gas cools enough, excess vapor becomes liquid condensation.

Relative humidity = (Absolute humidity ÷ water-vapor capacity at that temperature) × 100%

Body humidity and humidity deficit

Body Humidity (%) = (Absolute Humidity ÷ 44 mg/L) × 100
Humidity Deficit = 44 mg/L − delivered absolute humidity

Example: delivered gas contains 22 mg/L AH. Humidity deficit = 44 − 22 = 22 mg/L. The patient's airway must supply that missing water and heat. The larger the deficit and the more gas the patient moves, the greater the drying burden on airway mucosa.

The isothermic saturation boundary

The isothermic saturation boundary (ISB) is the point in the airway where inspired gas reaches body temperature and full saturation: 37°C, 100% RH, and 44 mg/L AH. In the course, the normal ISB is approximately 5 cm below the carina.

The ISB shifts deeper when the airway has more work to do. The lesson identifies mouth breathing, cold/dry inspired gas, artificial airways, and high minute ventilation as reasons for a deeper shift. An endotracheal or tracheostomy tube bypasses much of the upper airway, so the normal heat-and-moisture exchanger has been removed from the pathway.

Why insufficient humidity matters

Dry gas can thicken secretions, reduce ciliary function, irritate mucosa, increase secretion retention, and contribute to airway obstruction. Humidification is therefore not primarily about patient comfort in artificial-airway care; it helps preserve airway function and secretion clearance.

Common mistake: Assuming the gas is “humid enough” because RH is high. RH depends on temperature. Always connect the percentage to the gas temperature and actual water content.

Active versus passive humidification

Active humidifiers add heat and/or water from an external source. The lesson groups the bubble humidifier, passover humidifier, heated humidifier, and LVN as active devices. The HME is the only passive humidifier: it captures the patient's exhaled heat and moisture and returns part of it on the next inspiration.

Mechanically ventilated airway target taught in the lesson

The lesson gives an airway inlet target of 33°C ± 2°C with a minimum absolute humidity of 30 mg/L for mechanically ventilated patients. These values give students a way to judge whether an active system is providing clinically acceptable conditioning rather than simply producing visible moisture.

Heated humidifier setup logic

  1. Use the prescribed/approved heated humidifier system and sterile water.
  2. Fill or connect the water chamber using the manufacturer's/facility process.
  3. Connect the humidifier in the inspiratory gas pathway.
  4. Use the appropriate circuit, including heated-wire tubing when indicated.
  5. Verify temperature and humidity delivery at the patient end of the circuit.
  6. Place water traps at circuit low points as designed.
  7. Monitor secretions, airway resistance, condensation, and patient response.

Bland aerosol and LVN

The large-volume nebulizer (LVN) is not simply another humidifier. It generates visible liquid droplets using a jet, siphon tube, baffle, and large reservoir. The lesson teaches a typical operating flow of 6–15 L/min. Total aerosol flow must meet or exceed the patient's inspiratory demand or room air will dilute the aerosol around the interface.

Bland aerosol solutions

  • Sterile water: hypotonic and highly moisturizing but more irritating in reactive airways; used in the lesson for upper-airway edema/post-extubation applications.
  • Normal saline 0.9%: isotonic and least likely to cause bronchospasm; standard bland aerosol solution in many course examples.
  • Hypertonic saline 3–10%: draws water into the airway lumen osmotically, helping thin secretions and induce sputum; carries the highest bronchospasm risk in this lesson.

Choose the interface that fits the airway

Aerosol face masks can serve spontaneously breathing patients with intact upper airways. A tracheostomy mask/aerosol collar delivers aerosol over a tracheostomy. A T-piece connects directly to an endotracheal or tracheostomy tube. Face tents offer a less confining option when a sealed mask is poorly tolerated. Large-bore corrugated tubing is used to reduce resistance and support adequate aerosol flow.

RTE distinction: Device selection starts with the patient's airway and humidity need. A bubble humidifier that is reasonable for supplemental oxygen is not adequate simply because an intubated patient also receives oxygen.

The HME is an “artificial nose,” not a source of new humidity

The heat-moisture exchanger (HME) sits between the artificial airway/patient connection and the breathing circuit. During exhalation it captures some of the patient's heat and water; during the next inspiration it returns some of that stored heat and moisture. The lesson notes that an HME can return up to about 70% of exhaled heat and humidity.

Because the HME can only recycle what the patient exhales, it has limits. It cannot actively make up a large humidity deficit the way a heated humidifier can.

Course HME contraindications

Thick or bloody secretions
Secretion accumulation can obstruct the element, and inadequate humidity can worsen inspissation.
Hypothermia
Cool exhaled gas provides less heat for the HME to capture and return.
High minute ventilation
Large volumes of dry incoming gas can exceed the passive device's ability to maintain adequate humidity.
Small tidal volume
The HME's added dead space and resistance become more important when each breath is small.

Never combine HME with heated humidification

The course explicitly prohibits using an HME and a heated humidifier at the same time. Already-humidified gas can saturate the HME element, increasing resistance and potentially creating circuit obstruction. Choose one appropriate humidification strategy.

Remove/bypass the HME for aerosol therapy

Nebulized medication or water droplets can deposit in and clog the HME element. Before aerosol therapy in a ventilated patient, the HME must be removed/bypassed according to the circuit setup. After treatment, it may be reinstalled when appropriate. If aerosol therapy is frequent or ongoing, the course recommends changing the humidification strategy rather than repeatedly delivering aerosol through an HME pathway.

Clinical case: thickening secretions

A mechanically ventilated patient has progressively thicker secretions and increasing suction difficulty while using an HME. The temptation is to suction more often or simply replace the HME. The deeper reasoning is that the patient is showing evidence that passive humidity is inadequate. Thick secretions are themselves a course contraindication. The appropriate change is to move to active heated humidification rather than treating only the symptom.

Safety: A saturated or secretion-obstructed HME can increase circuit resistance and work of breathing. If ventilation becomes difficult, remove the obstruction from the gas pathway and support the patient while the humidification plan is corrected.
Putting C3 together
Use an HME only when the patient can safely supply enough exhaled heat and moisture and the device will not create excessive dead space, resistance, secretion obstruction, or conflict with another therapy. When the contraindication appears, change the humidification method rather than forcing the HME to work outside its limits.

Too little water and too much water can both be dangerous

Recognize inadequate humidification

The lesson's important clues include progressive secretion thickening, increasing suction difficulty, airway drying, and secretion retention. In a ventilated patient, these findings should prompt review of the humidification method, delivered temperature/humidity, HME suitability, circuit condition, and duration/clinical trajectory.

Condensation: why “rainout” forms

Hot, saturated gas leaves the humidifier and enters cooler tubing. As the gas cools, its water-holding capacity falls, so some water vapor becomes liquid. That liquid collects at low points in the circuit.

Why condensate matters

  • It can obstruct gas flow and increase circuit resistance.
  • It can accidentally drain toward the patient's airway.
  • It can serve as a reservoir for microorganisms.
  • It can interfere with accurate and stable ventilator-circuit performance.

Manage condensate away from the patient

Position water traps at circuit low points as designed. Drain accumulated water away from the patient into the designated trap/waste system. Never shake condensate toward the patient, and do not drain contaminated condensate back into the humidifier reservoir. Heated-wire circuits reduce the temperature gradient and therefore reduce rainout.

Troubleshoot from patient to device

  1. Assess the patient: airway patency, work of breathing, secretion quality, breath sounds, oxygenation, and ventilator response if applicable.
  2. Identify the current humidity/aerosol method.
  3. Check temperature, water source, circuit, HME status, or aerosol generation as appropriate.
  4. Look for resistance: saturated HME, kinked tubing, accumulated water, clogged jet, or obstructed interface.
  5. Correct the device problem or choose a more appropriate method.
  6. Reassess secretions, airway pressure/resistance, comfort, oxygenation, and ventilation.
Patient-first safety: If condensate, an HME, or another humidification component is obstructing ventilation, restore a patent gas pathway first. Do not delay support while trying to preserve the current device setup.

Humidity & aerosol: condition the gas without creating a new airway problem

Humidification should replace the conditioning work the patient's upper airway cannot perform. Bland aerosol should be chosen when liquid droplets serve the clinical purpose. The RTE expects you to distinguish the two, choose the right equipment, recognize when passive humidity is inadequate, and manage water safely in the circuit.

airway → humidity deficit → device → heat/moisture or aerosol → secretion response → resistance/condensation → correction → reassessment

RTE Depth of Clinical Judgment: take the next step

  • Information Gathering: identify whether the patient has an intact upper airway or artificial airway, current humidity method, secretion amount/consistency, temperature, minute ventilation, tidal volume, circuit condensation, and aerosol-treatment requirements.
  • Information Gathering: determine whether increased airway resistance is caused by secretion burden, a saturated HME, accumulated condensate, tubing obstruction, or worsening patient physiology.
  • Decision Making: replace passive HME humidification with active heated humidification when thick secretions or another course contraindication develops.
  • Decision Making: avoid using a bubble humidifier as the sole humidification strategy for an artificial airway.
  • Decision Making: remove/bypass the HME before aerosol therapy rather than allowing medication droplets to clog the element.
  • Decision Making: manage circuit rainout away from the patient and use appropriate water traps/heated-wire strategies rather than returning condensate toward the airway or humidifier.
  • Decision Making: increase/correct LVN flow or clear a blocked jet when aerosol output is weak or absent, while recognizing bronchospasm as a patient problem requiring treatment to stop.
  • Reassessment: verify improvement in secretion consistency, airway patency, resistance, ventilator function, comfort, and overall respiratory status after the equipment change.
  • Communication: report the humidification problem, evidence supporting the change, equipment intervention, and patient response.
