ET Tube Exchange & Airway Rescue Equipment

Tube exchange is a high-risk airway procedure. The RT’s role is to anticipate loss of airway, prepare rescue equipment before the first step, maintain oxygenation and ventilation, assist with the exchange, confirm placement, and monitor for complications after reconnection to the ventilator.

When ET Tube Exchange Is Needed

HIGH-RISK AIRWAY PROCEDURE

ET tube exchange is performed when the existing artificial airway is no longer safe or functional. The most important decision is whether this is a planned controlled exchange or an airway emergency. In both cases, rescue equipment must be ready before the tube is manipulated.

Planned / Controlled Exchange
Damaged pilot balloon · persistent cuff leak · need for different tube size/type · need for subglottic suction ETT · tube malposition that cannot be corrected safely by repositioning alone.
Urgent Exchange
Partial obstruction · recurrent high-pressure alarms from suspected tube issue · tube kink or bite damage · inability to maintain cuff seal · tube too shallow/deep with patient instability.
Emergency Airway Rescue
Complete obstruction · accidental extubation · inability to ventilate · no exhaled CO₂ after exchange · rapidly worsening oxygenation or hemodynamic instability.

RT Role & Team Setup

Before the Exchange
Verify order/indication · assess current tube size/depth · gather equipment · preoxygenate per protocol · ensure suction works · prepare ventilator/circuit · confirm provider and nurse are ready · communicate backup plan.
During the Exchange
Monitor SpO₂, HR, BP, and waveform · assist with suction/oxygenation · hand equipment to provider · protect airway catheter position if assigned · be ready to bag or reconnect ventilator immediately.
After the Exchange
Inflate cuff · attach CO₂ detector/capnography · reconnect ventilator/BVM · confirm chest rise and breath sounds · note depth · secure tube · reassess settings/alarms · document.
Safety Mindset
Do not remove a functioning airway until the team has a complete rescue plan. A controlled exchange can become a cannot-ventilate emergency very quickly.

Airway Exchange Catheter, Bougie, Stylet — Do Not Mix Them Up

Airway Exchange Catheter
A hollow, semi-rigid catheter placed through the existing ETT before removal. It helps maintain airway access while the replacement tube is advanced over it. Some allow oxygen insufflation/jet ventilation only per device instructions and provider order.
Bougie
A solid introducer commonly used during difficult intubation to guide an ETT into the trachea. It is not the same as an airway exchange catheter and does not typically provide oxygenation.
Stylet
A malleable shaping device placed inside the ETT before intubation. It should not extend beyond the tube tip and is removed after tube placement.
NBRC Trap: An airway exchange catheter can help preserve airway access during planned tube exchange. A bougie guides difficult intubation. A stylet shapes the ETT. The names are not interchangeable.

Planned ET Tube Exchange Workflow

  1. Verify the indication, order, provider presence, and backup airway plan.
  2. Assess current tube size, depth at teeth/lip, cuff pressure, ventilator settings, SpO₂, and patient stability.
  3. Prepare replacement ETT, backup tube sizes, cuff syringe, lubricant, securing device, suction, BVM, and CO₂ confirmation device.
  4. Preoxygenate and suction as appropriate.
  5. Provider inserts airway exchange catheter through existing ETT to appropriate depth per device/protocol.
  6. Existing ETT is withdrawn while catheter position is maintained.
  7. Replacement ETT is advanced over catheter into position.
  8. Catheter is removed after tube placement is secured/controlled by provider.
  9. Inflate cuff, connect BVM or ventilator, and confirm placement immediately.
  10. Secure tube, document depth, reassess ventilation, check alarms, and obtain CXR if ordered.
Critical Procedure Principle: Do not delay confirmation. After tube exchange, the airway is not considered safe until ventilation, chest rise, breath sounds, exhaled CO₂, tube depth, cuff seal, and patient response are verified.

Placement Confirmation After Exchange

Confirmation uses multiple findings. No single bedside observation should be used alone when the stakes are high.

Primary Confirmation
Continuous waveform capnography is preferred when available. Sustained exhaled CO₂ over multiple breaths supports tracheal placement and ventilation.
Clinical Confirmation
Bilateral chest rise · bilateral breath sounds · absent gastric insufflation · improved SpO₂ · stable/recovering HR and BP · appropriate exhaled tidal volume on ventilator.
Tube Position
Document depth at teeth/lip, compare with previous position, assess for right mainstem signs, and obtain CXR per policy/order.
Red Flags
No exhaled CO₂ · absent chest rise · unilateral breath sounds · rapidly falling SpO₂ · high pressure with no ventilation · large cuff leak or inability to ventilate.

Loss-of-Airway Rescue: What Must Be Ready

  • Call for help early. A failing exchange is not a one-person problem. Notify provider/anesthesia/airway team per facility process.
  • Oxygenate first. Use BVM with 100% oxygen when disconnected from the ventilator or when ventilation is uncertain.
  • Suction aggressively but safely. Secretions, blood, or vomit can rapidly turn an airway exchange into a failed airway.
  • Use exhaled CO₂ to confirm ventilation. Sustained waveform capnography is stronger confirmation than a single observation of chest rise.
  • Prepare for reintubation or surgical airway. The backup plan must be available before tube manipulation begins, not after the airway is lost.

FAQ

Common Questions

Is an airway exchange catheter the same as a bougie?

No. An airway exchange catheter is designed for planned exchange through an existing artificial airway and may preserve airway access while a new tube is advanced over it. A bougie is commonly used as an introducer during difficult intubation. A stylet shapes the ETT. The equipment is not interchangeable.

What should be ready before the old tube is removed?

Replacement ETT and backup sizes, airway exchange catheter or provider-requested guide, suction, BVM with oxygen, ventilator/circuit ready, CO₂ confirmation device, stethoscope, securing device, cuff syringe, monitoring, and rescue airway equipment.

Can SpO₂ alone confirm correct placement?

No. SpO₂ can remain temporarily normal even with a misplaced airway, especially after preoxygenation. Placement confirmation requires sustained exhaled CO₂, chest rise, breath sounds, tube depth assessment, and patient response. CXR may be used to confirm depth.

Summary & Board-Prep Reminders

Prepare Before Tube Movement
Tube exchange is safest when all replacement, rescue, oxygenation, suction, and confirmation equipment is ready before the existing airway is manipulated.
Use CO₂ Confirmation
Sustained waveform capnography is preferred after exchange. No exhaled CO₂ is a red flag until proven otherwise.
Know the Tools
Airway exchange catheter preserves access during exchange; bougie guides difficult intubation; stylet shapes the tube.
Monitor Afterward
Check tube depth, cuff seal, breath sounds, exhaled VT, alarms, SpO₂, pressure trends, and need for CXR.
