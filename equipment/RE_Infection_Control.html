Infection Control, PPE, & Isolation Precautions

Respiratory therapists work in close proximity to patients with serious respiratory illness — and their procedures generate aerosols that increase exposure risk. This lesson builds the foundational infection control knowledge and psychomotor skills every RT needs before entering the lab or clinical environment.

Introduction to Infection Control in Respiratory Care

Respiratory therapists are among the most frequently exposed healthcare workers. Your procedures place you face-to-face with patients who are coughing, intubated, and often fighting serious respiratory illness. Understanding infection control is not optional — it protects you, your coworkers, and every patient you touch.

Healthcare-Associated Infections (HAIs)
Infections acquired during the course of healthcare that were not present at admission. RTs are directly linked to HAI prevention through equipment hygiene and proper precautions.
RT Aerosol Exposure Risk
Many RT procedures generate aerosols — tiny airborne particles that linger in the environment. This creates elevated exposure risk compared to most other bedside procedures.
Vulnerable Patient Populations
Patients in respiratory care are often immunocompromised, elderly, or have chronic lung disease. They are at the highest risk of serious infection from what you might carry in — or out.

High-Risk RT Procedures

The following procedures carry elevated infection risk because of direct airway contact, aerosol generation, or equipment contamination potential:

Aerosol therapy
Nebulizers disperse medication and respiratory secretions into the environment.
Suctioning
Nasotracheal and endotracheal suctioning create direct airway contact and aerosol.
Intubation & HFNC
Both disturb the upper airway significantly and produce high-velocity aerosol.
NIV, Bronchoscopy, Ventilator Care
All create sustained aerosol exposure risk and require equipment contamination management.

Cross-contamination — transferring pathogens between patients, surfaces, or equipment — is a primary HAI mechanism in respiratory care. Your hands, your equipment, and your PPE are all vectors.

Chain of Infection

Infection requires a complete chain. Breaking any single link prevents transmission. As an RT, you interact with nearly every link — which means you have many opportunities to stop infection from spreading.

① Infectious AgentThe pathogen itself (bacteria, virus, fungus)
② ReservoirWhere the pathogen lives and multiplies (patient, equipment, surface)
③ Portal of ExitHow the pathogen leaves the reservoir (cough, secretions, wound)
④ Mode of TransmissionHow it travels (contact, droplet, airborne)
⑤ Portal of EntryHow it enters a new host (mucous membranes, airways, wounds)
⑥ Susceptible HostA person whose immune defenses cannot stop infection
RT Pearl: Proper hand hygiene breaks the mode of transmission link. PPE breaks the portal of entry link. Proper equipment disposal targets the reservoir link. You can break the chain at multiple points.

Types of Infectious Organisms

Respiratory therapists encounter multiple types of pathogens. Each behaves differently, requires different precautions, and responds differently to standard cleaning practices.

🦠 Bacteria
MRSA — methicillin-resistant; contact precautions; survives on surfaces for weeks.
Streptococcus pneumoniae — common pneumonia cause; droplet spread.
Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB) — airborne; acid-fast; requires negative pressure room.
🔬 Viruses
Influenza — droplet/airborne; seasonal; highly contagious.
RSV — contact and droplet; severe in infants and elderly.
COVID-19 — airborne capable; AGP risk elevated; N95 required.
🍄 Fungi
Aspergillus — opportunistic; inhaled spores; serious in immunocompromised patients.
Pneumocystis jirovecii (PJP) — causes PCP pneumonia; seen in HIV/AIDS and immunosuppressed patients.

Key Concepts

Colonization vs. Infection
Colonization = pathogen present but causing no harm. Infection = pathogen causing active disease. A colonized patient can still transmit.
Opportunistic Infection
Infections caused by organisms that normally do not harm healthy individuals — but become dangerous in immunocompromised patients (transplant, HIV, chemotherapy).
Antibiotic Resistance
Overuse of antibiotics creates resistant organisms (MRSA, VRE, ESBL). These require stricter contact precautions and are harder to eliminate from equipment.

Modes of Transmission

Understanding how pathogens travel is essential for selecting correct precautions. The mode of transmission determines what PPE you need and what room type is required.

Contact Transmission

  • Direct: Physical contact between RT and patient (touching, wound care)
  • Indirect: Contact with contaminated surface or equipment (ambu bag, stethoscope)
  • Examples: MRSA, C. diff, VRE

Droplet Transmission

  • Large respiratory droplets (>5 µm) — fall within 3–6 feet
  • Produced by coughing, sneezing, talking
  • Examples: Influenza, RSV, Pertussis, Strep pneumoniae

Airborne Transmission

  • Small droplet nuclei (<5 µm) — remain suspended in air
  • Can travel long distances; persist in environment
  • Examples: TB, Measles, Varicella (chickenpox)
Aerosol Transmission (RT-Specific): RT procedures like nebulizers, HFNC, NIV, suctioning, and intubation generate aerosols that behave like airborne particles — even for diseases normally classified as droplet. This is why PPE escalation during AGPs is critical.

Standard Precautions

Standard precautions are the minimum level of infection control applied to every patient, regardless of diagnosis or suspected infection status. The foundational principle: treat all patients as potentially infectious at all times.

Hand Hygiene
Before and after every patient contact. The single most effective infection control measure.
PPE Based on Exposure Risk
Gloves for contact with blood/body fluids. Mask and eye protection for splash risk. Gown for contamination risk.
Respiratory Hygiene / Cough Etiquette
Masks on symptomatic patients. Tissues and hand hygiene available at entry. Spatial separation of coughing patients.
Sharps Safety & Waste Handling
Never recap needles. Sharps containers at point of use. Proper disposal of contaminated supplies.
RT Clinical Note: Standard precautions apply even before you know a patient's diagnosis. A patient admitted for "COPD exacerbation" may also have active TB or influenza. Your standard precautions must be on before you walk through the door.

Hand Hygiene

Hand hygiene is the most critical and most frequently performed infection control action in healthcare. No PPE replaces it. No shortcut substitutes for it.

Alcohol-Based Hand Rub (ABHR)
Preferred for most situations. Fast, effective, less drying. Use when hands are not visibly soiled.
Soap & Water — Required When:
Hands are visibly soiled or contaminated with blood/body fluids. After contact with a patient with C. difficile (ABHR does NOT kill spores). After using the restroom.
Supplies (Competency Aligned)
Soap and water, alcohol-based hand rub, handwashing sink, paper towels. Remove jewelry (rings, bracelets) before beginning.

When to Perform Hand Hygiene

Follow the WHO "5 Moments for Hand Hygiene":

  • Before touching a patient
  • Before a clean or aseptic procedure
  • After body fluid exposure risk
  • After touching a patient
  • After touching patient surroundings
Critical Pearl: Gloves do not replace hand hygiene. Perform hand hygiene before donning and after removing gloves every time.

Hand Hygiene Procedure

Both techniques must be mastered for the competency. The steps below follow the lab manual sequence.

Alcohol-Based Hand Rub (ABHR) Technique

  1. Inspect hands for cuts, open wounds, or skin breakdown
  2. Remove jewelry (rings, bracelets, watches)
  3. Dispense the recommended amount of ABHR into palm
  4. Rub all hand surfaces — palms, backs, between fingers, fingertips, thumbs, wrists
  5. Continue rubbing until hands are completely dry — do not wipe off

PPE Equipment Overview

Selecting the correct PPE requires knowing what each item protects against — and what its limitations are. Wearing the wrong PPE, or wearing it incorrectly, may provide no protection at all.

🧤 Gloves
Protect against contact transmission. Use for all patient contact when exposure to blood/body fluids is possible. Change between patients — never reuse. Do not touch your face while gloved.
Failure risk: Wrong size causes tearing; gloves with holes may not be visible.
🥼 Gown
Protects clothing and skin from splashes and contact contamination. Required for contact precautions and any procedure with high splash risk. Tie securely at neck and waist.
Front surface is considered contaminated once worn.
👁️ Eye Protection
Goggles: Seal around the eyes; better for splash protection.
Face shields: Cover face and neck; better for aerosol procedures.
Required whenever splash, spray, or aerosol risk exists — including suctioning and intubation.
😷 Surgical Mask
Provides droplet precautions protection. Protects against large respiratory droplets. Does NOT filter airborne particles. Must fit snugly over nose and mouth.
Limitation: Not protective against airborne pathogens or aerosol-generating procedures.
🛡️ N95 Respirator
Filters ≥95% of airborne particles. Required for airborne precautions and most AGPs. Must be fit-tested annually. Perform a seal check before each use (positive and negative pressure).
An improperly fitted N95 may provide little to no airborne protection.
🌬️ PAPR
Powered Air-Purifying Respirator. Provides highest level of respiratory protection. Used for known highly infectious respiratory illness, bronchoscopy, or when N95 cannot achieve proper fit.
Advantage: No fit testing required. Disadvantage: Bulky, battery-dependent, requires cleaning.

Doffing PPE

Doffing is more dangerous than donning. The outside of your PPE is contaminated — and the goal is to remove it without transferring that contamination to yourself. Most healthcare exposure events occur during doffing.

1st
🧤 Gloves
Most contaminated — remove first
2nd
🥼 Gown
Roll outward — front is contaminated
3rd
😷 Mask / Respirator
Handle by ties/bands only
4th
👁️ Eye Protection
Handle by temples or strap
After Doffing: Perform hand hygiene before leaving the patient room. Perform hand hygiene again after exiting the room. Never touch your face during or after doffing until hand hygiene is complete.

Common Doffing Errors

  • Touching the front of the gown (contaminated surface) with bare hands during removal
  • Pulling the N95 off by grabbing the front — always use the bands/ties only
  • Removing eye protection before mask, reversing the correct sequence
  • Skipping hand hygiene between doffing steps or before leaving the room
  • Re-entering the room after doffing to retrieve forgotten equipment

Isolation Precautions

Transmission-based precautions are added on top of standard precautions whenever a patient has a known or suspected communicable illness. You must know which precaution level applies — and what PPE each requires.

Contact Precautions

PPE Required: Gloves + Gown

Room: Private room preferred

  • MRSA, VRE, C. diff
  • Multi-drug resistant organisms (MDROs)
  • Wound infections, impetigo, scabies

Droplet Precautions

PPE Required: Surgical Mask + Eye Protection

Room: Private room; door may remain open

  • Influenza, RSV, Pertussis
  • Strep pneumoniae, Meningitis
  • Mumps, Rubella

Airborne Precautions

PPE Required: N95 or PAPR + Eye Protection

Room: Negative pressure room required; door must remain closed

  • Tuberculosis (TB), Measles, Varicella
  • COVID-19 (for AGPs)
  • Suspected airborne illness pending workup
Negative Pressure Rooms: Air flows inward, preventing contaminated air from leaving the room. Required for all airborne precaution patients. The door must remain closed at all times. Confirm negative pressure status before entering.

Aerosol-Generating Procedures (AGPs)

AGPs produce aerosols — tiny particles that behave like airborne transmission even when the underlying disease is classified as droplet-spread. This is the principle that drove N95 use during COVID-19 and is fundamental to RT practice at all times.

RT-Specific AGPs

Nebulizer Therapy
Converts medication to aerosol. Can aerosolize respiratory secretions as patient exhales through the device.
High-Flow Nasal Cannula (HFNC)
High flow rates (up to 60 L/min) aerosolize secretions from the airway. Significant AGP risk in COVID and influenza.
Non-Invasive Ventilation (NIV)
BiPAP/CPAP mask creates pressurized airflow. Vented masks release exhaled air directly into the room environment.
Endotracheal Intubation
Highest AGP risk. Direct airway manipulation with aerosolization of tracheal contents during laryngoscopy and tube insertion.
Bronchoscopy
Instrumentation of the lower airways causes massive aerosol release. Requires full airborne-level PPE regardless of diagnosis.
Suctioning (NT and ET)
Open suctioning directly aerosolizes secretions. Closed circuit reduces but does not eliminate risk.

PPE Escalation During AGPs

Precaution levels during AGPs may exceed what the patient's diagnosis would normally require:

Escalation Rule: Standard → Droplet → Airborne/N95 → PAPR. When performing an AGP on a patient with a respiratory illness, escalate to at minimum N95-level protection — regardless of the listed isolation category. Facility policy may require PAPR for highest-risk procedures (intubation, bronchoscopy).

RT Equipment Contamination & Troubleshooting

RT equipment is a major cross-contamination vector. Understanding what is disposable, what is reusable, and how to handle each type is an essential component of infection control.

Equipment Contamination Risk

Nebulizers
Single-patient use or disposable. Nebulizer cups harbor pathogens — never share between patients.
Ventilator Circuits
Contamination accumulates in the circuit and humidifier. Change per facility policy (typically every 7 days or when visibly soiled). Never drain condensate back into the humidifier.
Suction Catheters
Single-use only. Dispose after each use. Yankauer devices require regular rinsing and replacement per protocol.
Manual Resuscitators (Ambu Bags)
High-touch, high-risk item. Single-patient use in most facilities. Reusable types require high-level disinfection between patients.
HFNC Systems
Humidifier chamber and nasal cannula are patient-specific. Do not reuse between patients.
Humidifiers
Can grow Pseudomonas and other gram-negative bacteria if water accumulates. Use sterile water only. Refill only when empty.

Escalation & RT Clinical Pearls

Escalation of infection precautions should occur before a diagnosis is confirmed whenever clinical signs suggest a serious respiratory illness. Waiting for lab results puts you at risk.

When to Escalate

  • Unknown or unconfirmed respiratory illness with cough or fever
  • Any aerosol-generating procedure — regardless of listed precaution level
  • Suspected or confirmed TB, measles, or varicella
  • Severe coughing, hemoptysis, or unexpected respiratory deterioration
  • Immunocompromised patient with atypical presentation
Escalation Pathway: Standard Precautions → Droplet Precautions → Airborne Precautions + N95 → Airborne + PAPR (highest risk procedures)

RT Clinical Pearls

  • Gloves do not replace hand hygiene — perform hand hygiene even after glove removal.
  • Alcohol-based hand rub is ineffective against C. difficile spores — soap and water required.
  • The front surface of your gown and the front of your mask are considered contaminated the moment you put them on in a patient's room.
  • An improperly fitted N95 may provide little to no protection — fit check every single time.
  • AGPs can create airborne-level risk even with droplet-only pathogens. Escalate first, ask questions later.
  • Explain isolation to your patient — it reduces anxiety and improves cooperation.

Common NBRC Traps

The NBRC tests infection control knowledge through clinical scenarios. These are the most commonly missed traps on board-style questions:

  • Entering an airborne isolation room wearing only a surgical mask. A surgical mask does not filter airborne particles. TB, measles, and varicella require an N95 or PAPR.
  • Removing the N95 before fully exiting the airborne room. The respirator must remain on until you are outside the room with the door closed behind you.
  • Forgetting eye protection during suctioning or intubation. These are AGPs with splash risk — eye protection is required in addition to respiratory PPE.
  • Not escalating PPE when performing an AGP on a droplet-precautions patient. The procedure determines the PPE, not just the diagnosis. Escalate for nebulizers, HFNC, NIV, and suctioning.
  • Reaching for alcohol gel after caring for a C. diff patient. ABHR does not kill C. difficile spores. Soap and water is required after contact with C. diff patients, even with intact gloves.
  • Assuming colonization means no isolation is needed. MRSA-colonized patients still require contact precautions — colonized patients can transmit organisms to others.

FAQ

Common Questions

What is the difference between droplet and airborne precautions?

Droplet precautions protect against large respiratory droplets (>5 µm) that fall within 3–6 feet and do not remain suspended in air. A surgical mask provides adequate protection. Airborne precautions protect against droplet nuclei (<5 µm) that can remain suspended in the air for extended periods and travel beyond 6 feet. An N95 or PAPR and a negative pressure room are required. TB, measles, and varicella require airborne precautions. Influenza and RSV require droplet — unless an AGP is being performed.

When is soap and water required instead of alcohol-based hand rub?

Soap and water is required when: (1) hands are visibly soiled with blood, body fluids, or other material; (2) after caring for a patient with C. difficile — ABHR does not kill C. diff spores, and physical removal by washing is necessary; and (3) after using the restroom. In all other situations, ABHR is equally or more effective and preferred for its speed and skin-protective properties.

Why must N95 respirators be fit-tested?

N95 respirators only work when they form a complete seal against the wearer's face. Face shapes vary significantly — a respirator that seals properly for one person may allow significant air leakage for another. Fit testing (required annually per OSHA) determines which specific model and size provides an adequate seal for each individual. Without proper fit, an N95 may provide no more protection than a surgical mask against airborne pathogens. In addition to annual fit testing, a seal check (user seal check) must be performed before each use.

Why are aerosol-generating procedures considered high risk?

AGPs produce fine aerosol particles that can remain suspended in room air for extended periods — similar to airborne transmission. This means even diseases normally spread only by droplet (like influenza) can become airborne-capable during procedures like nebulization, HFNC, suctioning, NIV, and intubation. The RT is typically the provider performing these high-risk procedures, making proper AGP PPE selection one of the most important infection control decisions in respiratory care.

Can gloves replace hand hygiene?

No. Gloves reduce the risk of direct hand contamination, but they do not eliminate it. Gloves can develop microscopic tears, and contamination can occur during donning or doffing. Hand hygiene must be performed both before donning gloves and after removing them — every time. Wearing gloves without performing hand hygiene before and after represents a failure of standard precautions.

Why is doffing more dangerous than donning?

During donning, you are applying clean PPE to a clean body. During doffing, you are removing contaminated PPE without contaminating yourself. The front of your gown and the front of your gloves are the most contaminated surfaces — and those surfaces must be kept away from your face, clothing, and bare skin during removal. Most healthcare-associated exposure events occur during doffing, which is why the sequence is strict and hand hygiene steps are required multiple times during the process.

Summary & Board-Prep Reminders

Infection control in respiratory care requires consistent application of principles across every patient encounter, every procedure, and every piece of equipment. The following are the highest-yield concepts from this lesson:

Hand Hygiene First
Perform before and after every patient contact, before donning, and after doffing. Soap and water for C. diff and visible soiling. ABHR for all other situations.
Isolation = Precaution + Room + PPE
Contact: gloves + gown. Droplet: mask + eye protection. Airborne: N95 + negative pressure room. All types stack on standard precautions.
AGPs Change the PPE Rule
Nebulizers, HFNC, NIV, intubation, bronchoscopy, and suctioning all require escalated PPE regardless of listed isolation level. When in doubt, escalate.
Doffing Is the Danger Zone
Remove gloves first, gown second, mask third, eye protection last. Hand hygiene before leaving and after exiting. Never remove N95 inside an airborne room.
High-Yield NBRC Reminders: Surgical mask ≠ airborne protection · ABHR does not kill C. diff spores · N95 must stay on until outside the airborne room · AGPs require PPE escalation · Gloves do not replace hand hygiene
