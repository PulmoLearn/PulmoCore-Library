Respiratory Equipment • Competency Guide

Airway Management Equipment & Procedures

Learn how to assess airway difficulty, open and ventilate the airway, select basic and rescue airway devices, prepare for intubation, confirm tube placement, manage cuff pressure and malposition, and recognize when airway failure requires immediate escalation.

Before you begin

Airway management is one of the most time-sensitive responsibilities in respiratory care. The equipment matters, but the sequence matters even more. The respiratory therapist must recognize whether the airway is open, whether the patient can protect it, whether ventilation is effective, whether a basic airway is enough, and when a rescue or definitive airway is required.

For the 2027 Respiratory Therapy Examination (RTE), Breadth of Knowledge includes airway anatomy, difficult-airway clues, maneuvers, mask technique, manual resuscitators, airway-device sizing, laryngoscope blades, endotracheal tube components, cuff pressure, placement confirmation, and emergency airway equipment. Depth of Clinical Judgment means recognizing airway failure, choosing the right intervention in the right order, identifying failed ventilation or malposition, and escalating without delaying oxygenation or ventilation.

Big idea: Follow the chain airway assessment → open airway → oxygenate/ventilate → basic adjunct → rescue/definitive airway → confirm → secure → monitor → troubleshoot → escalate.

Core vocabulary: glottis, cricoid cartilage, epiglottis, cricothyroid membrane, Mallampati classification, thyromental distance, head-tilt chin-lift, jaw thrust, E-C clamp, bag-valve-mask (BVM), self-inflating resuscitator, flow-inflating resuscitator, T-piece resuscitator, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), oropharyngeal airway (OPA), nasopharyngeal airway (NPA), laryngeal mask airway (LMA), Combitube, endotracheal tube (ETT), Murphy eye, pilot balloon, cuff manometer, Miller blade, Macintosh blade, stylet, bougie, capnography, end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO₂), right-mainstem intubation, tracheostomy, obturator, inner cannula, cricothyrotomy, extubation.

Know the anatomy that changes airway decisions

The lesson emphasizes the glottis as the narrowest airway point in adults and the cricoid region as the narrowest point taught for infants/young children. The pediatric airway is also more anterior and cephalad, and the epiglottis is longer and more floppy. These differences help explain pediatric tube sizing and why a straight Miller blade is commonly useful in infants.

Difficult-airway predictors

No single bedside finding perfectly predicts intubation difficulty. The lesson asks students to combine findings such as small mouth opening, micrognathia, overbite, short neck, limited neck movement, thyromental distance less than 6 cm, Mallampati class III–IV, prior difficult intubation, obstructive sleep apnea, neck radiation/surgery/trauma, cervical instability, obesity, and airway edema.

Head-tilt chin-lift versus jaw thrust

Head-tilt chin-lift opens the airway by extending the head and lifting the tongue away from the posterior pharynx. Use it for an unconscious patient when cervical spine injury is not suspected.

Jaw thrust lifts the mandible anteriorly without intentionally extending or rotating the neck. The lesson identifies it as the airway-opening maneuver for suspected cervical spine injury.

Patient-first safety: A trauma patient with agonal respirations and SpO₂ 82% needs the airway opened now. Use jaw thrust with cervical stabilization while definitive airway support is prepared; do not leave the airway obstructed while waiting for imaging.
Putting C1 together
Assess the anatomy and clinical context before the airway emergency progresses. Recognize difficult-airway clues, preserve cervical alignment when trauma is suspected, and choose the maneuver that opens the airway with the least additional risk.

Effective bag-mask ventilation starts with an open airway and a seal

A manual resuscitator cannot ventilate effectively through an obstructed airway or a leaking mask. Position the airway first, use an appropriate adjunct when needed, and create a seal before blaming the bag.

E-C clamp technique

The lesson uses the E-C clamp for mask ventilation: the thumb and index finger form a “C” around the mask while the remaining fingers lift the mandible to create an “E.” The goal is to press the mask onto the face while also lifting the jaw into the mask rather than simply pushing the face backward.

When one-person ventilation produces a poor seal, moving to a two-person technique can improve jaw lift and mask seal while the second rescuer squeezes the bag.

Troubleshoot poor chest rise

  1. Reposition/open the airway.
  2. Improve the mask seal; use two-person technique when needed.
  3. Insert/select an appropriate airway adjunct if indicated.
  4. Verify the bag, valve, oxygen source, reservoir, and connections.
  5. Look for obstruction or severe airway pathology.
  6. If effective ventilation still cannot be established, escalate to a rescue/definitive airway.
Common mistake: Squeezing the bag harder when the mask seal or airway position is poor. More force does not fix a leak and can increase gastric inflation.
Putting C2 together
Choose the resuscitator that fits the patient and clinical setting, open the airway, create an effective seal, verify the equipment, watch for chest rise, and escalate when manual ventilation remains ineffective.

OPA sizing

The lesson sizes the OPA from the corner of the mouth to the angle of the mandible. A device that is too small may push the tongue backward; one that is too large can traumatize the airway or displace the epiglottis.

NPA sizing

The lesson sizes the NPA from the nostril to the earlobe/tragus region. Lubricate with a water-soluble product and advance gently along the floor of the nose rather than aiming upward.

Supraglottic rescue airways

The laryngeal mask airway (LMA) sits above the glottis and can provide a rescue ventilation pathway when mask ventilation or intubation is difficult. It is faster to place than an ETT but does not provide the same aspiration protection as a cuffed endotracheal tube.

The lesson also introduces the Combitube as an emergency rescue airway that can permit ventilation depending on whether the tube enters the esophagus or trachea. The clinical principle is rescue oxygenation/ventilation when a definitive airway is not immediately achievable.

Clinical example

A completely unresponsive patient has no gag reflex and needs BVM ventilation. The lesson favors an OPA because the absent gag reflex removes the major contraindication and the device provides reliable tongue displacement. If the patient had an intact gag reflex, an NPA would be more appropriate if no nasal contraindication were present.

RTE distinction: A supraglottic device is a rescue airway, not automatically the first device for every unconscious patient. Start with the least invasive airway that safely solves the immediate ventilation problem, then escalate if it fails.
Putting C3 together
Match the airway adjunct to consciousness, gag reflex, injury pattern, and urgency. Size and place it correctly, verify that ventilation improves, and move to a rescue or definitive airway when the adjunct cannot maintain oxygenation or ventilation.

Prepare the airway plan before the tube is needed

Intubation preparation includes more than handing the provider a laryngoscope. The respiratory therapist should anticipate oxygenation, suction, manual ventilation, tube size, backup tube/device, blade, stylet or bougie, cuff inflation, capnography, securing equipment, and rescue-airway needs.

Miller versus Macintosh

Miller blade: straight; used to directly lift the epiglottis. The lesson highlights its frequent use in infants because of pediatric airway anatomy.

Macintosh blade: curved; placed in the vallecula to indirectly elevate the epiglottis. It is commonly used in adults.

Know the ETT parts

Important components include the tube connector, depth markings, radiopaque line, inflatable cuff, inflation line, pilot balloon/valve, bevel, and Murphy eye. The cuff seals the trachea for positive-pressure ventilation and helps reduce gross leakage/aspiration around the tube; the Murphy eye provides an alternate gas path if the bevel becomes occluded.

Confirm placement in layers

The lesson identifies continuous waveform capnography as the fastest reliable bedside confirmation of tracheal placement and chest radiography as the most reliable method taught for final depth/position verification. Other bedside clues include bilateral chest rise, bilateral breath sounds, absence of gastric sounds, improving oxygenation, and appropriate tube depth—but no single secondary sign should replace carbon dioxide confirmation when available.

Right-mainstem intubation

The right mainstem bronchus is more vertical, so an ETT inserted too deeply most commonly enters the right mainstem. The classic pattern is good ventilation of the right lung with markedly decreased or absent left breath sounds. Correct by withdrawing the tube to appropriate tracheal depth while maintaining airway control, then reconfirm.

Esophageal placement

Persistent absence of exhaled carbon dioxide with poor chest rise, gastric inflation, worsening oxygenation, and inability to ventilate should raise immediate concern for esophageal placement. Remove the incorrectly placed tube and resume effective oxygenation/ventilation while the airway is reattempted according to the emergency plan.

Patient-first safety: Do not wait for a chest radiograph to correct obvious esophageal or dangerously deep mainstem placement in a deteriorating patient. Restore effective ventilation first, then obtain final position confirmation.

The course cuff-pressure target is 20–30 cmH₂O

A cuff must create an adequate seal without applying excessive pressure to tracheal mucosa. The lesson target is 20–30 cmH₂O, measured with a cuff manometer.

FindingWhy it mattersCourse response
Cuff <20 cmH₂O + audible leakPoor seal, loss of tidal volume, increased aspiration riskUse cuff manometer to increase pressure into 20–30 cmH₂O target and reassess leak/ventilation
Cuff >30 cmH₂OExcess tracheal-wall pressure and mucosal ischemia/injury riskReduce to target while maintaining adequate seal; investigate persistent leak rather than overinflating

Artificial airway position can change after placement

A tube confirmed earlier can move with transport, repositioning, procedures, traction, or inadequate securement. Reassess depth markings, bilateral breath sounds, exhaled CO₂, ventilator volumes/pressures, and patient response after movement or when unexpected deterioration occurs.

Tracheostomy components

The lesson includes the tracheostomy tube, faceplate/flange, cuff when present, pilot balloon, inner cannula, obturator used during insertion, and tracheostomy ties/securement. The inner cannula may be removable for cleaning/replacement depending on design; the obturator is removed after insertion and should not remain in the patient's airway during ventilation.

Common mistake: Treating every cuff leak by adding more air. If the cuff is already within or above the target range, investigate tube position, cuff integrity, tube size, or circuit leak instead of creating tracheal injury.
Putting C5 together
Use cuff pressure, tube depth, breath sounds, exhaled CO₂, ventilator data, and the patient's condition together. Correct the cause of the problem rather than treating one isolated number.

Escalation is a response to failure, not a fixed equipment sequence

The airway plan should become more advanced when the current strategy cannot maintain oxygenation, ventilation, or airway protection. The therapist must recognize failure early enough that there is still time to act.

Basic airway support may be enough

An unresponsive patient with tongue obstruction, no gag reflex, and otherwise manageable anatomy may improve with positioning, OPA, and effective BVM ventilation. A rescue supraglottic airway is not automatically required before basic measures are tried.

When to move beyond BVM/basic adjuncts

Escalation is required when mask ventilation remains ineffective despite repositioning, improved seal, appropriate adjuncts, and functioning equipment; when airway protection is lost; when prolonged ventilatory support is required; or when upper-airway pathology prevents adequate ventilation.

Rescue supraglottic airway

If endotracheal intubation is unsuccessful and ventilation/oxygenation must be restored quickly, an LMA or other rescue supraglottic device may provide a bridge. The purpose is to restore gas exchange while a definitive plan is developed.

Cricothyrotomy

The lesson identifies the cricothyroid membrane as the emergency surgical-airway site. Cricothyrotomy is the emergency pathway when a patient cannot be oxygenated/ventilated by less invasive methods and a definitive airway cannot be established through the usual route.

Artificial-airway exchange and extubation

Airway exchange may be needed when an artificial airway is damaged, obstructed, incorrectly sized, or otherwise unsafe. Extubation is appropriate only when the patient can maintain airway patency/protection and adequate spontaneous respiratory function according to the broader clinical plan. Removing a tube because it is inconvenient is never the goal; the patient must be ready to function without it.

Emergency decision pattern

  1. Can the airway be opened?
  2. Can the patient be oxygenated and ventilated with mask/BVM?
  3. Would a basic airway adjunct improve patency?
  4. Can a definitive ETT be placed safely?
  5. If intubation fails, can a rescue supraglottic airway restore ventilation?
  6. If the patient cannot be intubated and cannot be oxygenated/ventilated, is an emergency surgical airway required?
Patient-first safety: Repeated failed intubation attempts while oxygen saturation is falling are not progress. Restore oxygenation with the best available rescue method and move to the next airway strategy.
Putting C6 together
Recognize when the current airway strategy is failing, protect oxygenation and ventilation, move from basic to rescue to definitive airway support as needed, and understand when airway exchange, extubation, or emergency surgical access becomes the appropriate next step.

Airway management: oxygenation and ventilation come before perfect procedure

The safest airway clinician does not merely know the devices. They recognize airway failure early, choose the least invasive device that can solve the immediate problem, verify that it worked, and escalate before the patient loses oxygenation, ventilation, or airway protection.

assess → open → ventilate → adjunct → definitive/rescue airway → confirm → secure → monitor → troubleshoot → escalate

RTE Depth of Clinical Judgment: take the next step

  • Information Gathering: evaluate consciousness, gag reflex, airway patency, facial/neck trauma, cervical-spine risk, Mallampati/difficult-airway features, oxygenation, breathing pattern, and ability to ventilate.
  • Information Gathering: after intubation, assess exhaled CO₂, chest rise, bilateral breath sounds, tube depth, cuff pressure, ventilator volumes/pressures, SpO₂, and patient response.
  • Decision Making: choose OPA + BVM for an unresponsive patient without a gag reflex rather than moving immediately to a more invasive rescue device when basic ventilation has not yet been attempted.
  • Decision Making: use jaw thrust during suspected cervical-spine injury rather than head extension.
  • Decision Making: improve mask seal/airway position and use an adjunct before simply squeezing the bag harder.
  • Decision Making: recognize absent left breath sounds after intubation as probable right-mainstem placement and withdraw/reconfirm rather than treating it as primary unilateral lung disease first.
  • Decision Making: correct a 14 cmH₂O cuff with an air leak toward the 20–30 cmH₂O target rather than reintubating or changing ventilator settings.
  • Decision Making: avoid blindly adding cuff volume when cuff pressure is already adequate/high and investigate displacement, cuff failure, or circuit leak.
  • Decision Making: use a supraglottic rescue airway when intubation fails and ventilation must be restored.
  • Decision Making: recognize the cannot-intubate/cannot-oxygenate pattern requiring emergency surgical-airway escalation.
  • Reassessment: verify that every airway intervention restores effective oxygenation/ventilation and does not create new malposition or obstruction.
  • Communication: report airway difficulty, device used, tube depth/cuff pressure, confirmation evidence, complications, and the reason for escalation.
