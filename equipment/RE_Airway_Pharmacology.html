Airway Pharmacology — Inhaled Medication Classes, Clinical Use & Patient Safety

Respiratory therapists administer inhaled medications as a core clinical function. This lesson covers the five major inhaled medication classes, how each works, when each is used, and the safety issues every RT must recognize — with NBRC-focused activities throughout.

Introduction to Inhaled Medication Therapy

Inhaled medications are the cornerstone of respiratory care. Delivering medication directly to the airways allows lower doses than systemic medications, faster onset for bronchodilation, and reduced systemic side effects — when used correctly.

Why Inhaled?
Direct delivery to the target organ. Lower dose needed vs. systemic. Faster onset for bronchodilators (2–5 minutes vs. 30+ min for oral). Localized anti-inflammatory effect for steroids. Fewer systemic side effects when used correctly.
The RT's Role
Administer medications correctly. Teach patients proper technique. Monitor for therapeutic effect and side effects. Recognize when a medication is inappropriate for the clinical situation. Document before and after assessment.
Two Categories All RTs Must Know
Rescue Medications act quickly (minutes) to relieve acute symptoms. Controller Medications work slowly (days to weeks) to reduce inflammation and prevent symptoms — they do not provide immediate relief.

Delivery Devices — Quick Overview

This lesson focuses on medication classes. Devices are covered in depth in the aerosol delivery lesson. Brief reference only:

💨 MDI
Pressurized canister. Requires slow, coordinated inhalation. Use spacer if coordination is poor.
🌬️ DPI
Powder form. Requires fast, forceful inhalation. Not for patients in severe distress or with weak effort.
🫧 SVN
Liquid medication → aerosol via gas flow. Best for acute distress, poor coordination, or pediatric patients.
🌫️ Respimat
Soft mist inhaler. Slow, gentle mist. Less coordination needed than MDI. Used for maintenance medications.

Short-Acting Beta-2 Agonists (SABAs)

🟢 SABA Bronchodilators

RESCUE MEDICATION
Albuterol (ProAir, Ventolin, Proventil) Levalbuterol (Xopenex)
Mechanism

Stimulate beta-2 adrenergic receptors in airway smooth muscle → relaxation → bronchodilation. Rapid onset (2–5 minutes). Duration: 4–6 hours.

Indications

Acute asthma or COPD bronchospasm. Acute dyspnea. Pre-treatment before mucolytics. Exercise-induced bronchospasm. Emergency bronchodilation.

Levalbuterol vs. Albuterol

Levalbuterol is the active R-isomer of albuterol. May produce fewer cardiac side effects (less tachycardia). Often considered when albuterol causes significant HR elevation.

Side Effects to Watch: Tachycardia (most common and most NBRC-tested) · Tremors (especially hands) · Shakiness · Nervousness/anxiety · Dizziness · Palpitations · Hypokalemia with prolonged or high-dose use. If HR rises above 120–130 or patient reports significant dizziness: stop and reassess.

Long-Acting Beta-2 Agonists (LABAs)

🔵 LABA Bronchodilators

CONTROLLER MEDICATION
Salmeterol (Serevent) Formoterol (Foradil, Perforomist)
Mechanism

Same receptor as SABAs (beta-2 agonist) but with longer duration: 12+ hours. Slow onset — not appropriate for acute relief.

Clinical Use

Maintenance bronchodilation in asthma and COPD. Taken on a fixed schedule (typically twice daily). Often combined with inhaled corticosteroids (Advair, Symbicort).

NBRC Alert

LABAs must NEVER be used as monotherapy for asthma (without ICS). Not for acute rescue. Using a LABA for an acute attack instead of a SABA is a critical NBRC error.

Side Effects: Similar to SABAs but generally milder at therapeutic doses (tachycardia, tremor). Formoterol has slightly faster onset than salmeterol and is sometimes described as having mild rescue potential — but both are primarily maintenance drugs.

Rescue vs. Controller — The Most Important Distinction

🚨 Rescue Medications
Fast-acting. Used when acute symptoms begin. Provide bronchodilation within 2–5 minutes. Examples: albuterol, levalbuterol.

Used for: acute bronchospasm, wheezing attacks, sudden shortness of breath.
🛡️ Controller Medications
Slow-acting. Used every day on a schedule to prevent symptoms. Do NOT relieve acute symptoms. Examples: LABAs, ICS, tiotropium, LAMA.

Used for: long-term maintenance, reducing frequency of attacks.
The Most Dangerous Confusion: A patient in acute respiratory distress who reaches for their controller inhaler (LABA or ICS) instead of their SABA rescue inhaler will not get immediate relief — and their condition may deteriorate. The RT must always identify which type of medication is being discussed in clinical scenarios.

Anticholinergics

🟡 Anticholinergic Bronchodilators

PRIMARILY CONTROLLER — IPRATROPIUM CAN BE ACUTE USE
Ipratropium (Atrovent) — SAMA Tiotropium (Spiriva) — LAMA Ipratropium/Albuterol (Combivent)
Mechanism

Block muscarinic (acetylcholine) receptors in airway smooth muscle. Prevents parasympathetic bronchoconstriction. Also reduces airway secretion production.

Indications

COPD maintenance (tiotropium is first-line). Acute COPD exacerbation (ipratropium). Combined with albuterol in Combivent for acute COPD. Chronic asthma refractory to SABAs.

Ipratropium vs. Tiotropium

Ipratropium = short-acting (SAMA), 4–6 hours. Can be used in acute settings. Tiotropium = long-acting once daily (LAMA), maintenance only. Not for acute bronchospasm.

Side Effects: Dry mouth (most common) · Urinary retention (especially in men with BPH) · Constipation · Headache · Paradoxical bronchospasm (rare) · Eye pain or blurred vision if spray contacts eyes. Monitor patients with glaucoma or BPH carefully.

Inhaled Corticosteroids (ICS)

🟣 Inhaled Corticosteroids

CONTROLLER MEDICATION — NEVER A RESCUE DRUG
Budesonide (Pulmicort) Fluticasone (Flovent) Beclomethasone (QVAR)
Mechanism

Reduce eosinophilic airway inflammation. Decrease airway hyperresponsiveness. Reduce mucus production. Effects develop over days to weeks — NOT minutes.

Clinical Use

Persistent asthma (all grades). COPD with frequent exacerbations (in combination with LABA). Often combined with LABAs: Advair (fluticasone + salmeterol), Symbicort (budesonide + formoterol).

Critical Teaching Points

Rinse mouth and spit after EVERY use — prevents oral thrush (candidiasis). ICS is NOT a rescue medication. Will not relieve acute bronchospasm. Must be used daily as scheduled.

Mucolytics

🔴 Mucolytic Agents

ADJUNCT THERAPY — NOT FIRST-LINE BRONCHODILATORS
Acetylcysteine (Mucomyst) Dornase Alfa (Pulmozyme)
Acetylcysteine (Mucomyst)

Breaks disulfide bonds in mucoproteins, reducing mucus viscosity. Used for thick, retained secretions in COPD, cystic fibrosis, and retained secretion complications. Also used IV as acetaminophen overdose antidote.

Known for strong "rotten egg" (sulfur) odor — warn patients in advance.

Dornase Alfa (Pulmozyme)

Breaks down DNA in mucus (from dead neutrophils) — specific to cystic fibrosis. Reduces mucus viscosity and frequency of infections in CF patients.

Delivered via nebulizer. Requires refrigeration. Not used for non-CF conditions.

Critical Safety Point — Acetylcysteine Causes Bronchospasm: Acetylcysteine can trigger airway irritation and bronchospasm, especially in patients with reactive airway disease. It is often pre-treated or co-administered with a SABA (albuterol). Always have a bronchodilator available and monitor breath sounds, WOB, and SpO₂ during and after the treatment.

Topical Airway Numbing Agents

⚪ Topical Airway Anesthetics

PRE-PROCEDURE AGENTS
Lidocaine (topical/nebulized) Benzocaine (topical spray)
Purpose

Suppress gag reflex and airway irritation before bronchoscopy, nasotracheal suctioning, intubation, or airway instrumentation. Reduces cough reflex during airway procedures.

Patient Safety — MOST IMPORTANT

Aspiration precautions are critical. Gag reflex is suppressed — patient cannot protect their airway normally. NPO status required before use. Monitor closely after procedure until sensation fully returns. Risk: aspiration of food, fluids, or secretions.

Common NBRC Traps — Airway Pharmacology

  • Using an inhaled corticosteroid as a rescue drug. ICS reduce inflammation over days to weeks — they cannot relieve acute bronchospasm. A patient reaching for fluticasone during an acute asthma attack needs albuterol, not more ICS puffs.
  • Giving a DPI to a patient with severe respiratory distress. DPIs require strong, forceful inspiratory effort. A patient in acute distress cannot generate sufficient flow — medication will not reach the airways. SVN or MDI with spacer is the appropriate choice.
  • Continuing albuterol when HR is significantly elevated. Tachycardia is the most commonly tested albuterol side effect. When HR rises significantly (typically above 120–130) and the patient is symptomatic, the treatment should be stopped and the patient assessed before continuing.
  • Forgetting to rinse and spit after inhaled corticosteroids. Inhaled steroid residue in the oropharynx promotes Candida growth. This is a competency-level teaching requirement. Rinse mouth, gargle, and spit — do not swallow.
  • Not pre-treating with a SABA before acetylcysteine. Acetylcysteine can trigger bronchospasm, especially in patients with reactive airway disease. A SABA should be available (and often given) before or with every acetylcysteine treatment.
  • Using tiotropium for acute bronchospasm. Tiotropium is a once-daily LAMA — onset is too slow for acute use. Ipratropium (SAMA) can be used in acute settings; tiotropium cannot.
  • Using a LABA as monotherapy for asthma. LABAs must be used with an inhaled corticosteroid in asthma management. LABA monotherapy in asthma is associated with increased asthma-related mortality — it is a contraindicated practice.
Guided Unfolding Case

Albuterol SVN — Tachycardia & Side Effect Management

Stage 1: Recognizing the Side Effect

Situation: A 34-year-old patient with asthma is ordered albuterol 2.5 mg via SVN. The RT assesses baseline vitals: HR 92, SpO₂ 93%, RR 22, mild expiratory wheezing bilaterally. Midway through the treatment, the patient says "I feel shaky and my heart is racing." The RT reassesses: HR is now 130, the patient appears anxious, and hands are visibly trembling.

Decision: What medication class is causing these symptoms, and what is the correct immediate action?

Stage 2: Next Steps & Documentation

Update: The treatment is stopped. After 10 minutes of monitoring, HR decreases to 108 and tremors improve. The provider reviews and asks the RT for a recommendation. The patient still has persistent expiratory wheezing and SpO₂ 90%.

Decision: The provider asks whether to retry albuterol or try an alternative. What is the most appropriate RT recommendation?

Case Summary: Albuterol-induced tachycardia is the most commonly tested bronchodilator side effect on the NBRC. The correct sequence: recognize the side effect → stop the treatment → reassess → notify the provider → document. Levalbuterol is the standard alternative for patients with significant albuterol-related cardiovascular effects. Always document before-and-after vital signs, breath sounds, SpO₂, and patient-reported symptoms for every bronchodilator treatment.

FAQ

Common Questions

What is the difference between a SABA and a LABA?

Both SABAs and LABAs are beta-2 agonists — they work by stimulating beta-2 receptors in airway smooth muscle to cause bronchodilation. The key difference is duration and onset. SABAs (short-acting beta-2 agonists) like albuterol work within 2–5 minutes and last 4–6 hours — they are rescue medications. LABAs (long-acting beta-2 agonists) like salmeterol have a slow onset (15–30 minutes for salmeterol) and last 12+ hours — they are maintenance/controller medications taken on a fixed schedule. LABAs must never be used for acute relief and should not be used as monotherapy in asthma.

Why does fluticasone not help during an acute asthma attack?

Fluticasone is an inhaled corticosteroid (ICS). It works by reducing airway inflammation — specifically by reducing eosinophil activity, decreasing mucus production, and lowering airway hyperresponsiveness. These are slow cellular processes that require days to weeks to produce clinically meaningful effects. In an acute asthma attack, the problem is not primarily inflammation — it is acute bronchoconstriction (smooth muscle tightening). Only bronchodilators (beta-2 agonists like albuterol) can quickly reverse bronchoconstriction. ICS cannot relax smooth muscle acutely.

Why must you rinse your mouth after inhaled steroids?

When a patient inhales a corticosteroid, a significant fraction of the medication deposits in the mouth, throat, and pharynx — not just the lungs. This oropharyngeal deposition creates local immunosuppression, which allows Candida albicans (a yeast naturally present in the mouth) to proliferate. The result is oral candidiasis (thrush) — white plaques in the mouth and throat, hoarseness, and discomfort. Rinsing with water and spitting after each ICS dose physically removes the deposited drug before it can be absorbed or cause local immunosuppression. This simple action dramatically reduces thrush incidence.

Why does acetylcysteine cause bronchospasm?

Acetylcysteine (Mucomyst) is a directly irritating agent — its mechanism of action (breaking sulfide bonds in mucoproteins) and its chemical properties create direct airway irritation upon contact with the bronchial mucosa. This irritation can trigger a reflex bronchoconstriction response, particularly in patients with reactive airway disease (asthma, COPD). The sulfur-containing molecule is also metabolized to compounds that can trigger mast cell release of mediators. This is why a SABA (albuterol) is often administered before or alongside acetylcysteine — to pre-dilate the airways and blunt the bronchospasm response.

What is the difference between ipratropium and tiotropium?

Both are anticholinergics (muscarinic antagonists) that block parasympathetically mediated bronchoconstriction. The difference is duration: Ipratropium (Atrovent) is short-acting (SAMA) — onset within 15 minutes, duration 4–6 hours. It can be used in acute COPD exacerbations and is a component of Combivent (with albuterol). Tiotropium (Spiriva) is long-acting (LAMA) — once-daily dosing, 24+ hour duration. It is a maintenance-only medication and should NOT be used for acute bronchospasm. Tiotropium is generally considered the cornerstone of COPD pharmacologic maintenance therapy.
