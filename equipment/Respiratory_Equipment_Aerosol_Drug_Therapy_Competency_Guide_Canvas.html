Aerosol Drug Therapy — Equipment & Delivery

Learn how aerosol particle size, inhalation pattern, device design, residual volume, respiratory support, and patient technique determine whether inhaled medication reaches the intended airway target.

Before you begin

Aerosol therapy is not simply “put the medication in a device and let the patient breathe.” The respiratory therapist must understand where the particles are likely to deposit, whether the patient can use the selected device correctly, whether the device is assembled and operated as intended, and whether respiratory-support equipment changes delivery.

The 2027 Respiratory Therapy Examination (RTE) can test this lesson at two levels. Breadth of Knowledge includes particle deposition, inhaler technique, nebulizer design, residual volume, setup, and common errors. Depth of Clinical Judgment requires you to select the delivery system that matches the patient, recognize why therapy is ineffective, and decide whether technique, device, circuit configuration, or the treatment plan should change.

Big idea: Follow the chain medication target → particle size → device → patient technique → respiratory support → actual delivery → clinical response → troubleshooting → reassessment.

Core vocabulary: aerosol, mass median aerodynamic diameter (MMAD), inertial impaction, sedimentation, diffusion, metered-dose inhaler (MDI), valved holding chamber (VHC), spacer, breath-actuated inhaler, soft-mist inhaler (SMI), dry powder inhaler (DPI), small-volume nebulizer (SVN), jet nebulizer, breath-enhanced nebulizer, breath-actuated nebulizer, ultrasonic nebulizer (USN), vibrating mesh nebulizer (VMN), residual volume, dead volume, driving gas, inspiratory flow, breath hold, high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC), ventilator circuit, heat-moisture exchanger (HME).

Particle size helps determine where the drug lands

An aerosol is a suspension of solid or liquid particles in a gas. Aerosol drug therapy is valuable because medication can be delivered directly to airway smooth muscle, airway mucosa, or more distal lung regions while using a lower total dose than many systemic routes.

Mass median aerodynamic diameter (MMAD) describes aerosol particle size. It is the particle diameter at which half the aerosol mass is made of smaller particles and half is made of larger particles. In this lesson, particle size is used to predict likely deposition:

Particle size Likely deposition Mechanism emphasized in lesson
≥5 µmUpper airway/oropharynxInertial impaction
2–5 µmBronchi and bronchioles; key bronchodilator targetSedimentation
1–3 µmMore distal/alveolar depositionDiffusion contributes as particles become very small
<0.5 µmMay remain suspended and be exhaledBrownian motion / limited deposition

Why fast inhalation can waste an MDI dose

A fast inspiratory flow creates more turbulence. Larger, fast-moving particles cannot follow airway curves and impact on the mouth and pharynx. This is why the course repeatedly emphasizes slow, controlled inhalation for an MDI. Slower inhalation reduces inertial impaction and gives particles more opportunity to travel toward the lower airways.

Why a breath hold helps

Once smaller particles reach the lower airways, a breath hold gives gravity more time to settle particles onto airway surfaces by sedimentation. That is why the lesson includes breath holding after inhaler delivery whenever the patient can tolerate it.

Airway obstruction changes delivery

Narrowed airways and high respiratory rates change airflow patterns and can reduce distal aerosol delivery. A severely distressed patient may not be able to perform a long, coordinated maneuver even if that maneuver is ideal under stable conditions. Device selection must therefore account for the patient's current ability, not only the medication.

Common mistake: Thinking “smaller is always better.” Particles that are too large are lost in the upper airway, but extremely tiny particles may be exhaled before depositing. The therapeutic target determines the useful particle range.
Putting C1 together
Connect particle size with deposition location, then connect patient technique and respiratory pattern with the forces that move those particles. Good aerosol delivery requires the right particle size and a breathing pattern that allows the particles to reach the target.

MDI technique

  1. Verify the medication and dose.
  2. Shake the inhaler when required by the product.
  3. Exhale before inhalation.
  4. Seal lips around the mouthpiece.
  5. Begin a slow, deep inhalation while actuating the canister.
  6. Continue inhaling fully.
  7. Hold the breath as tolerated, up to about 10 seconds in the lesson examples.
  8. Wait the required interval before another puff when applicable.

Why a spacer or valved holding chamber helps

A spacer/VHC reduces the timing precision needed between canister actuation and inhalation. It also allows some larger, fast particles to deposit in the chamber instead of the oropharynx, improving lower-airway delivery. This makes it useful for children, patients with poor coordination, and many patients who cannot reliably synchronize actuation and inhalation.

The lesson specifically warns that pediatric “blowby”—holding a mask or nebulizer near the child's face without a seal—is ineffective because aerosol disperses rapidly. Use a properly sealed mask with a spacer/VHC or appropriate nebulizer interface.

Breath-actuated MDI

A breath-actuated MDI releases the dose when inspiratory flow triggers the device, reducing hand-breath coordination demands. It still requires enough inspiratory effort to actuate. In severe distress or very weak breathing, that triggering requirement can become a limitation.

Soft-mist inhaler

The soft-mist device in the lesson uses a spring to generate a slow-moving mist without propellant. The slower plume reduces oropharyngeal impaction and lowers coordination demands compared with a traditional MDI. The lesson technique includes loading the dose, exhaling away from the device, sealing the lips without blocking the side vents, pressing the dose-release button while inhaling slowly and deeply, and holding the breath as tolerated.

DPI technique

  1. Load the dose according to the device.
  2. Exhale fully away from the DPI so moisture does not enter the powder chamber.
  3. Seal the lips around the mouthpiece.
  4. Inhale fast, forcefully, and deeply.
  5. Hold the breath as tolerated.

A spacer does not solve inadequate DPI inspiratory flow. DPI powder is not stored in a spacer the way MDI aerosol can be held in a chamber.

RTE trap: Do not give a DPI to a severely distressed patient simply because it is breath-actuated. If the patient cannot generate reliable, forceful inspiratory flow, the powder will not aerosolize effectively.
Putting C2 together
Select the inhaler by matching device requirements with patient ability. Teach MDI/soft-mist inhalation slowly, DPI inhalation forcefully, use a spacer when coordination is poor, and verify technique by having the patient demonstrate it back.

Nebulizers do not all deliver medication with the same efficiency

Small-volume jet nebulizer

The SVN uses compressed gas to draw liquid medication into a high-velocity jet and break it into aerosol. It is especially useful in acute distress because it requires little patient-device coordination.

  1. Verify the order and medication.
  2. Add the medication to the cup.
  3. Connect air or oxygen driving gas at the lesson range of 6–8 L/min.
  4. Use a mouthpiece when possible or an appropriately sealed mask.
  5. Verify visible aerosol output.
  6. Coach slow tidal breathing with occasional deeper breaths.
  7. Keep the nebulizer cup upright.
  8. Continue until sputtering indicates treatment completion.
  9. Clean/disinfect the device according to the process being taught.

Residual volume matters

Residual volume is medication left in the nebulizer cup that cannot be aerosolized. More residual volume means more drug is wasted. Tilting a jet-nebulizer cup can move medication away from the siphon tube/jet and increase the effective residual volume, which is why the lesson repeatedly emphasizes keeping the cup upright.

Ultrasonic nebulizer

An ultrasonic nebulizer uses a piezoelectric transducer and does not require compressed gas. It can generate high aerosol output and is relatively quiet, but heat from the transducer can be a problem for heat-sensitive medications. The lesson presents it as less commonly used than VMN for many modern clinical applications.

Vibrating mesh nebulizer

The VMN moves liquid through thousands of tiny holes in a vibrating mesh. Its major lesson advantages are minimal residual volume, no added gas flow, quiet operation, controlled particle size, and no medication heating. These features are especially useful for HFNC and ventilator circuits.

Clinical example: severe asthma

A patient is gasping, tachypneic, using accessory muscles, and cannot coordinate repeated inhaler maneuvers. Even though an MDI can be effective when used correctly, the immediate equipment question is whether the patient can perform the technique reliably. The lesson favors an SVN for severe acute distress because it can deliver aerosol during tidal breathing without precise coordination.

Common mistake: Treating all visible nebulizer mist as evidence of equivalent dose delivery. Driving flow, residual volume, cup position, design, mask seal, inspiratory pattern, and circuit placement can all change how much medication reaches the patient.
Putting C3 together
Know how each nebulizer generates aerosol, what it adds to the respiratory system, where medication can be lost, and what patient/device features make one technology better than another for the situation.

Respiratory support becomes part of the aerosol-delivery system

Aerosol delivered through HFNC or a ventilator passes through additional tubing, connectors, humidification components, flow, and an artificial airway. Each part can change how much medication reaches the lower respiratory tract.

Why the VMN is preferred in these course examples

A jet nebulizer requires approximately 6–8 L/min of driving gas. In a ventilator or HFNC system, that extra gas can change total flow, FiO₂, triggering, or other operating conditions. A VMN adds zero driving-gas flow, which allows aerosol generation without intentionally changing the underlying support flow.

HME management is a major safety point

An HME between the aerosol source and the patient can trap medication particles. Aerosol can also wet the HME material, increasing resistance. The lesson therefore requires the HME to be removed or bypassed before inline aerosol therapy according to the circuit configuration.

HFNC delivery

The course emphasizes VMN use because it can be placed in the HFNC system without adding driving flow. The therapist must still verify correct placement in the system, current HFNC flow/FiO₂, aerosol generation, and patient response.

Mechanical ventilation

Inline delivery should minimize unnecessary circuit disconnection. Before treatment, identify the current humidification method and whether an HME is present. Place the aerosol device according to the system being used, ensure medication can travel toward the patient rather than being filtered or trapped, and monitor ventilator performance during treatment.

Do not create a new circuit problem while giving a medication

If a new high-pressure alarm, increased resistance, altered triggering, or unexpected change in delivered support appears during aerosol therapy, evaluate the circuit. The treatment is not successful if medication delivery compromises ventilation.

Safety: Do not leave an HME in the aerosol pathway simply because the patient also needs humidification. Medication delivery through the HME can obstruct the element and reduce both drug delivery and ventilation.
Putting C4 together
When the patient is on HFNC or a ventilator, assess the whole circuit. Select a device that does not unintentionally disrupt support, remove barriers such as an HME from the aerosol pathway, minimize unnecessary disconnections, and verify that both medication delivery and respiratory support remain effective.

Patient response still matters

Even technically perfect aerosol delivery does not guarantee clinical improvement. Reassess breath sounds, work of breathing, respiratory rate, oxygenation, symptoms, and other relevant measures after therapy. If the patient worsens or has adverse effects such as marked tachycardia or dizziness during the course treatment, stop and reassess rather than continuing automatically.

Separate four questions

  1. Was the right medication/device selected?
  2. Was the device assembled and operated correctly?
  3. Could the patient perform the required technique?
  4. Did the patient clinically respond after effective delivery?

If the first three are wrong, fix delivery before concluding the therapy failed. If delivery is verified and the patient still does not improve, the care plan itself may require reassessment.

RTE distinction: “No improvement” is not enough information. Determine whether no drug reached the target, an inappropriate device was chosen, the patient could not perform the technique, or the correctly delivered therapy simply did not produce the expected clinical response.
Putting C5 together
Troubleshoot from medication and device selection through patient technique and circuit configuration, then evaluate the actual physiologic response. Correct delivery problems first; reconsider the treatment plan when effective delivery has been verified but the patient is not improving.

Aerosol delivery: the prescribed dose is not the same as the deposited dose

Medication can be lost in the mouth, device, cup, mask leak, HME, tubing, or exhaled gas. Respiratory therapists improve delivery by matching particle behavior, device design, patient technique, and respiratory-support configuration to the therapeutic target.

target → particle → device → technique → circuit → deposited drug → patient response → troubleshoot → reassess

RTE Depth of Clinical Judgment: take the next step

  • Information Gathering: determine the patient's coordination, inspiratory strength, respiratory rate, work of breathing, age, airway, current respiratory support, device technique, and prior response.
  • Information Gathering: inspect aerosol output, gas flow, nebulizer position, interface seal, residual medication, HME status, and circuit configuration before concluding the treatment is ineffective.
  • Decision Making: choose an SVN rather than a DPI when severe distress makes forceful inspiratory flow unreliable.
  • Decision Making: add a spacer/VHC or choose another device when MDI coordination is poor.
  • Decision Making: select a VMN when added driving-gas flow would interfere with HFNC or ventilator operation.
  • Decision Making: remove/bypass the HME before inline aerosol therapy and restore the appropriate humidification configuration afterward.
  • Decision Making: distinguish poor clinical response caused by ineffective delivery from poor response despite verified effective delivery.
  • Reassessment: evaluate breath sounds, respiratory rate, work of breathing, oxygenation, symptoms, and adverse effects after therapy.
  • Communication/Education: explain the specific technique error or device limitation to the patient/team and document the intervention and response.
