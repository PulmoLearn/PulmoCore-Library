Bronchoscopy Assisting — Equipment, Airway Safety, Specimens & Post-Procedure Monitoring

Bronchoscopy is a provider-performed procedure, but the RT is essential to airway preparation, ventilator/oxygen support, suction readiness, specimen collection, monitoring, and rapid troubleshooting. This lesson focuses on what students must set up, watch, adjust, and document at the bedside.

Learning Objectives

  1. Select bronchoscopy-related airway, suction, oxygen, ventilator, and specimen collection supplies.
  2. Differentiate flexible and rigid bronchoscopy from an RT procedure-assist perspective.
  3. Prepare an intubated or tracheostomy patient for bronchoscopy through an artificial airway.
  4. Set up suction, oxygen delivery, monitoring, and emergency airway equipment before the procedure.

  6. Recognize and troubleshoot hypoxemia, bronchospasm, bleeding, secretion plugging, suction failure, and circuit leaks.
  7. Monitor and document patient response before, during, and after bronchoscopy.

RT Role During Bronchoscopy

Bronchoscopy allows direct visualization of the airway. It may be diagnostic, therapeutic, or both. The provider advances the bronchoscope, but the RT helps keep the airway safe and the procedure functional.

Before
Verify order and patient identity, confirm procedure location and consent per facility policy, prepare suction, oxygen, airway rescue equipment, ventilator adjustments, and specimen collection supplies.
During
Monitor SpO₂, ventilation, breath sounds, hemodynamics, airway pressure, exhaled volume, patient tolerance, bleeding, secretion burden, and equipment function.
After
Reassess airway and breath sounds, restore ventilator settings/circuit, check oxygenation, monitor for bleeding or bronchospasm, label specimens, clean/secure equipment, and document.

When Bronchoscopy Is Used

Diagnostic Uses
Visualize airways · Obtain BAL samples · Collect brushings or biopsies · Evaluate infection · Investigate bleeding · Assess airway anatomy or obstruction.
Therapeutic Uses
Remove mucus plugs · Assist foreign body removal · Suction secretions not reachable with routine suction · Support airway clearance after aspiration · Guide interventional procedures.
Flexible Bronchoscopy
Most common. Can be performed at bedside, through mouth/nose, ETT, or trach. Usually requires local anesthetic and sedation depending on setting.
Rigid Bronchoscopy
Usually done under general anesthesia. Used for large foreign body removal, significant bleeding, airway stenting, or larger interventional procedures.

Pre-Procedure Safety Workflow

  1. Perform hand hygiene and don appropriate PPE based on isolation status and expected exposure.
  2. Verify patient identity using two identifiers and confirm the correct procedure.
  3. Confirm the provider order, procedure plan, specimen needs, and whether bronchoscopy will occur through an ETT, trach, mouth, or nose.
  4. Confirm baseline vitals, SpO₂, breath sounds, airway pressure, exhaled volume, ventilator settings, sedation plan, and oxygen source.
  5. Set up and test suction before the scope enters the airway.
  6. Prepare BVM and emergency airway supplies before sedation or circuit manipulation.
  7. Coordinate a time-out according to facility policy.

Bronchoscopy Through an ETT or Trach

For ventilated patients, bronchoscopy adds resistance, occupies airway diameter, may reduce delivered VT, and can trigger hypoxemia. The RT should anticipate changes in airway pressure and exhaled volume.

Before Scope Insertion
Confirm ETT/trach size is compatible with bronchoscope diameter, remove HME if aerosol/lavage/adapter configuration requires it, place swivel adapter, preoxygenate per policy/order, verify alarms are appropriate.
During Scope
Watch SpO₂, peak pressure, exhaled VT, minute ventilation, ETCO₂ if available, patient synchrony, and visible secretions or blood. Be prepared to suction and pause the procedure if deterioration occurs.
Red Flags
Falling SpO₂, rising peak pressure, low exhaled volume, absent breath sounds, severe bronchospasm, bleeding, inability to ventilate, or persistent circuit leak around the bronchoscopy adapter.
NBRC Trap: If oxygenation or ventilation becomes unstable during bronchoscopy, the immediate action is to stabilize the patient. That may mean pausing/removing the bronchoscope, increasing oxygen support, suctioning, manually ventilating, or calling for help depending on the scenario.

Specimen Collection Workflow

BAL / SPECIMEN TRAP

Specimen collection is a high-risk handoff point because mislabeled or contaminated samples can make the procedure diagnostically useless.

BAL Setup
Prepare sterile saline syringes per provider preference, connect specimen trap inline with suction tubing, confirm trap orientation, and keep labels/requisitions ready.
During Collection
Provider instills saline and suctions return into the trap. RT assists with suction control and monitors SpO₂ and ventilation because lavage can worsen oxygenation.
After Collection
Cap trap securely, label at bedside per facility policy, place in biohazard bag, send promptly to lab, and document specimen type/location if known.
Common Error
Using the regular suction canister instead of a specimen trap can lose the sample. Labeling away from the bedside increases wrong-patient/wrong-specimen risk.

Troubleshooting During Bronchoscopy

  • Hypoxemia: Increase oxygen support per policy/order, ask provider to pause or withdraw scope, suction if needed, verify ventilation, and manually ventilate if necessary.
  • High-pressure alarm: Consider scope obstruction, bronchospasm, secretions, biting, water in tubing, kinked circuit, or mucus plug. Stabilize first; troubleshoot quickly.
  • Low exhaled volume: Check bronchoscopy adapter seal, cuff pressure/leak, circuit connections, and ventilator disconnection.
  • Poor suction: Check wall suction source, regulator setting, tubing connections, canister, specimen trap orientation, and obstruction in suction path.
  • Bleeding: Maintain airway, suction as needed, monitor oxygenation, and communicate immediately with provider. Prepare for escalation if bleeding worsens.
  • Bronchospasm: Expect wheeze, rising airway pressure, low exhaled VT, and falling SpO₂. Pause procedure, support ventilation, and administer bronchodilator if ordered/protocol allows.

Post-Procedure Reassessment & Documentation

Reassess
SpO₂, RR, HR, BP, breath sounds, airway pressure, exhaled VT, secretion/bleeding amount, cough, mental status, and comfort.
Restore/Verify
Return ventilator settings/circuit to ordered configuration, verify humidification/HME status, secure airway, clear tubing, and confirm alarms.
Document
Pre/post vitals, oxygen/ventilator changes, specimens collected, patient tolerance, complications, interventions, communication, and equipment/circuit changes.

Summary & Board-Prep Reminders

RT Priorities
Airway safety, oxygenation/ventilation support, suction readiness, specimen workflow, and monitoring.
Artificial Airway Setup
Use a bronchoscopy adapter, verify ETT/trach compatibility, monitor pressure/volume changes, and prepare to stabilize quickly.
Common Hazards
Hypoxemia, high pressure, low exhaled volume, bleeding, bronchospasm, suction failure, circuit leak, and specimen errors.
NBRC Trap
Do not keep going when oxygenation or ventilation deteriorates. Communicate, pause the procedure, stabilize, and troubleshoot.
