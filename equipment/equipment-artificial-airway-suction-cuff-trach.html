Artificial Airway Equipment: What Must Be Recognized at the Bedside

Before suctioning, cuff care, medication delivery, or airway exchange, the RT must identify the airway type and the equipment attached to it. The most common error is treating all airways the same. Equipment choices depend on whether the patient has an ETT, tracheostomy tube, cuffed tube, cuffless tube, ventilator circuit, HME, heated humidifier, or speaking valve/capping device.

Endotracheal Tube Equipment
Tube size and depth marking · 15-mm connector · pilot balloon · cuff inflation line · bite block if needed · tube holder or tape · inline suction catheter if ventilated · ETCO₂ adapter if monitored · BVM and oxygen source available.
Tracheostomy Equipment
Outer cannula · inner cannula if present · obturator · flange/neck plate · cuff and pilot balloon if cuffed · trach ties or holder · spare same-size and one-size-smaller trach tubes · suction catheters · BVM with trach adapter or mask.
Connection Equipment
Ventilator circuit · HME or heated humidifier circuit · aerosol collar · T-piece · MDI/SVN adapter · inline medication port · exhalation valve or leak port depending on device · oxygen source and flowmeter.
Red Flags
Tube depth changed · pilot balloon flat · cuff pressure cannot hold · large leak with volume loss · accidental decannulation risk · visible obstruction · inner cannula occluded · patient distress after equipment change.

Tube Securement, Depth, and Dislodgement Prevention

Every artificial airway procedure begins and ends with confirming that the airway is still in the correct place. A suction pass, cuff pressure check, circuit repositioning, linen change, patient turn, oral care session, or transport can accidentally move the airway.

Before Procedure
Confirm tube type and size · note ETT depth at teeth/lip or trach position · inspect holder/ties · confirm circuit has slack · verify oxygen/BVM and suction are available.
During Procedure
Stabilize the airway before disconnecting or manipulating equipment · avoid traction from ventilator tubing · keep one hand on the airway during high-risk maneuvers · ask for assistance when turning or repositioning.
After Procedure
Recheck tube depth/position · confirm breath sounds and chest rise if indicated · verify exhaled volumes/pressure trends · assess SpO₂/ETCO₂ · ensure securement device or trach ties are intact.
Escalate immediately: sudden loss of ETCO₂ waveform, severe leak, inability to ventilate, absent breath sounds, displaced tube marking, accidental decannulation, or persistent desaturation after airway manipulation.

Artificial Airway Suctioning Equipment and Setup

CORE SKILL: L-34 / M-24

Suctioning removes secretions from an artificial airway when the patient cannot clear them independently. The equipment choice should minimize hypoxemia, infection risk, derecruitment, and airway trauma.

EquipmentUseProcedure Notes
Closed inline suction catheterVentilated patients; high PEEP; infection-control needs; frequent suctioningAllows suction without disconnecting the circuit. Verify catheter size/depth markings and return catheter fully after use.
Open sterile suction catheterNon-ventilated trach care, one-time sterile suctioning, when closed system unavailableRequires sterile technique and circuit disconnection if ventilated. Preoxygenate and monitor closely.
YankauerOral secretion removal before/after extubation or airway proceduresNot for deep artificial airway suctioning. Use to clear mouth and pharynx.
Suction regulator and tubingProvides negative pressureAdult artificial airway suction commonly uses approximately -100 to -150 mmHg unless facility policy differs.
SalineNot routine lavageRoutine saline instillation is generally avoided unless policy/provider direction supports a specific indication.

Monitoring Before, During, and After Suction

Before
Assess need: visible/audible secretions, sawtooth waveform, increased peak pressure, coarse breath sounds, decreased SpO₂, patient cough. Preoxygenate if indicated. Verify suction pressure and catheter function.
During
Advance catheter without suction, withdraw while applying intermittent suction, limit each pass to facility policy/clinical tolerance, monitor SpO₂/HR/rhythm/appearance, stop if distress occurs.
After
Reoxygenate as needed, reassess breath sounds, SpO₂, peak pressures, exhaled VT, secretion amount/color/consistency, patient tolerance, and catheter position.
Stop / Escalate
Significant desaturation, bradycardia/tachyarrhythmia, bleeding, severe bronchospasm, inability to pass catheter, persistent obstruction, or inability to ventilate after suction.

Cuff Care: Manometer Use, Pressure Targets, and Leak Troubleshooting

CORE SKILL: L-35 / M-25

The cuff helps create a seal for ventilation and reduces gross aspiration around the airway. Cuff pressure must be high enough to maintain ventilation but low enough to reduce tracheal mucosal injury.

Equipment
Cuff manometer · syringe if needed · pilot balloon · ventilator data (exhaled VT/leak) · stethoscope · documentation tool.
Typical Target
Common adult target range: 20–30 cmH₂O. Always follow facility policy and provider order. Pressure above target may injure mucosa; pressure below target may cause leak and aspiration risk.
Red Flags
Cuff will not hold pressure · persistent audible leak · low exhaled VT alarm · pilot balloon cut/flat · high cuff pressure requirement · new voice sounds around ETT · aspiration concern.

Cuff Pressure Procedure

  1. Verify airway type, tube size, and patient stability.
  2. Connect the cuff manometer to the pilot balloon without losing cuff volume.
  3. Read current cuff pressure.
  4. Adjust to the target range per policy/order.
  5. Reassess ventilator leak, exhaled volume, patient comfort, and breath sounds if indicated.
  6. Document pressure, action taken, patient response, and notification if abnormal.

Inline Medication Administration Equipment

CORE SKILL: L-33 / M-23

Inline medication delivery should deliver the medication while preserving ventilator support, minimizing circuit breaks, and avoiding equipment combinations that block aerosol delivery.

SVN Inline Setup
Use the ventilator-approved nebulizer or adapter. Place according to manufacturer/facility guidance. Ensure adequate flow/power source. Remove or bypass HME during aerosol therapy because aerosol particles can clog the HME.
MDI Inline Setup
Use an inline MDI adapter or chamber. Coordinate actuation with inspiration if applicable. Confirm the canister is seated and adapter returns to closed/normal position after use.
Humidification Check
Heated humidifier circuits and aerosol adapters must be configured correctly. Do not place aerosol through an HME. If frequent aerosol therapy is needed, heated humidification is generally more appropriate than repeated HME removal/replacement.
Troubleshooting
No mist/poor aerosol output · adapter closed or wrong position · medication cup empty · nebulizer not powered · HME left in circuit · condensate obstructing tubing · ventilator alarms after adapter placement.

ET Tube Exchange: Equipment and RT Assist Role

NBRC GAP: BOK-III.A.5 — Exchanging Artificial Airways

Artificial airway exchange is a provider-led procedure. The RT role is to prepare the correct airway equipment, maintain oxygenation/ventilation readiness, assist with tube transition, and verify that the new airway works.

Equipment CategoryItems to PrepareWhy It Matters
Oxygenation and ventilationBVM connected to oxygen, mask, PEEP valve if indicated, ventilator ready, suction readyMaintains oxygenation during the exchange and provides immediate rescue ventilation.
New airwayCorrect ETT size and backup size, syringe, stylet if used, lubricant, tube holder/tapeAllows rapid replacement and backup if the first tube is inappropriate.
Exchange assistAirway exchange catheter if used, laryngoscope/video device, ETCO₂ detector/capnographySupports guided exchange and placement confirmation.
RescueOral/nasal airway, suction catheters, trach/emergency airway equipment per patient contextPrepares for failed exchange or lost airway.
Stop and escalate: inability to ventilate, loss of ETCO₂, severe desaturation, failed tube passage, cuff failure in the new tube, or new high-pressure/low-volume alarms after exchange.

Tracheostomy Tube Equipment and Tracheotomy Assist

NBRC GAP: BOK-III.H.5 — Tracheotomy

For this equipment course, “tracheotomy” is covered from the RT assist perspective: airway support, suction, oxygen, equipment readiness, and post-procedure stabilization. The procedure itself is provider-led.

Trach Tube Components
Outer cannula · inner cannula · obturator · flange · cuff · pilot balloon · 15-mm connector · fenestration if present · speaking valve/cap if ordered.
Tracheotomy Assist Setup
Oxygen source · BVM · suction regulator/tubing/catheters · sterile trach tray or facility kit · selected trach size and backup · cuff syringe/manometer · ETCO₂ if available · ventilator/trach collar interface.
Immediate Post-Procedure Checks
Confirm airflow and chest rise · assess SpO₂/ETCO₂ · secure tube · inflate cuff if ordered · connect ventilator or humidified oxygen · verify suction catheter passes · document tube type/size and patient response.

FAQ

Common Questions

Open vs closed suction — which should students choose?

Use closed inline suction for most mechanically ventilated patients, especially those requiring elevated PEEP, frequent suctioning, or minimized circuit disconnection. Open sterile suction may be used for non-ventilated trach patients or when a closed system is unavailable, but it requires more interruption and sterile setup.

Why is a cuff manometer better than adding air until the leak stops?

The manometer gives an objective pressure. Adding air by feel can overinflate the cuff and injure the tracheal mucosa. It can also miss a cuff/pilot-balloon failure if pressure cannot be maintained.

What is the RT role during tracheotomy?

The provider performs the procedure. The RT prepares oxygen, BVM, suction, ventilator/trach collar interface, selected trach tube and backup sizes, cuff inflation equipment, ETCO₂ if available, and emergency airway supplies. After placement, the RT helps confirm ventilation, secure the airway, and monitor response.

Summary & Board-Prep Reminders

Suctioning
Choose closed inline suction for most ventilated patients. Monitor SpO₂, HR, rhythm, ventilator pressures/volumes, secretion characteristics, and patient tolerance. Stop for instability.
Cuff Care
Use a cuff manometer, not guesswork. Typical adult target is commonly 20–30 cmH₂O per policy/order. Persistent leak or inability to hold pressure may require escalation for airway exchange.
Inline Meds
Use ventilator-approved adapters. Remove or bypass HME for aerosol therapy. Frequent aerosol therapy usually favors heated humidification rather than repeated HME interruption.
Exchange / Tracheotomy
Have oxygen/BVM, suction, backup airway sizes, cuff tools, ETCO₂, and securement ready. Fresh trach and airway exchange situations require immediate rescue equipment.
