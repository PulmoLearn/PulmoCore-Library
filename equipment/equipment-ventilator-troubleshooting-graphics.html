Ventilator Troubleshooting, Alarm Response & Graphics Recognition

Ventilator alarms are not just machine problems — they are patient safety events until proven otherwise. This lesson focuses on the bedside procedure: assess the patient, stabilize ventilation when needed, troubleshoot the circuit and airway, use graphics to identify the problem, and document the corrective action.

Learning Objectives

  1. Use a patient-first response to ventilator alarms: assess patient, oxygenation, ventilation, circuit, and airway.
  2. Differentiate high-pressure, low-pressure, low exhaled volume, and apnea alarms by likely cause and immediate RT action.
  3. Identify equipment problems including disconnects, leaks, water in circuit, blocked filters, occluded tubing, and inappropriate alarm settings.
  4. Identify artificial airway problems including secretions, biting, cuff leak, mainstem intubation concern, and ETT/trach obstruction.
  5. Use basic ventilator graphics to recognize leaks, obstruction, auto-PEEP/air trapping, flow starvation, and ineffective triggering.
  6. Recognize and correct common patient-ventilator dyssynchrony patterns.
  7. Choose when to suction, reposition, drain circuit condensate, adjust settings per order/protocol, bag the patient, or call for help.
  8. Document alarm event, assessment findings, interventions, patient response, and provider notification.
Course boundary: This is not a full ventilator theory lesson. The focus is bedside troubleshooting: which equipment to check, what the waveform suggests, what to do next, and when to escalate.

Alarm Response Priority: Patient First, Machine Second

ALARM RESPONSE FRAMEWORK

Every ventilator alarm is treated as a patient safety event until the RT confirms the patient is stable. Do not silence and walk away. The first decision is whether the patient needs immediate manual ventilation.

1. Assess Patient
Look at the patient, not the ventilator screen first. Check color, chest rise, work of breathing, mental status, SpO₂, HR, and whether the patient is connected and receiving breaths.
2. Stabilize
If the patient is unstable, remove from ventilator and ventilate with BVM connected to oxygen while help is called and the circuit/airway is assessed.
3. Troubleshoot
Once oxygenation/ventilation is protected, check circuit, airway, humidifier, filters, cuff, patient condition, settings, and waveforms.

Activity: Alarm Response Priority Sort

Classify the RT response as appropriate or unsafe.

Equipment to Have Ready Before Troubleshooting

A ventilated patient can deteriorate quickly. Before disconnecting or changing anything, the RT should know where the rescue and troubleshooting supplies are.

Rescue Equipment
BVM connected to oxygen · suction setup · Yankauer · inline/open suction catheter · spare circuit components · extra HME/filter · oxygen analyzer if needed · stethoscope · pulse oximeter/monitor.
Airway Equipment
10 mL syringe · cuff manometer · bite block · securement supplies · replacement trach/ETT supplies per policy · difficult airway/reintubation equipment available when unstable.
Circuit Checks
Inspiratory/expiratory limbs · wye · humidifier chamber · heated wire plug · water traps · expiratory filter · nebulizer adapter position · ETCO₂ adapter · exhalation valve · pressure line.
Do Not Forget
Alarm settings can be wrong, but do not assume nuisance alarm first. Verify patient stability and actual delivered/exhaled values before changing limits.

High-Pressure Alarm Troubleshooting

HIGH PRESSURE = BLOCKED FLOW OR HARD-TO-INFLATE LUNGS
Airway Causes
Secretions · mucus plug · patient biting ETT · kinked tube · ETT mainstem concern · cuff herniation/ETT obstruction · bronchospasm.
Circuit Causes
Water in circuit · kinked tubing · blocked HME/filter · closed clamp/valve · blocked expiratory limb · nebulizer/filter obstruction.
Patient Causes
Coughing · agitation · pain · decreased lung compliance · pneumothorax · pulmonary edema · abdominal distention.
Procedure flow: Assess patient → check chest rise and breath sounds → check tubing for kinks/water → suction if secretions suspected → remove/replace obstructed HME/filter → evaluate bronchospasm/compliance → manually ventilate/call help if unstable.

Low-Pressure Alarm Troubleshooting

LOW PRESSURE = PRESSURE CANNOT BUILD
Disconnection
Circuit disconnected at wye · loose humidifier chamber · disconnected inspiratory/expiratory limb · disconnected inline suction or nebulizer adapter · exhalation valve issue.
Leak
Cuff leak · cuff deflation · pilot balloon damage · circuit leak · loose HME/filter · chest tube/bronchopleural fistula in some patients.
Settings/Sensing
Alarm limit set too low/high for condition · pressure support too low in spontaneous mode · patient not triggering · trigger sensitivity problem.
Procedure flow: Assess patient → verify connection at patient wye → follow circuit back to ventilator → check cuff pressure/leak if connected → verify exhaled VT/minute ventilation → fix leak/disconnect → manually ventilate if unstable.

Apnea and Low Exhaled Volume Alarms

Apnea Alarm
Patient may not be initiating breaths in spontaneous/CPAP/PSV mode, or the ventilator may not be sensing effort. Assess patient immediately. Causes include oversedation, fatigue, neurologic change, disconnection, or trigger/sensitivity issue.
Low Exhaled VT / VE
Common causes: circuit disconnect, cuff leak, bronchopleural fistula/chest tube leak, HME/adapter leak, patient fatigue, inadequate pressure support, obstruction with incomplete exhalation, or ventilator measurement issue.
  • Board trap: Do not treat every low exhaled volume alarm by increasing pressure support. First determine whether the volume is missing because of a leak/disconnect or because the patient is not generating adequate ventilation.
  • Escalation: If apnea occurs during CPAP/PSV and the patient is not breathing adequately, return to a supported mode or manually ventilate while the cause is addressed.

Ventilator Graphics Basics

GRAPHICS = TROUBLESHOOTING TOOL

For this equipment lesson, graphics are used to answer a practical question: what is the problem, and what do I do next?

Pressure-Time

Shows pressure delivery, trigger dips, plateau/peaks, and pressure spikes.

Flow-Time

Shows inspiratory/expiratory flow, air trapping, ineffective effort, and leaks.

Loops

PV/FV loops show resistance, compliance, leaks, and overdistention patterns.

Patient-Ventilator Dyssynchrony: Recognition and Correction

Dyssynchrony means the ventilator breath pattern and the patient's effort are not matching. Students should recognize the bedside signs and connect them to equipment/settings checks.

Flow Starvation
Patient looks air hungry; pressure curve may show scooping/concavity. Check inspiratory flow, rise time, pressure support, mode, anxiety/pain. Notify provider if setting change needed.
Auto-PEEP / Air Trapping
Expiratory flow does not return to baseline before next breath. Patient may fail to trigger. Check obstructive disease, RR, VT, expiratory time, flow, secretions, bronchospasm.
Ineffective Triggering
Patient effort visible but ventilator does not deliver breath. Check trigger sensitivity, auto-PEEP, weakness, sedation, circuit leaks, mode/support level.
Double Triggering
Two breaths delivered close together. Patient demand exceeds set inspiratory time or VT; assess distress, VT, inspiratory time, sedation/pain, mode appropriateness.

FAQ

Common Questions

Should the RT silence the alarm first?

Silencing may be necessary briefly, but only after the patient is being assessed and ventilation/oxygenation are protected. Never silence an alarm and leave without identifying the cause.

When should I disconnect and bag the patient?

Disconnect and manually ventilate when the patient is unstable, not receiving breaths, cyanotic, severely desaturating, or when you cannot rapidly identify/correct the ventilator/circuit problem while the patient is deteriorating.

What is the simplest way to remember high vs low pressure?

High pressure usually means gas cannot move forward easily: obstruction, secretions, kink, biting, bronchospasm, poor compliance. Low pressure usually means pressure cannot build: disconnect, leak, cuff problem, loose adapter.

Why are graphics included in an equipment/procedure lesson?

Graphics are a troubleshooting tool. In this course, students do not need a complete graphics theory course; they need enough waveform recognition to choose bedside actions: check leak, suction, drain circuit, treat obstruction, adjust settings per order/protocol, or call for help.

Summary & Board-Prep Reminders

Patient First
Ventilator alarms are patient safety events. Assess patient, stabilize, then troubleshoot.
High Pressure
Think obstruction/resistance/compliance: secretions, kink, biting, bronchospasm, water, filter/HME, pneumothorax or poor compliance.
Low Pressure
Think leak/disconnect: wye, circuit, cuff, adapter, humidifier chamber, inline suction, nebulizer setup.
Graphics
Flow not back to baseline = auto-PEEP. Volume not back to baseline = leak. Pressure spike = resistance. Scooped pressure = flow starvation. Effort without breath = ineffective trigger.
  • NBRC trap: Do not change alarm limits as the first answer when patient/circuit/airway cause is likely.
  • NBRC trap: Low exhaled volume does not always mean increase pressure support; first rule out leak or disconnect.
  • NBRC trap: If the patient is unstable, manual ventilation with BVM/O₂ is safer than continuing to troubleshoot on the ventilator.
