Introduction to Medical Gas Therapy

Medical gas administration is one of the RT's primary clinical responsibilities. Every gas therapy order involves selecting the correct device, setting it up correctly, monitoring the patient's response, adjusting as needed, and knowing when to escalate to the next level of support.

Goals of Oxygen Therapy
Treat or prevent hypoxemia. Reduce the work of breathing. Prevent myocardial stress from hypoxia. Maintain PaO₂ ≥60 mmHg (SpO₂ ≥90%) unless clinical goals differ. Reverse or prevent the consequences of tissue hypoxia.
Indications
Documented hypoxemia (PaO₂ <60 mmHg, SpO₂ <90%) · Suspected hypoxemia · Severe trauma or shock · Acute MI (reduce cardiac workload) · CO poisoning · Short-term peri-procedural or emergency oxygen needs
Precautions & Complications
Oxygen toxicity (FiO₂ >0.50 for extended periods) · Absorption atelectasis · Suppression of ventilatory drive (chronic hypercapnic patients) · Fire risk · Bacterial contamination of delivery equipment · Oxygen alone does not fix anemia, poor perfusion, or CO₂ retention
Key Limitation: Oxygen therapy treats hypoxemia — it does not correct hypoventilation. A patient with ventilatory failure (rising PaCO₂, falling pH) needs ventilatory support — NIV or mechanical ventilation — not just more oxygen. This distinction drives escalation decisions on the NBRC.

Low-Flow vs. High-Flow Devices

CRITICAL DISTINCTION — MOST MISUNDERSTOOD NBRC CONCEPT

The terms "low-flow" and "high-flow" refer to whether the device meets the patient's peak inspiratory flow demand — not to the number of liters per minute set on the flowmeter. This distinction determines whether the delivered FiO₂ is fixed or variable.

Low-Flow Devices
Do NOT meet the patient's full peak inspiratory flow demand. Room air is entrained alongside the delivered oxygen, diluting the FiO₂. The amount of dilution changes with the patient's breathing pattern — faster breathing, deeper breaths, or higher peak flow all entrain more room air and lower FiO₂.

FiO₂ is variable and unpredictable.
Examples: Nasal cannula, simple mask, partial rebreather, nonrebreather.
High-Flow Devices
Meet or exceed the patient's peak inspiratory flow demand. No room air is entrained — the patient breathes only the delivered gas mixture. FiO₂ is stable and consistent regardless of breathing pattern.

FiO₂ is fixed and reliable.
Examples: Venturi mask (air entrainment device), HFNC, oxygen hoods with adequate flow, blended gas systems.
The NBRC Trap: A nasal cannula set at 6 L/min is still a low-flow device — it does not meet peak inspiratory flow. A Venturi mask set at 4 L/min O₂ input that produces 40 L/min total flow IS a high-flow device. Flow in L/min on the flowmeter is not what classifies the device.

Nasal Cannula

MOST COMMON OXYGEN DELIVERY DEVICE
Indications & Use
Mild hypoxemia · Stable spontaneously breathing patients · Long-term oxygen therapy · Low oxygen requirement. Adult range: 1–6 L/min. Neonates: 0.25–2 L/min. Humidification recommended at flows >4 L/min to prevent nasal dryness.
FiO₂ Formula
FiO₂ ≈ 20% + (4% × L/min)

1 L/min → 24% · 2 L/min → 28% · 3 L/min → 32% · 4 L/min → 36% · 5 L/min → 40% · 6 L/min → 44%

This is an approximation — actual FiO₂ varies with breathing pattern.
Setup & Troubleshooting
Connect tubing to O₂ source → set flow → check flow at prongs → place in nares → loop over ears and secure. Skin irritation: pad tubing. No flow: check flowmeter and connections. Low SpO₂: increase flow or escalate device. Maximum adult flow: 6 L/min standard.

Oxygen-Conserving Devices

Conserving devices reduce the total oxygen consumption needed to maintain target SpO₂ — important for home oxygen patients on portable cylinders.

Reservoir Nasal Cannula
A small reservoir (pendant or moustache style) fills with oxygen-rich gas during early exhalation. This reservoir gas is delivered at the start of the next breath — when it is most effective. Allows the same SpO₂ at roughly half the flow rate of a standard cannula. Useful for home oxygen patients on portable cylinders.
Pulse-Dose (Demand) System
An electronic sensor detects the beginning of inspiration and delivers a brief pulse of oxygen. No oxygen is wasted during exhalation. Reduces oxygen consumption by 50–75% vs. continuous flow. Used with portable systems and travel tanks. Battery-dependent.
Transtracheal Oxygen
A small catheter is surgically placed into the trachea, delivering oxygen directly into the airway. Lower flows needed — oxygen is not wasted in the upper airway dead space. Requires daily catheter care. Risks: infection, obstruction (mucus ball on catheter tip), disconnection. Used for stable LTOT patients with high flow requirements.

Simple Mask

EQUIPMENT: Simple Oxygen Mask
Indications & Range
Short-term moderate oxygen need · Mouth breathing patients · When nasal cannula is inadequate or uncomfortable. Flow range: 5–8 L/min. Approximate FiO₂: 40–60%. Variable depending on breathing pattern.
Minimum Flow: 5 L/min
The mask creates dead space. At flows below 5 L/min, exhaled CO₂ accumulates in the mask volume and is rebreathed with the next inspiration. This causes rising PaCO₂ and respiratory acidosis. Never run a simple mask below 5 L/min.
Setup & Troubleshooting
Set minimum 5 L/min → place over nose and mouth → adjust flexible nose clip → secure strap. Claustrophobia: coach patient; consider alternate device. Mask too loose: refit. Poor SpO₂: increase flow or escalate to PRB, Venturi, or HFNC.

Partial Rebreather Mask (PRB)

EQUIPMENT: Mask With Reservoir Bag, No One-Way Valves
Key Feature
A reservoir bag attached below the mask collects the patient's initial exhaled gas (anatomical dead space gas — relatively oxygen-rich, minimal CO₂) plus continued O₂ flow. This gas is partially rebreathed on the next inspiration. No one-way valves — distinguishes PRB from NRB.
Settings & FiO₂
Flow range: 10–15 L/min. Approximate FiO₂: 40–70%.

Critical monitoring point: The reservoir bag must remain at least one-third full during inspiration. If it collapses completely, the patient is not receiving adequate flow — increase immediately.
Setup
Flush the reservoir bag with oxygen before placing on the patient's face. Attach tubing to oxygen source. Set 10–15 L/min. Place mask over face. Monitor bag for adequate inflation during inspiration.
Troubleshooting
Bag collapses: increase flow immediately.
Bag not inflating before placement: flush with O₂ first.
Patient worsening despite PRB: escalate to NRB, HFNC, NIV, or mechanical ventilation based on clinical status and WOB.

Nonrebreather Mask (NRB)

HIGH-YIELD — INDICATIONS GO BEYOND LOW SpO₂
Key Design Feature
One-way valve between the mask body and reservoir bag prevents exhaled gas from entering the reservoir — all exhaled gas exits through exhalation port valves. One-way valves at exhalation ports prevent room air from entering during inspiration. This is what separates NRB from PRB.
Settings & FiO₂
Flow: 10–15+ L/min. Approximate FiO₂: 60–80%+. Actual FiO₂ depends on mask seal, flow, and valve condition. A tightly fitting, properly flushed NRB at 15 L/min can approach near-100% FiO₂.

Reservoir bag must stay 2/3 full during inspiration. Flush bag before placement.
⭐ NRB Even When SpO₂ is 100% — Critical NBRC Concept
The NRB is indicated in situations where the goal is NOT simply correcting a low pulse oximetry reading. Indications for NRB even with normal/high SpO₂ include:
  • Carbon monoxide poisoning — SpO₂ is falsely normal (COHb reads as O₂Hb). 100% O₂ accelerates CO dissociation from hemoglobin.
  • Smoke inhalation — suspected CO exposure; maximize O₂ delivery regardless of pulse ox reading.
  • Severe trauma or shock — maximize oxygen delivery to hypoperfused tissues even if SpO₂ appears normal.
  • Preoxygenation before intubation — maximize O₂ reservoir before apnea.
  • Peri-arrest or rapidly deteriorating patient — prepare for worst while definitive care is arranged.
  • Pneumothorax awaiting decompression — high FiO₂ promotes nitrogen reabsorption from the pleural space.

Switching Between Devices — Maintaining FiO₂

When switching a patient from one oxygen device to another, the RT must ensure the new device delivers approximately the same — or higher — FiO₂. Accidentally downgrading FiO₂ during a device change is an NBRC trap and a real patient safety risk.

Current DeviceApprox FiO₂Appropriate Switch Options
NC 3 L/min~32%Venturi 31–35%; NC at same flow with proper mask if mouth breathing
NC 5 L/min~40%Simple mask 5–6 L/min; Venturi 40%; HFNC set to ~40% FiO₂
Simple mask 6–8 L/min~40–60%Venturi 40–50%; HFNC at equivalent or higher FiO₂
PRB 10–15 L/min~40–70%NRB (↑ FiO₂) or HFNC at equivalent/higher FiO₂
NRB 15 L/min~60–80%+HFNC at high FiO₂; NIV if WOB elevated; intubation if failing
The Most Common Switching Error: Switching a patient from a simple mask at 8 L/min (~60% FiO₂) to a nasal cannula at 3 L/min (~32% FiO₂) to "increase comfort." This reduces FiO₂ by nearly half. The new device must be matched to the patient's current oxygen requirement, not just their comfort preference.

Titration Principles

  • Titrate to ordered SpO₂ goal — do not over-oxygenate chronically hypercapnic patients (COPD target often 88–92%).
  • Increase support for worsening WOB even if SpO₂ appears acceptable — rising work of breathing predicts impending respiratory failure.
  • Reassess after every change — give each adjustment time to equilibrate (typically 5–10 minutes) before re-evaluating.
  • Do not delay escalation for patients showing fatigue, altered mental status, or rising PaCO₂ — these are pre-failure signs.

Venturi Mask — Air Entrainment Device

HIGH-YIELD — PRECISE FiO₂ DELIVERY

The Venturi (air entrainment) mask delivers a precise, fixed FiO₂ by mixing oxygen with a defined amount of entrained room air. Because total flow exceeds the patient's peak inspiratory demand, the FiO₂ is stable regardless of the patient's breathing pattern — making it the preferred device for COPD and other patients where precise FiO₂ control is critical.

How It Works
Oxygen passes through a narrow jet (orifice) at high velocity, creating a Venturi (Bernoulli) effect that draws room air in through surrounding entrainment ports. The ratio of oxygen to entrained air is fixed by the jet and port sizes — controlling the final FiO₂ precisely.
Venturi Physics
Larger jet = higher FiO₂ (less room air entrained). Larger entrainment ports = lower FiO₂ (more room air entrained). Changing O₂ input flow changes total output volume but does NOT significantly change delivered FiO₂ — the ratio is set by the device geometry.
Troubleshooting
Covered entrainment ports: FiO₂ rises unpredictably. Kinked tubing: total flow decreases. High downstream resistance: accuracy affected. Patient demand exceeds total flow: extra room air is entrained, effectively lowering FiO₂. Always verify port patency.
COPD Clinical Application: For COPD patients who need precise, controlled FiO₂ to avoid suppressing hypoxic drive, the Venturi mask is the preferred device. Unlike the nasal cannula (where FiO₂ varies with breathing), the Venturi delivers a consistent concentration even during rapid or deep breathing episodes.

High-Flow Nasal Cannula (HFNC)

EQUIPMENT: Optiflow · Vapotherm · Heated Humidified High-Flow Systems

HFNC delivers heated, humidified oxygen-air blends through specialized large-bore nasal cannulas at flow rates (10–60 L/min) that meet or exceed peak inspiratory flow. This makes FiO₂ stable and consistent while providing additional physiologic benefits not achievable with standard low-flow devices.

Physiologic Benefits
Stable FiO₂ (high-flow device) · Washes out nasopharyngeal CO₂ dead space · Heated and humidified gas improves mucociliary clearance and reduces airway drying · Small positive airway pressure effect (1–4 cmH₂O) may reduce WOB · Significantly more comfortable than NIV masks for most patients
What Must Be Set
FiO₂: 21–100% (via integrated blender or external air/O₂ source). Flow rate: 10–60 L/min for adults; lower for pediatric/neonates. Both parameters are independent — adjusting flow does not change FiO₂ and vice versa. Start with flow sufficient to meet inspiratory demand (~30–40 L/min for most adults).

HFNC Setup Sequence

Put the six HFNC setup steps in the correct order.

Connect oxygen and air supply lines to the blender (or confirm integrated blender has dual gas supply)
Fill the humidifier chamber with sterile water to the indicated fill line
Connect the heated circuit from humidifier to the patient interface port; ensure all connections are secure
Select the correct size nasal cannula for the patient (should fit nares without occluding them completely)
Set the prescribed FiO₂ and flow rate on the blender/device
Allow the circuit to warm to the set temperature; then apply cannula to the patient and assess tolerance and SpO₂
Order all six steps to continue.
HFNC Failure — When to Escalate: If the patient's WOB remains high, SpO₂ does not improve, or ABG shows worsening CO₂ retention and acidosis despite optimal HFNC settings, escalate to CPAP/BiPAP (NIV) if appropriate, or prepare for intubation and mechanical ventilation. HFNC is not a substitute for ventilatory support when the primary problem is hypercapnic failure.

Pediatric Oxygen Devices & Oxygen Blenders

Oxygen Hood (Oxyhood)
A clear plastic enclosure placed over an infant's head. FiO₂ must be measured at the level of the infant's nose — not at the inlet — to confirm delivered concentration. Noise levels inside the hood can be significant. Requires adequate flow to prevent CO₂ accumulation inside the enclosure.
Isolette / Incubator
Provides temperature, humidity, and oxygen control for premature or critically ill neonates. FiO₂ can vary significantly when portholes or doors are opened during nursing care. Monitor FiO₂ continuously; allow stabilization after any disruption before recording values.
Oxygen Blender
Uses 50 psi oxygen and medical air to blend precise FiO₂. Alarms activate when either supply pressure drops significantly (typically a 10 psi change triggers the alarm). Used for HFNC systems, ventilators, isolettes, and any device requiring precise FiO₂ from dual gas sources.

Hyperbaric Oxygen & Inhaled Nitric Oxide

Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy (HBO)
Delivery of 100% O₂ at pressures greater than atmospheric (typically 2–3 ATA). Dramatically increases dissolved plasma oxygen. Indicated for: decompression sickness (diving), carbon monoxide poisoning, smoke inhalation, necrotizing infections, diabetic foot wounds, and other conditions per facility protocol.

Primary benefit in CO poisoning: accelerates CO-Hb dissociation beyond what normobaric 100% O₂ achieves.
Inhaled Nitric Oxide (iNO)
A potent, selective pulmonary vasodilator. Delivered in parts per million (ppm) concentration via the ventilator circuit. Causes vasodilation only in well-ventilated lung regions — improving V/Q matching and oxygenation.

Indications: persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN), neonatal hypoxic respiratory failure, acute RV failure. Requires careful delivery systems and monitoring for NO₂ (toxic byproduct) and methemoglobin levels.

Heliox Therapy & Corrected Flow Calculations

HIGH-YIELD — HELIOX CORRECTION FACTORS

Heliox is a helium-oxygen mixture (typically 80/20, 70/30, or 60/40 He/O₂). Because helium is much less dense than nitrogen (which makes up most of air), heliox reduces turbulent flow resistance in obstructed airways — decreasing the work of breathing without increasing oxygen delivery.

Clinical Indications
Upper airway obstruction (croup, laryngeal edema, post-extubation stridor) · Severe asthma exacerbation · Bronchiolitis · Any condition where turbulent airflow through a narrowed airway increases WOB

Heliox improves work of breathing — it does not primarily improve oxygenation.
Why Flowmeter Correction Is Needed
Flowmeters are calibrated for oxygen, which has higher density than helium. When heliox flows through an O₂-calibrated flowmeter, the actual flow delivered is HIGHER than the meter indicates. The low-density helium moves faster through the same orifice.

Failing to correct for this leads to under-delivery or over-delivery of heliox.
Correction Factors
80/20 Heliox: Actual flow = Indicated flow × 1.8

70/30 Heliox: Actual flow = Indicated flow × 1.6

Higher helium fraction = more correction needed (helium is less dense than oxygen, creates more discrepancy).

Oxygen Escalation Algorithm & RT Clinical Pearls

Escalation Pathway

1
Mild Hypoxemia (PaO₂ 60–79, SpO₂ 90–95%)
Nasal cannula 1–6 L/min · Reservoir cannula · Venturi mask for precise FiO₂ control
2
Moderate Hypoxemia (PaO₂ 40–59, SpO₂ 75–90%)
Simple mask 5–8 L/min · Venturi 40–50% · HFNC — preferred when WOB is elevated or NC/simple mask inadequate
3
Severe Hypoxemia (PaO₂ <40, SpO₂ <75%)
NRB mask at 15 L/min · HFNC at high FiO₂/flow · NIV (CPAP/BiPAP) if WOB is severe or CO₂ is rising
4
Refractory Hypoxemia or Ventilatory Failure
Intubation and mechanical ventilation — when NIV fails, is contraindicated, or patient is unable to protect airway

RT Clinical Pearls

  • Low-flow does not mean low L/min — it means the device does not meet inspiratory demand. A 6 L/min nasal cannula is still low-flow.
  • Never run a simple mask below 5 L/min — CO₂ accumulates in dead space and is rebreathed.
  • Always flush reservoir bags before placement — both PRB and NRB bags must be filled with oxygen before placing on the patient's face.
  • NRB when SpO₂ is normal can be the right call — CO poisoning, smoke inhalation, and preoxygenation are key exceptions.
  • Heliox improves work of breathing — not oxygenation — if FiO₂ must be high, the helium fraction is insufficient for benefit.
  • Increasing Venturi O₂ input flow does not change FiO₂ — it changes total output volume only. Use a different Venturi adapter to change FiO₂.
  • Cover Venturi entrainment ports accidentally = unpredictable FiO₂ increase — always verify port patency when placing a Venturi mask.

Common NBRC Traps — Oxygen Delivery

  • Calling nasal cannula "high-flow" because the L/min setting is high. A nasal cannula at 6 L/min is still a low-flow device. High-flow refers to whether the device meets peak inspiratory demand — not the flowmeter reading.
  • Switching devices and accidentally dropping FiO₂. Example: switching from a simple mask at 8 L/min (~60% FiO₂) to nasal cannula at 3 L/min (~32% FiO₂). Always match the new device's FiO₂ capability to the patient's oxygen requirement.
  • Running a simple mask at less than 5 L/min. Below 5 L/min, exhaled CO₂ accumulates in the mask dead space and is rebreathed. This is one of the most commonly tested safety facts in oxygen delivery.
  • Not increasing flow when the reservoir bag collapses (PRB or NRB). A collapsing bag means the patient's inspiratory demand exceeds flow. The bag must remain at least one-third full at all times. Increase flow immediately.
  • Assuming NRB is only indicated when SpO₂ is low. NRB is indicated for CO poisoning, smoke inhalation, preoxygenation, and trauma regardless of SpO₂ reading.
  • Thinking heliox increases FiO₂ or treats hypoxemia. Heliox reduces turbulent airflow resistance and work of breathing. It does not correct hypoxemia. In fact, using 80/20 heliox limits FiO₂ to 20%.
  • Changing Venturi O₂ input flow and expecting FiO₂ to change. Increasing or decreasing O₂ input to a Venturi device changes total output volume, not the delivered FiO₂. FiO₂ is set by the adapter's fixed jet and port geometry.
  • Forgetting the heliox flowmeter correction. An oxygen-calibrated flowmeter reading 10 L/min of 80/20 heliox is actually delivering 18 L/min. Failure to apply the correction factor (×1.8 for 80/20, ×1.6 for 70/30) leads to delivery errors.

FAQ

Common Questions

Why is a nasal cannula at 6 L/min still called "low-flow"?

The low-flow/high-flow classification is about whether the device meets the patient's peak inspiratory flow demand — not about the flowmeter setting. A typical adult's peak inspiratory flow during normal breathing is approximately 30 L/min, and during labored breathing it can exceed 60 L/min. A nasal cannula at 6 L/min delivers only 6 L/min of oxygen-enriched gas — the remaining 24+ L/min that the patient inhales is room air drawn in around the prongs. This room air dilutes the oxygen, making the delivered FiO₂ variable and lower than it would be with a high-flow device.

Why is there a 5 L/min minimum for the simple mask?

The simple mask creates a volume of dead space (the mask interior) between the patient's face and the exhalation ports. Exhaled gas, which contains ~4% CO₂, fills this space at the end of each exhalation. On the next inhalation, this CO₂-containing gas is re-inhaled before fresh gas enters. At flows below 5 L/min, the incoming oxygen flow is not sufficient to flush this CO₂ out of the mask between breaths, so the patient progressively rebreathes CO₂ with each breath cycle, causing rising PaCO₂ and respiratory acidosis — even if the SpO₂ appears normal initially.

If I change the O₂ input flow on a Venturi mask, does the FiO₂ change?

No — not significantly. The FiO₂ delivered by a Venturi device is determined by the geometry of the jet orifice and entrainment ports, which create a fixed air:O₂ ratio. Increasing or decreasing the O₂ input flow changes the total volume of gas delivered (higher flow = more total output) but does not meaningfully change the ratio of air to oxygen — and therefore does not change FiO₂. To change the FiO₂, you must change the Venturi adapter (which has a different jet and port configuration) or switch to a different FiO₂ setting on an integrated device.

Why does heliox require a flow correction factor?

Standard clinical flowmeters are calibrated for oxygen, which has a specific density (approximately 1.43 g/L at standard conditions). Heliox mixtures contain helium, which is much less dense than oxygen (helium density is approximately 0.18 g/L). When less-dense heliox passes through a flowmeter orifice calibrated for denser oxygen, it flows through faster than the meter indicates — the actual flow is higher than the displayed reading. For 80/20 heliox, the actual flow is approximately 1.8 times the indicated reading. For 70/30 heliox (less helium, slightly denser), the factor is approximately 1.6. Failure to apply these corrections results in actual delivery being significantly higher or lower than intended.

Can HFNC be used instead of NIV for COPD exacerbation?

HFNC can be used as a bridge for mild-to-moderate COPD exacerbations and provides important benefits (stable FiO₂, dead space washout, reduced WOB, improved comfort). However, HFNC does not provide positive pressure ventilation — it cannot directly support exhalation or force CO₂ elimination. For patients with significant hypercapnia (PaCO₂ ≥50–55 mmHg) and respiratory acidosis (pH <7.35), NIV (BiPAP) is the evidence-based first-line treatment and is superior to HFNC for reducing CO₂ and preventing intubation in COPD exacerbation. HFNC may be appropriate as an alternative when NIV is not tolerated or contraindicated.
