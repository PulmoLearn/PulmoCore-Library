Cardiac Conduction System — Equipment Context

BACKGROUND FOR WAVEFORM UNDERSTANDING

The ECG machine detects and records the electrical impulses generated as the cardiac conduction system fires. Understanding the pathway explains what each waveform represents.

SA Node
Primary pacemaker. Located at the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium. Fires at 60–100 bpm. Each SA node impulse triggers the P wave on the ECG.
AV Node
Delays the electrical impulse by ~0.12–0.20 sec — allows the atria to fully contract and fill the ventricles before ventricular depolarization begins. Seen on ECG as the PR interval.
Purkinje Fibers
Distribute the impulse rapidly throughout the ventricular myocardium, causing simultaneous ventricular depolarization and contraction. Generates the QRS complex on ECG.

ECG Waveforms — What the Machine Is Recording

HIGH-YIELD — WAVEFORM IDENTIFICATION
P

P Wave

Atrial depolarization — SA node fires, impulse spreads through both atria. First small deflection on the tracing.

PR

PR Interval

Time from atrial depolarization to ventricular depolarization — represents AV node conduction delay. Normal: 0.12–0.20 sec (3–5 small boxes).

QRS

QRS Complex

Ventricular depolarization — the largest deflection. Normal: <0.12 sec (3 small boxes). Wide QRS = ventricular conduction delay.

ST

ST Segment

Ventricular muscle fully depolarized. Should be isoelectric (flat). Elevation or depression indicates myocardial injury or ischemia.

T

T Wave

Ventricular repolarization — ventricles reset. Tall peaked T waves may indicate hyperkalemia. Inverted T waves may indicate ischemia.

ECG Paper: Small box = 0.04 seconds (1 mm). Large box = 0.20 seconds (5 mm). Speed: 25 mm/sec standard. Amplitude: 10 mm = 1 mV standard calibration. Always verify calibration mark before interpreting.

ECG Machine Components & Electrode Types

EQUIPMENT: 12-LEAD ECG SYSTEM
ECG Machine Components
• Monitor/display screen — real-time rhythm view
• Acquisition module / recording unit
• Lead wires (10 total for standard 12-lead)
• Electrodes (adhesive tabs, suction cups, or floating)
• Printer — generates the ECG tracing on paper
• Interpretation software (many devices offer auto-interpretation — always have physician confirm)
Electrode Types
Tab electrodes (adhesive): Standard for routine 12-lead and short-term monitoring. Pre-gelled adhesive backing. Single use. Most common in clinical practice.

Floating electrodes: Gel-filled sponge design. Less motion artifact. Used for long-term continuous monitoring (telemetry, Holter).

Plate/suction cup: Older design; now rarely used clinically.
Limb Lead Wires
4 limb leads: RA (right arm), LA (left arm), RL (right leg — ground), LL (left leg). Color coded per AHA standard: RA = white, LA = black, RL = green, LL = red. Must be placed on the correct limb — errors produce mirrored or absent waveforms.
Chest Lead Wires
6 precordial leads: V1 through V6. Often labeled V1–V6 on the lead wires. Must be placed at precise anatomical locations — errors produce incorrect ST changes or false ischemia findings. Chest lead errors are a common cause of unnecessary cardiac workup.
Cables & Connectors
Lead wires connect to a patient cable, which connects to the ECG acquisition module. Verify all connections before recording. Loose connections = artifact. Some systems use a single 10-wire cable; others use separate limb and chest cable sets.

Skin Preparation — The Foundation of a Quality ECG

VERY HIGH-YIELD — MOST COMMON SOURCE OF ECG ARTIFACT

Poor skin preparation is the most common source of ECG artifact, wandering baseline, and failed recordings. Proper skin prep ensures optimal electrode adhesion and electrical contact.

  1. Position patient supine — flat or with minimal head elevation (30° max). Arms at sides. Legs flat and uncrossed. Patient relaxed and warm (shivering = muscle artifact).
  2. Expose electrode sites — expose the chest, wrists/forearms, and ankles/lower legs. Maintain patient privacy with a drape.
  3. Inspect electrode sites — identify any rashes, wounds, surgical dressings, or implanted devices (pacemakers, defibrillator patches) that require electrode repositioning.
  4. Remove hair if present — clip (do not shave) excess chest hair over electrode sites. Hair prevents adhesion and creates air gaps that cause artifact. A dry razor can be used on chests with very dense hair.
  5. Clean with alcohol wipe — wipe firmly (scrubbing, not dabbing) to remove skin oils, lotion, and debris. The mechanical action also lightly abrades the skin, removing dead cells and improving conductance.
  6. Dry completely — allow alcohol to fully evaporate before applying electrodes. Wet skin prevents adhesion. Air dry or blot dry — do NOT rub with the alcohol pad after cleaning.
  7. Apply electrodes firmly — press firmly from center outward, ensuring the gel is fully in contact with skin and no air pockets are trapped under the electrode edges.
  8. Connect lead wires — attach lead wires without tension; ensure lead wires are not crossing over each other (crossing leads = electromagnetic interference artifact).
Why This Matters: A poorly adherent electrode creates high electrical resistance at the skin-electrode interface — the ECG machine then amplifies the noise created by that resistance along with the cardiac signal. The result is a degraded tracing that can mimic dysrhythmias, produce false ST changes, or completely obscure the rhythm. A 60-second investment in skin prep prevents repeated recordings.

ECG Recording Procedure — Step by Step

VERY HIGH-YIELD PROCEDURE
  1. Verify the order and check the patient's medical record — confirm identity using two identifiers.
  2. Gather equipment — ECG machine (charged/plugged), 10 electrodes, alcohol wipes, razor if needed, paper, pen.
  3. Explain the procedure to the patient — ask them to lie still and breathe normally. Instruct them not to talk during the recording.
  4. Prepare the skin — follow full skin preparation protocol (see previous section): clip hair if needed → alcohol wipe (scrub) → air dry → apply electrodes.
  5. Position the patient — supine, arms at sides, legs uncrossed, no metallic jewelry near electrode sites. Patient should be relaxed and not shivering.
  6. Apply all 10 electrodes — 4 limb leads + 6 chest leads per correct anatomical locations.
  7. Connect lead wires — match each wire to the correct electrode. Verify all connections are snapped in and secure.
  8. Enter patient data — enter name, medical record number, date, and time into the ECG machine.
  9. Record the ECG — initiate recording. Standard: 10-second recording at 25 mm/sec with 10 mm/mV calibration. Most machines record automatically once connections are confirmed.
  10. Review tracing immediately — check for artifact, baseline wander, lead reversal, or poor contact before removing electrodes. If artifact present: re-prepare skin and re-record.
  11. Remove electrodes and dispose — remove adhesive electrodes and dispose. Provide patient care as needed. Document completion in the medical record.
  12. Deliver ECG for interpretation — report to ordering physician or nurse. Note any urgent findings immediately.
Lead II — Most Common Rhythm Strip Lead: Lead II provides the clearest view of P waves and QRS complexes for rhythm analysis because the electrical axis of the heart runs roughly from right shoulder to left leg — aligned with Lead II's positive electrode on the left leg. When monitoring rhythm continuously (telemetry), Lead II is the standard monitoring lead in most institutions.

Calculating Heart Rate from the ECG Strip

HIGH-YIELD — 1500 METHOD IS NBRC TESTED
1500 METHOD — REGULAR RHYTHM
Heart Rate (bpm) = 1500 ÷ (Number of small boxes between two consecutive R waves)
Example: 20 small boxes between R waves → 1500 ÷ 20 = 75 bpm
Example: 15 small boxes → 1500 ÷ 15 = 100 bpm
Why 1500?
ECG paper runs at 25 mm/sec. Each small box = 0.04 sec. In one minute: 60 sec ÷ 0.04 sec/box = 1500 boxes per minute. Dividing 1500 by the number of boxes between R waves gives cycles per minute = heart rate.
Irregular Rhythms
For irregular rhythms, count the number of complete R-R intervals in 6 seconds of tracing (30 large boxes), then multiply by 10 to get approximate heart rate per minute. Less precise but appropriate for irregular rhythms like atrial fibrillation.

Noninvasive Blood Pressure Monitoring Equipment

EQUIPMENT: SPHYGMOMANOMETER & AUTOMATED NIBP
Sphygmomanometer Components
Bladder/cuff — inflatable bladder inside the cuff; the bladder must cover 80% of arm circumference. Aneroid manometer — dial gauge measuring pressure in mmHg. Bulb + pressure valve — hand inflation and controlled release. Stethoscope — for Korotkoff sounds with manual technique.
Cuff Sizing — Critical for Accuracy
Cuff too small → falsely HIGH blood pressure reading. Cuff too large → falsely LOW blood pressure reading. Cuff width should be 40% of arm circumference. Pediatric, adult, and large adult sizes must be correctly matched to the patient. A common clinical error is using a standard adult cuff on an obese patient.
Automated NIBP Systems
Oscillometric technology — detects arterial wall oscillations during cuff deflation. Advantages: trending capability, programmable alarm intervals, continuous automated measurements. Limitations: motion artifact (patient movement or shivering), inaccurate with arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation), poor perfusion states cause unreliable readings.

Arterial Line (A-Line) — Setup & Equipment

VERY HIGH-YIELD — SETUP AND TROUBLESHOOTING ARE NBRC TESTED

An arterial line provides continuous beat-to-beat arterial pressure monitoring and a reliable access point for frequent arterial blood gas sampling. It is the standard of care for hemodynamically unstable patients and during major surgical procedures.

Preferred Insertion Sites

Radial Artery — First Choice
Most commonly used. Accessible, relatively superficial, and has excellent collateral circulation from the ulnar artery. Before insertion, many providers perform an Allen's test to confirm ulnar collateral flow. Located at the wrist just proximal to the thumb side.
Brachial Artery
Alternative when radial is inaccessible. Located in the antecubital fossa. Carries slightly higher complication risk (less collateral circulation than the radial site). Avoid if possible in trauma patients with arm injuries.
Femoral Artery
Large vessel, easy to palpate, often used in emergencies or when peripheral access is difficult. Higher infection risk due to groin location. Cannot be easily compressed if bleeding occurs. Requires immobilization of the hip.

Pressure Bag
Maintains the flush solution (heparinized saline or normal saline) in the pressurized bag at approximately 300 mmHg. This pressure is higher than arterial pressure — keeps the system flushed continuously at 3–5 mL/hour, preventing clotting at the catheter tip. Must be visually checked; if bag empties, catheter clots.
Transducer
The transducer converts the mechanical pressure of the arterial pulse into an electrical signal that the monitor displays as a waveform. The transducer must be properly zeroed and leveled to the phlebostatic axis for accurate readings. The transducer is a critical equipment component — a defective transducer produces inaccurate pressures regardless of catheter position.

Transducer Leveling — Phlebostatic Axis

⭐ Phlebostatic Axis
The correct reference point for transducer leveling. Located at the intersection of:
• 4th intercostal space
• Midaxillary line (halfway between anterior and posterior chest)

This point approximates the level of the right atrium — zeroing the transducer here ensures readings are referenced to cardiac level.
Why Level Matters
If transducer is TOO HIGH (above phlebostatic axis): BP reads FALSE LOW (gravity removes some pressure from the reading).

If transducer is TOO LOW (below phlebostatic axis): BP reads FALSE HIGH (hydrostatic pressure adds to reading).

Every 2.5 cm above/below the phlebostatic axis changes the reading by approximately 2 mmHg.

Zeroing the Transducer

  1. Level transducer at the phlebostatic axis
  2. Open the transducer stopcock to air (closes connection to patient, opens to atmosphere)
  3. Press the "zero" button on the bedside monitor
  4. Confirm zero reading on the display (0 mmHg)
  5. Close stopcock back to the patient (restore monitoring position)
  6. Verify waveform and pressure reading are appropriate

Pulmonary Artery Catheter (PAC) — Swan-Ganz

VERY HIGH-YIELD — INSERTION PATHWAY AND MEASUREMENTS ARE NBRC TESTED

The pulmonary artery catheter (PAC), also called a Swan-Ganz or balloon-tipped flow-directed catheter, is a long, flexible catheter that is floated through the right heart into the pulmonary artery using a small inflated balloon at its tip. Each anatomical location the catheter passes through generates a characteristic pressure waveform — allowing the clinician to confirm position and collect hemodynamic data.

Cardiac Output Monitoring — Thermodilution & Other Methods

HIGH-YIELD — THERMODILUTION IS CLINICAL GOLD STANDARD
⭐ Thermodilution — Clinical Gold Standard
Procedure: A precise volume of cold saline (typically 10 mL at a known temperature, usually room temperature or iced) is injected rapidly into the PAC's proximal port (right atrium). The thermistor near the catheter tip detects the temperature change in the pulmonary artery as the cold bolus mixes with blood. A cardiac output computer integrates the temperature-time curve and calculates CO.

Formula: CO = Stewart-Hamilton (temperature change × time curve integration). Performed in triplicate — average of 3 measurements within 1°C of each other used.

Normal CO: 4–8 L/min. Normal Cardiac Index (CI = CO/BSA): 2.5–4.0 L/min/m²
Other Cardiac Output Methods
Fick Method: VO₂ / (CaO₂ − CvO₂). Physiologic gold standard (reference standard). Requires simultaneous arterial and mixed venous blood gas samples and O₂ consumption measurement. Complex and time-consuming.

Impedance Cardiography / Plethysmography: Noninvasive. Surface electrodes detect changes in thoracic electrical impedance with each heartbeat. Less accurate in edema, arrhythmias, or obesity.

Transesophageal Doppler: Probe in esophagus measures aortic flow velocity. Minimally invasive; good trending capability.

Hemodynamic Concepts — Equipment Context

These concepts explain what the monitoring equipment is measuring and why each number matters for clinical decisions.

Preload
The filling pressure/volume in the ventricle at the END of diastole — just before the next contraction. Measured indirectly by CVP (right preload) and PAOP/wedge (left preload). High preload: volume overload, heart failure. Low preload: hypovolemia, dehydration.
Afterload
The resistance the ventricle must overcome to eject blood. For the left ventricle: estimated by Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR). For the right ventricle: estimated by Pulmonary Vascular Resistance (PVR). High afterload = cardiac stress. Low afterload (vasodilation/shock) = reduced BP despite adequate CO.
Cardiac Output (CO) & Cardiac Index (CI)
CO = Heart Rate × Stroke Volume (volume per beat). Normal CO: 4–8 L/min. Cardiac Index adjusts CO for body size: CI = CO / BSA. Normal CI: 2.5–4.0 L/min/m². CI is more meaningful for comparing patients of different sizes.
Ejection Fraction (EF)
The percentage of blood ejected from the left ventricle with each contraction. Normal: 50–70%. EF <40%: reduced systolic function (HFrEF). Measured by echocardiography, not by the PAC. Reduced EF is associated with increased PAOP (backup pressure from failing left heart).

Common NBRC Traps — Cardiovascular Monitoring

  • V1 is at the RIGHT sternal border (4th ICS), not the left. This is the most commonly misplaced chest lead. V2 is the left sternal border at the same level (4th ICS). Swapping V1 and V2 produces a mirrored chest lead configuration that can simulate posterior MI.
  • V4 is at the 5th ICS midclavicular line. V5 is at the anterior axillary line at the same horizontal level as V4, and V6 is at the midaxillary line at the same level. Placing V5 and V6 too low is a common error.
  • Lead II is the most common rhythm strip lead. The standard monitoring lead for rhythm analysis is Lead II. When asked which lead provides the clearest P waves for rhythm interpretation, the answer is Lead II.
  • Cuff size error produces inaccurate BP. Too small = falsely high. Too large = falsely low. The cuff bladder should cover 80% of arm circumference and the cuff width should be 40% of arm circumference.
  • Transducer too high = false low BP; transducer too low = false high BP. Level the transducer at the phlebostatic axis (4th ICS, midaxillary line). Every 2.5 cm of incorrect leveling changes the reading by approximately 2 mmHg.
  • Dampened A-line waveform: flush the line first. A flattened, indistinct waveform typically indicates air or clot. The first action is flushing. Re-leveling and re-zeroing are for accuracy issues, not dampening.
  • PAOP requires the balloon to be INFLATED (wedged). PAP is measured with balloon deflated (continuous monitoring). PAOP/wedge is measured with balloon inflated (briefly). Never leave the balloon inflated continuously — pulmonary infarction risk.
  • Thermodilution is the clinical gold standard for CO measurement. Fick method is the physiologic reference standard but is impractical clinically. Thermodilution via PAC is standard in critical care.

FAQ

Common Questions

Why does scrubbing with alcohol improve the ECG signal?

Two reasons. First, skin oils and dead skin cells are poor electrical conductors — they create high impedance at the skin-electrode interface, which the amplifier then amplifies alongside the cardiac signal (producing noise). Alcohol dissolves the oils. Second, the mechanical scrubbing action removes the outermost layer of dead skin (stratum corneum), which is the highest-impedance layer. Removing it allows the electrode gel to make better contact with the living skin below, dramatically reducing interface impedance and improving signal quality. This is why simply wiping (not scrubbing) alcohol is insufficient — the mechanical abrasion is as important as the solvent action.

Why is the radial artery the preferred A-line site?

Several reasons make the radial artery ideal: (1) Dual circulation — the hand receives blood from both the radial and ulnar arteries via the palmar arch. If the radial artery is damaged by catheterization, the ulnar artery maintains hand perfusion. The Allen's test is performed to confirm adequate ulnar collateral flow before radial cannulation. (2) Superficial location — easily palpable and accessible. (3) Compressible — bleeding from the insertion site can be controlled with direct pressure. (4) Low infection risk compared to femoral sites. (5) Allows arm mobility. The femoral artery is an alternative in emergencies but carries higher infection and complication rates.

What exactly is the phlebostatic axis and why does it matter?

The phlebostatic axis is an anatomical reference point that approximates the position of the right atrium when a patient is supine. It is located at the intersection of the 4th intercostal space and the midaxillary line (halfway between the anterior and posterior chest surfaces). All invasive hemodynamic pressures — arterial line, CVP, PAP, PAOP — are referenced to this zero point because they are generated by the heart and must be measured relative to cardiac level. If the transducer is above the right atrium, gravity reduces the measured pressure (reads low). If below, hydrostatic pressure adds to the measurement (reads high). Every 2.5 cm of incorrect leveling introduces approximately a 2 mmHg error — significant for CVP and PAOP measurements where the normal range is narrow (2–6 and 6–12 mmHg respectively).

Why must the PAC balloon be deflated quickly after measuring PAOP?

When the PAC balloon is inflated, it occludes blood flow in a small pulmonary artery branch. This creates a zone of no-flow that is necessary for the PAOP measurement — but it is also creating a pulmonary infarction if maintained. The pulmonary parenchyma distal to the occluded vessel depends entirely on that blood flow. Ischemic damage begins within seconds to minutes of complete occlusion. Guidelines recommend deflating the balloon within 15–20 seconds (some protocols say 8–10 seconds), immediately after confirming the wedge pressure reading. The return of the PA waveform (pulsatile with dicrotic notch) confirms balloon deflation. If the balloon cannot be deflated — which can happen if it ruptures inside and is trapped — this is a medical emergency requiring immediate provider notification.
