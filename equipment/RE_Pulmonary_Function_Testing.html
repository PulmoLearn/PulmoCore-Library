Introduction to Pulmonary Function Testing

Pulmonary function tests provide objective measurements of how well the lungs work. Unlike assessment findings (which are subjective and observer-dependent), PFT data is quantifiable, reproducible, and comparable to predicted normal values based on age, height, sex, and ethnicity.

What PFTs Are Used For
Diagnose lung disease (asthma, COPD, fibrosis) · Monitor disease progression · Assess response to bronchodilator therapy · Evaluate surgical risk · Screen high-risk occupational exposures · Confirm disability claims
What PFTs Measure
Volume — in liters (L) or milliliters (mL): how much air the lungs hold or move. Flow — in L/sec or L/min: how fast air moves. Pressure — in cmH₂O: the strength of respiratory muscles.
The RT's Role
RTs perform, coach, and quality-check PFT maneuvers. The validity of results depends as much on RT coaching skill as on the patient's effort. A perfectly functioning spirometer still produces invalid data from poor technique.

Accuracy vs. Precision in PFT

Accuracy
How close a measurement is to the true value. A spirometer that consistently reads 2.7 L when the actual volume is 3.0 L is inaccurate — even if it reads 2.7 L every time. Calibration corrects inaccuracy.
Precision (Reproducibility)
How consistently a measurement is repeated. A spirometer that reads 3.0, 2.9, and 3.1 on the same input is precise. PFT validity requires both accuracy (verified by calibration) and precision (verified by repeated acceptable maneuvers).
Clinical Relevance: Poor patient effort reduces accuracy — the result doesn't reflect true lung function. If two of three FVC maneuvers differ by more than 150 mL, the test lacks reproducibility. Both must be met for a valid PFT report. The RT's coaching and quality control determine whether data is usable or discardable.

Spirometer Types

HIGH-YIELD EQUIPMENT OVERVIEW

Volume-Displacement Spirometers

These measure volume directly by tracking physical displacement — a bell rising, bellows expanding, or a piston moving. They were the gold standard before electronic flow sensors.

Water-Sealed Spirometer
A lightweight bell floats on water. As the patient exhales, the bell rises. Volume is read from the bell's displacement. Accurate but bulky, requires level surface, not portable. Classic reference device.
Wedge Bellows Spirometer
A wedge-shaped bellows expands as air is exhaled into it. Volume is calculated from bellows displacement. More portable than water-sealed. Still gravity-dependent — must remain upright.
Dry Rolling Seal Spirometer
A piston moves inside a cylinder sealed by a rolling plastic membrane. Can measure both volumes and expiratory flow rates. Portable, reliable, minimal maintenance compared to wet-seal systems.

Flow-Sensing Spirometers

These measure airflow electronically and integrate the signal over time to calculate volume. They are the most common type in modern clinical and office spirometry.

Pneumotachograph
Measures pressure drop across a resistance element to calculate flow. Highly accurate. Used in most modern spirometers and ventilators. Covered in detail in a later section.
Turbine (Vane) Flowmeter
A rotating vane spins as air passes through. Rotation rate = flow rate. Low resistance, portable. Must be kept clean — debris impairs vane rotation. Can be damaged at very high flow rates (>300 L/min).
Ultrasonic Flowmeter
Uses ultrasonic signals crossing the airstream at an angle. Flow rate alters signal travel time. No moving parts — highly durable and infection-resistant. Used in many modern portable spirometers.

Point-of-Care Spirometry & Accessories

EQUIPMENT: Bedside Spirometer System
Indications
Asthma monitoring · COPD assessment · Preoperative screening · Office-based pulmonary evaluation · Bedside assessment in ICU or step-down. Not appropriate when patient cannot follow commands or produce effort.
Contraindications
Recent thoracic or abdominal surgery (forced maneuvers increase intrathoracic pressure) · Hemoptysis of unknown origin · Active pneumothorax · Unstable cardiovascular status · Severe respiratory distress.
Complete Setup
Handheld spirometer body · Disposable mouthpiece · Bacterial/viral filter · Connecting tubing · Nose clips · Patient data entry (age, sex, height, ethnicity for predicted values) · Calibration verification.

Accessories — Equipment Profiles

Nose Clips
Pinch nose closed to prevent air escaping during maneuvers. Applied before the patient seals the mouthpiece. Must be snug but not painful. If a patient has nasal anatomy that prevents seal, have them pinch their nose manually. Without nose clips, exhaled air leaks through the nose, artificially lowering measured FVC and FEV₁.
Disposable Mouthpieces
Provide a sealed pathway from patient to sensor. Patient must: seal lips completely around the mouthpiece · keep tongue out of the opening · not bite down or block the lumen. A partial seal or blocked mouthpiece is the most common cause of falsely low spirometry values on the NBRC.
Bacterial/Viral Filters
Placed between the mouthpiece and the spirometer to prevent cross-contamination of the sensor and patient-to-patient pathogen transmission. Essential after COVID-era aerosol precaution changes. Replace between patients. A clogged or wet filter increases resistance and falsely lowers measured flows.

Calibration — The 3-Liter Syringe

VERY HIGH-YIELD — CALIBRATE BEFORE EVERY SESSION

Spirometer calibration uses a precision 3-liter syringe (sometimes called the "super syringe") to verify that the spirometer's volume measurement is accurate. This must be performed before patient testing every day and after any maintenance or transport.

Why 3 Liters?
3 L is within the clinical range of expected lung volumes and is large enough to reveal sensor drift or leaks. ATS/ERS standards require calibration within ±3.5% of the syringe volume, meaning the spirometer must read between 2.895 L and 3.105 L.
Why Multiple Flow Rates?
Spirometers must be accurate across the full range of clinical flow rates — from a slow, controlled exhalation to a maximal forced effort. Calibration at slow, medium, and fast flows reveals whether the sensor is accurate throughout the patient's expected effort range.
Calibration Failure — Next Steps
If any flow rate fails (reading outside ±3.5%), the spirometer must not be used for patient testing. Repeat calibration after checking tubing connections and ensuring the syringe is not leaking. If failure persists, remove device from service and report to biomedical engineering.

Activity: Spirometer Calibration Procedure

Put each calibration step in the correct order. All must be correct to continue.

Power on the spirometer and allow adequate warm-up time; verify patient data is cleared
Attach the 3-L calibration syringe firmly to the spirometer inlet (no patient mouthpiece)
Inject the full 3-liter volume three times — at a slow, medium, and fast flow rate
Verify all readings fall within ±3.5% of 3.00 L (acceptable range: 2.895–3.105 L)
Document calibration results; if failed, remove device from service before any patient testing
Put all five steps in the correct order to continue.

Peak Flow Meters

HIGH-YIELD — ASTHMA MANAGEMENT DEVICE

Peak flow meters measure Peak Expiratory Flow (PEF) — the maximum speed of air expelled during a forced exhalation. They are simple, inexpensive, and extremely practical for asthma monitoring in both clinical and home settings.

Components
Mouthpiece · Sliding indicator marker · Graduated scale (L/min) · Vane or orifice mechanism that resists airflow and measures peak flow. No batteries required — purely mechanical.
Indications
Home asthma monitoring · Emergency assessment of asthma severity · Tracking response to bronchodilator therapy · Identifying patterns of diurnal variation (morning dips common in asthma). Not a replacement for spirometry.

Correct Peak Flow Technique

  1. Reset the sliding marker to zero before each attempt
  2. Patient stands upright if possible (or sits erect)
  3. Take the largest breath possible — maximal inhalation
  4. Seal lips tightly around the mouthpiece
  5. Blast air out as hard and fast as possible in a single short burst — PEF is a PEAK, not a sustained effort
  6. Record the value shown by the sliding marker
  7. Repeat 3 times; record the best (highest) of three acceptable attempts

The Asthma Zone System

Compare current PEF to the patient's personal best (established during a well period). The percentage determines the management zone.

PEAK FLOW PERCENTAGE
% of Personal Best = (Current PEF ÷ Personal Best PEF) × 100
Example: 300 L/min ÷ 450 L/min × 100 = 67% → Yellow Zone
🟢 Green Zone: 80–100% of personal best — Lungs are functioning well. Continue regular medications.
🟡 Yellow Zone: 50–79% — Caution. Asthma is not well controlled. Add rescue inhaler; contact provider.
🔴 Red Zone: <50% — Emergency. Severe airway obstruction. Administer bronchodilator; seek emergency care immediately.

Clinical Check: Peak Flow Zone

A patient with asthma has a documented personal best peak flow of 500 L/min. During an acute visit she reports severe shortness of breath. Her current peak flow reads 215 L/min. What zone does this fall into, and what is the immediate action?

Pneumotachographs & Flow Sensors

EQUIPMENT: Pneumotachograph

A pneumotachograph (pneumotach) measures airflow by detecting the pressure difference across a known resistance element. Flow is directly proportional to pressure drop. The flow signal is integrated electronically over time to calculate volume.

Fleisch Pneumotachograph
A bundle of brass capillary tubes creates resistance. Heated to prevent condensation. Measures bidirectional flow (inspiration and expiration). High accuracy — used in research-grade spirometers and ventilators. Requires careful cleaning.
Screen (Lilly) Pneumotachograph
A fine wire mesh screen creates resistance. Less expensive, often disposable. Unidirectional flow (expiration only in some designs). Common in modern disposable spirometer heads — one screen per patient eliminates cross-contamination risk.
Variable Orifice Pneumotachograph
A flap or cone creates variable resistance proportional to flow. Bidirectional. Less accurate at very low and very high flows. Used in some portable spirometers and ventilators as a compromise between cost and performance.
Ultrasonic/Vortex Flowmeter
Ultrasonic signals cross the airstream at an angle. Flow alters signal transit time — calculated electronically. No moving parts, no heat required, no cleaning concerns. High durability. Used in premium portable spirometers.
RT Clinical Note: In modern practice, the RT rarely needs to identify the internal sensor type — but understanding how each works helps troubleshoot. A wet or condensation-blocked Fleisch pneumotach will read inaccurately. A damaged screen in a screen pneumotach produces turbulence and flow measurement errors.

Spirometry Maneuvers & Patient Coaching

VERY HIGH-YIELD — COACHING IS EVERYTHING

Spirometry is entirely effort-dependent. A technically perfect setup with a calibrated spirometer and cooperative patient can still produce invalid results if the RT does not coach the maneuver correctly. Coaching technique is a testable RT competency.

The FVC Maneuver — Coaching Script

What to say — in order:
"Take the biggest breath in you possibly can — fill your lungs completely…" (pause while patient inhales)
"Now BLAST it out! HARD! FAST! Keep going! Keep blowing! Don't stop! Blow, blow, BLOW!"
"Good — that's it! You can stop now."
After 3 acceptable maneuvers: "You did great — rest for a moment."
Common ErrorCauseWhat the RT Should Do
Hesitation before blast (submaximal start)Patient not ready; poor coachingRepeat: "When I say go, blast immediately — don't wait!"
Early termination (<6 seconds)Fatigue; misunderstanding"Keep going even when it feels empty — blow until I say stop!"
Coughing during maneuverAirway irritation; high effortDiscard that attempt; allow rest; try again
Air leak around mouthpiecePoor lip seal; anatomic difficultyReposition mouthpiece; coach lip seal; try nose clip tighter
Tongue blocking mouthpiecePatient unfamiliar with deviceDemonstrate visually; "keep your tongue down and out of the way"

Activity: FVC Maneuver Sequence

Put the steps of a correct FVC spirometry maneuver in the right order.

Instruct the patient to inhale as deeply and completely as possible — maximum inspiration before starting
Have the patient seal lips tightly around the mouthpiece (nose clips already applied)
Coach the patient to blast air out immediately — as hard and as fast as possible from the first instant
Actively coach continued exhalation: "Keep going! Keep blowing! Don't stop!" until effort is complete
Maintain the effort for at least 6 seconds (or until a volume plateau <25 mL over 1 second) before ending
Order all five FVC steps to continue.

FEV₁, FVC & Spirometry Interpretation

VERY HIGH-YIELD — NBRC EXAM CORE

Key Spirometry Values

ParameterDefinitionTypical Normal (Adult)
FVCForced Vital Capacity — total air expelled during a maximal forced exhalation3–5 L (varies by demographics)
FEV₁Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 second — volume exhaled in the first second of FVC2.5–4.5 L
FEV₁/FVC RatioThe fraction of FVC exhaled in the first second — the obstruction indicator>0.70 (70%)
PEFPeak Expiratory Flow — the highest flow rate achieved during the FVC maneuver350–700 L/min (varies)

Obstructive vs. Restrictive Patterns

Obstructive Pattern

FEV₁/FVC < 0.70 — the ratio is reduced because air cannot exit fast enough.

The lungs may hold normal or increased volume, but airflow OUT is impaired (airway narrowing, collapse, mucus). FEV₁ is disproportionately reduced relative to FVC.

Examples: COPD, asthma, bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis

Flow-volume loop: Concave (scooped) expiratory limb; reduced PEF

Restrictive Pattern

FEV₁/FVC ≥ 0.70 with reduced FVC and TLC — the ratio is normal or elevated because the lungs are simply small.

The lungs cannot fully inflate. Airflow per unit volume is normal or fast — the problem is the container, not the pipes. FVC and TLC are both reduced proportionally.

Examples: Pulmonary fibrosis, neuromuscular disease, obesity, kyphoscoliosis, pleural effusion

Flow-volume loop: Small but normally shaped loop; proportionally reduced volumes

Flow-Volume Loop Comparison

Expiratory flow-volume curves. Obstructive: lower peak, concave (scooped) mid-curve. Restrictive: smaller loop, normal shape.

Lung Volumes & Capacities

Understanding lung volumes is foundational for interpreting PFT results and knowing what each test can — and cannot — measure.

Volume Compartments

📏 IRV — Inspiratory Reserve Volume~3,000 mL
💨 VT — Tidal Volume (normal breath)~500 mL
📉 ERV — Expiratory Reserve Volume~1,200 mL
🔒 RV — Residual Volume (cannot be measured by spirometry)~1,200 mL
CapacityComponentsCan Spirometry Measure?
VC (Vital Capacity)IRV + VT + ERV✅ Yes
FVCForced VC — same volumes, maximal effort✅ Yes
IC (Inspiratory Capacity)IRV + VT✅ Yes
FRC (Functional Residual Capacity)ERV + RV❌ No — requires gas dilution or body box
TLC (Total Lung Capacity)All volumes combined❌ No — requires body box or dilution
RV (Residual Volume)Air remaining after maximal exhalation❌ No — cannot be expelled; requires gas dilution or plethysmography
Critical NBRC Point: Residual Volume (RV) cannot be measured by any spirometry technique because it is air that cannot be exhaled — no matter how hard the patient tries, this air stays in the lungs. Measuring RV requires either body plethysmography or gas dilution (helium dilution or nitrogen washout).

Body Plethysmography ("Body Box")

HIGH-YIELD ADVANCED PFT EQUIPMENT

Body plethysmography measures lung volumes that spirometry cannot — especially RV and TLC — and quantifies airway resistance. It is considered the gold standard for comprehensive lung volume measurement.

What It Measures
Residual Volume (RV) · Total Lung Capacity (TLC) · Functional Residual Capacity (FRC) · Airway resistance (Raw) · Trapped gas volume. Invaluable in severe COPD, emphysema, and suspected restriction.
Key Components
Sealed, airtight chamber · Pressure sensors (box pressure and mouth pressure) · Mouthpiece with shutter valve · Computer system for data calculation. Patient breathes inside the sealed chamber — pressure changes in the box reflect lung volume changes.
Procedure Overview
Patient enters chamber; technician seals door. Nose clips applied. Patient breathes through mouthpiece. Shutter closes; patient makes panting maneuvers against the closed shutter. Boyle's law relates the pressure changes to lung volumes.
ProblemCauseRT Action
Claustrophobia / panic during testPatient anxiety in sealed chamberReassure; keep chamber door visible; window if available; allow adjustment time; may need to abort
Invalid panting techniquePatient moving jaw, swallowing, or using tongue instead of chestRecoach: "Keep your cheeks still and puff from your chest — like fogging a mirror"
Air leak around mouthpiecePoor seal; patient pulling awayReposition; use different mouthpiece size; recoach
Inconsistent pressure tracingsVariable effort; irregular breathing patternRest patient; repeat instructions; try again after equilibration

Clinical Check: When Is the Body Box Needed?

A 68-year-old patient with severe emphysema has bedside spirometry showing FEV₁/FVC of 42%, FEV₁ at 35% predicted, and FVC at 62% predicted. The physician wants to assess the degree of air trapping and total lung hyperinflation. Which test provides the necessary measurements?

Gas Dilution Techniques

Gas dilution tests measure FRC (and from it, RV and TLC) by analyzing changes in known gas concentrations as the patient breathes. They are less accurate than body plethysmography in severe obstruction with trapped air — trapped air may not equilibrate with the dilution gas.

Helium Dilution
The patient breathes a known concentration of helium from a closed circuit. Helium equilibrates with the lung gas. The final helium concentration reveals lung volume. Requires a helium analyzer, CO₂ absorber, and breathing circuit.

Limitation: Helium cannot enter trapped air (bullae, severely obstructed airways) — so plethysmography is more accurate in severe COPD.
Nitrogen Washout
The patient breathes 100% oxygen, washing nitrogen out of the lungs. The total volume of nitrogen exhaled (collected) is used to calculate FRC. Requires 7–10 minutes of breathing 100% O₂.

Limitation: Also cannot measure trapped air — the nitrogen in poorly ventilated regions may not wash out completely. Less practical than helium dilution in most labs.
Comparison Summary: Body plethysmography (> gas dilution in obstruction) because it measures ALL gas in the thorax — including trapped air that doesn't communicate with the airway. Gas dilution only measures communicating gas. In restriction, all three methods typically agree well.

Respiratory Pressure Measurements

EQUIPMENT: Aneroid Manometer · Pressure Gauge

Maximum inspiratory and expiratory pressures measure the strength of respiratory muscles. These measurements are essential for evaluating neuromuscular disease, guiding ventilator weaning decisions, and assessing respiratory muscle fatigue.

MIP / NIF — Maximum Inspiratory Pressure
(Also: Negative Inspiratory Force — NIF)
The most negative airway pressure generated during a maximal inspiratory effort against a closed airway.

Normal: -60 to -100 cmH₂O
Values less negative than -20 to -25 cmH₂O suggest inability to sustain spontaneous ventilation or protect the airway.
MEP — Maximum Expiratory Pressure
The highest positive airway pressure generated during a maximal expiratory effort against a closed airway. Reflects expiratory muscle strength — needed for coughing effectively.

Normal: 80–100+ cmH₂O
Low MEP = weak cough = retained secretions = atelectasis risk.

MIP/NIF Procedure

  1. Apply mouthpiece and nose clips; connect to aneroid pressure manometer
  2. Have patient exhale to residual volume (or functional residual capacity) — measured at end of normal exhalation
  3. Occlude the airway with the shutter valve
  4. Instruct patient to inhale as hard as possible and sustain the effort for at least 1 second
  5. Record the most negative pressure achieved — this is the MIP/NIF value
  6. Repeat for reproducibility; record the best (most negative) value
Clinical Significance: MIP/NIF is a key component of ventilator weaning assessment. A patient with MIP of -20 cmH₂O or less negative is unlikely to sustain spontaneous breathing. A target of at least -20 to -25 cmH₂O (more negative = stronger) is often required before extubation trials in neuromuscular patients.

Escalation & RT Clinical Pearls

When to Escalate Beyond Bedside Spirometry

  • Unexplained dyspnea with normal or near-normal bedside spirometry → full PFT lab with gas dilution or body box
  • Spirometry pattern unclear (possible mixed obstruction and restriction) → body plethysmography
  • Suspected air trapping in severe COPD/emphysema → body box for RV/TLC measurement
  • Neuromuscular weakness suspected → MIP/MEP + bedside spirometry; may escalate to serial NIF monitoring
  • Asthma severity not responding to expected treatment → bronchodilator study with pre- and post-spirometry

RT Clinical Pearls

  • Spirometry is entirely effort-dependent — even perfect equipment produces invalid data from a patient who doesn't understand or won't perform the maneuver.
  • Calibrate before testing, not after — a calibration performed after patient testing does not validate the results; it only tells you what happened after the fact.
  • Residual Volume cannot be measured by spirometry — regardless of how long or hard the patient blows, some air always remains. Period.
  • Nose clips are not optional — air leaking through the nose during an FVC maneuver produces falsely low FVC and FEV₁ that can be mistaken for obstruction or restriction.
  • FEV₁/FVC ratio classifies the pattern — FVC magnitude confirms severity — the ratio tells you obstructive vs. restrictive; how far FVC or FEV₁ drops from predicted tells you mild/moderate/severe.
  • Peak flow is a screening tool, not a spirometry replacement — it cannot distinguish obstruction from restriction, and cannot replace FVC/FEV₁ for diagnosis or NBRC clinical scenarios.

Common NBRC Traps — PFT

  • Treating a low FVC with a normal FEV₁/FVC ratio as obstruction. A normal or elevated ratio with low FVC indicates restriction — not obstruction. Obstruction requires a low ratio (<0.70).
  • Stating that RV can be measured by bedside spirometry. It cannot. RV is air that cannot be expelled — spirometry only measures what can be blown out. This is one of the most frequently tested PFT facts on the NBRC.
  • Interpreting poor effort as pathology. A patient who terminates the FVC maneuver at 2–3 seconds will produce artificially low FVC and may appear to have restriction. Always rule out technique error before concluding disease.
  • Calibrating after patient testing. Calibration must be performed before testing. Calibrating afterward cannot validate results already collected — it only confirms whether the device was working at the end of the session.
  • Confusing FVC and VC. VC is a slow, relaxed vital capacity maneuver. FVC is performed with forced, maximal effort. In obstruction, FVC can be lower than VC due to dynamic airway collapse during forced effort.
  • Assuming body plethysmography and helium dilution always agree. In severe obstruction with trapped air, plethysmography reports higher TLC than helium dilution — because trapped air communicates with the box pressure but not with the helium circuit.
  • Using PEF alone to classify obstruction severity. Peak flow is effort-dependent and cannot differentiate obstruction from restriction. Only FEV₁/FVC provides the pattern classification needed for clinical decision-making.

Stage 1: Emergency Department Presentation

Situation: A 24-year-old female with known asthma presents to the ED with wheezing, chest tightness, and dyspnea. She is alert and able to cooperate. Her documented personal best peak flow is 480 L/min. A peak flow measurement is obtained: 192 L/min.

Decision: What zone is this result, and what is the immediate clinical response?

Stage 2: Post-Bronchodilator Spirometry

Update: After an initial albuterol nebulizer treatment, the patient's breathing improves somewhat. The physician orders spirometry to assess current lung function. Results:

Pre-bronchodilator: FVC 2.4 L (pred 3.8 L = 63%), FEV₁ 1.2 L (pred 3.2 L = 38%), FEV₁/FVC = 50%

Decision: What spirometry pattern do these results represent?

Stage 3: Bronchodilator Response

Update: After a second albuterol treatment, post-bronchodilator spirometry is performed. Results: FVC 3.0 L, FEV₁ 1.7 L. A significant bronchodilator response is defined as ≥12% improvement in FEV₁ AND ≥200 mL absolute increase from baseline.

Pre-bronchodilator FEV₁: 1.2 L → Post-bronchodilator FEV₁: 1.7 L

Decision: Does this patient demonstrate a significant bronchodilator response?

Case Summary: This case demonstrates the complete asthma assessment pathway: peak flow for rapid severity classification → spirometry for pattern identification → post-bronchodilator spirometry for reversibility. A significant bronchodilator response (≥12% and ≥200 mL FEV₁ improvement) confirms reversible obstruction — the hallmark of asthma versus fixed COPD.

FAQ

Common Questions

Why are nose clips required for spirometry?

During a forced spirometry maneuver, the patient generates significant positive airway pressure. Without nose clips, air follows the path of least resistance — and the nose is an easy escape route. This produces an air leak that reduces the measured FVC and FEV₁. The patient may appear to have restriction or obstruction when the only problem is a missing nose clip. This is so commonly tested on the NBRC that the reflex response to "low spirometry values" should always include: "were nose clips in place?"

Why must calibration happen before patient testing?

Calibration before testing verifies that the instrument is accurate for that session. Environmental factors — temperature, humidity, altitude, and barometric pressure changes — can alter spirometer performance day to day. A calibration performed after the session tells you whether the device was drifting at the end — but cannot retroactively validate results already recorded. ATS/ERS guidelines require daily calibration (at minimum) before any patient spirometry. Some labs with heavy volume calibrate at the beginning of each clinic session or after environmental changes.

Why can't bedside spirometry measure Residual Volume?

Spirometry measures what moves — specifically, the air that can be inhaled and exhaled. Residual Volume (RV) is the air that permanently remains in the lungs after a maximal exhalation. No matter how hard or how long a patient blows, this air cannot exit. Since spirometry is a flow and volume measurement device (not a pressure measurement device), it has no way to sense or calculate gas that isn't moving through the sensor. RV measurement requires a physical principle that can account for static gas — either Boyle's Law (body plethysmography) or a tracer gas that diffuses throughout the lung (helium dilution or nitrogen washout).

What is the body plethysmograph actually measuring?

The body box uses Boyle's Law (P₁V₁ = P₂V₂) — the inverse relationship between pressure and volume of a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature. When the patient makes breathing efforts against a closed shutter inside the sealed chamber, their lung volume changes slightly with each effort. This causes a reciprocal change in the pressure inside the sealed chamber (as the patient's thorax compresses the chamber air). By measuring both mouth pressure (lung) and box pressure, the system can calculate the volume of gas in the lungs at the moment of measurement — even gas that is trapped and doesn't communicate with the airway.

What makes spirometry effort-dependent?

Spirometry measures voluntary maneuvers — the patient must choose to inhale as deeply as possible and exhale as hard and fast as possible. Unlike pulse oximetry or a chest X-ray, the device cannot force the result. A patient who inhales only 70% of their capacity before blowing will show a falsely low FVC. A patient who stops exhaling at 3 seconds will have an artificially shortened curve. A patient who hesitates at the start of the blast will lose PEF. The RT's coaching during each maneuver is the single most important factor determining whether results reflect true lung function.

How is a significant bronchodilator response defined?

Per ATS/ERS guidelines, a significant bronchodilator response requires both: (1) an absolute increase in FEV₁ of ≥200 mL (0.2 L), AND (2) a percentage increase of ≥12% relative to the pre-bronchodilator baseline. Both criteria must be met simultaneously. A large absolute increase in a patient with severe obstruction may not meet the percentage threshold; a small absolute increase with a high percentage change may not meet the absolute threshold. A significant response supports reversible airway obstruction — the hallmark of asthma versus fixed COPD.

Summary & Board-Prep Reminders

Spirometry Essentials
FVC = total forced exhalation. FEV₁ = first second. FEV₁/FVC <70% = obstruction. Normal/high ratio + low FVC = restriction. Calibrate before testing. Nose clips every time.
What Spirometry Can't Do
Cannot measure RV, TLC, or FRC. These require body plethysmography or gas dilution. If pattern is unclear or trapped air is suspected → escalate to full PFT lab.
Peak Flow Zones
Green = 80–100% (normal) · Yellow = 50–79% (caution) · Red = <50% (emergency). Always compare to personal best. PEF is a screening tool — spirometry provides the diagnosis.
Pressure Measurements
MIP/NIF normal: -60 to -100 cmH₂O. MEP normal: 80–100+ cmH₂O. MIP ≥-20 cmH₂O in neuromuscular disease = probable need for ventilatory support. Serial monitoring guides weaning.
NBRC Must-Know List: RV cannot be measured by spirometry · Calibrate BEFORE testing · FEV₁/FVC <70% = obstruction · Normal ratio + low FVC = restriction · Poor effort ≠ disease · Nose clips prevent air leak · Peak flow zone <50% = red zone emergency · Significant bronchodilator response: ≥12% AND ≥200 mL FEV₁ improvement
