Why Professionalism Matters in Clinical Respiratory Care

Professionalism is not just etiquette — it directly affects patient safety, team function, and your career. Every clinical rotation is evaluated by preceptors, educators, and staff who make hiring recommendations, write references, and build your professional reputation.

The Continuous Job Interview
Clinical rotations are — from day one — extended job interviews. The staff RT working beside you is evaluating your attitude, communication, reliability, and judgment. Many RTs are hired directly from their clinical sites. The person you're most casual with may be the person who interviews you in 6 months.
What Professionalism Affects
Patient trust and willingness to accept care · Physician confidence in your assessments · Team cohesion and efficiency · Reference quality for future employment · Your personal reputation in the local respiratory community (which is smaller than you think)
Definition of Professionalism
Behaving in a way that makes others see you as: trustworthy, respectful, accountable, competent, and effective. Professionalism is NOT just appearance — it is your values, ethics, behavior, communication, and reliability together as a consistent package.

Core Professional Behaviors

VERY HIGH-YIELD — NBRC SCENARIO TESTED
⚖️

Accountability

Own your actions. Arrive on time. Meet deadlines. Communicate setbacks. Admit mistakes without excuse. Correct errors. Prepare for every clinical day.

🤝

Respect

Treat all people — patients, nurses, techs, housekeeping — professionally. Say please and thank you. Avoid insults, harassment, or dismissive language.

🧭

Ethics

Follow laws and codes of conduct. Protect patient privacy. Report concerns through appropriate channels. Tell the truth — especially when it's inconvenient.

💡

Integrity

Do the right thing when no one is watching. Chart accurately even when no one will check. Don't cut corners on procedures or documentation.

🔄

Reliability

Show up consistently prepared. Follow through on commitments. Be where you are supposed to be. Your team depends on you.

💬

Communication

Speak and write clearly. Ask questions rather than guessing. Give reports that are concise, complete, and relevant. Listen actively.

🌱

Positivity

Accept feedback professionally without becoming defensive. Focus on solutions. Stay resilient during difficult shifts. Motivate teammates.

🔓

Flexibility

Adapt to different personalities, patient needs, and workflow demands. Clinical environments are unpredictable. Rigid responses to change create friction.

⭐ Non-Negotiable Rule: Students should NEVER perform a skill they are not yet competent to perform without direct supervision, even if asked by staff. The professional response to "Can you do X?" when X is beyond your current competency is: "I'm not yet signed off on that — can you supervise me or perform it?" This protects the patient and demonstrates mature professional judgment.

Workplace Gossip — Why It Damages Teams

VERY HIGH-YIELD — NBRC SCENARIO TESTED

Gossip is any conversation about someone who is not present in that conversation. In clinical settings, it ranges from harmless to career-ending — and it spreads faster than any virus in a healthcare environment.

Why Negative Gossip Is Harmful
Shifts responsibility away from direct communication · Escalates into drama rapidly · Destroys trust between team members · Creates "us vs them" cliques that fracture teams · Can reach the subject directly — often distorted · In healthcare, gossip about patients is a HIPAA violation · Can end clinical rotations and careers
Five Strategies to Stop Gossip
1. Ask "What's the purpose of sharing this?" — often stops it immediately. 2. Suggest a direct solution: "Have you talked to them about it?" 3. Withdraw attention: change topic or walk away. 4. Encourage positive conversation instead. 5. Report harmful gossip (about patients, racial/gender comments) through appropriate channels.
What NOT to Do
Do not agree with gossip to avoid awkwardness. Do not add information or "top" the story. Do not spread the conversation to third parties. Do not screenshot or share information digitally. Do not assume the story is accurate — gossip is rarely a complete or unbiased account.

Patient Confidentiality & HIPAA

VERY HIGH-YIELD — VIOLATIONS HAVE LEGAL CONSEQUENCES

HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) establishes the legal standard for patient privacy. For RTs, the core principle is simple: patient health information may only be accessed by those directly involved in that patient's care, for the purpose of providing that care.

HIPAA Violations — Common Examples
• Accessing a patient's chart out of curiosity or personal interest
• Discussing patient details in public areas (elevators, cafeteria, hallways)
• Sharing patient information with family members or friends who are not authorized
• Photographing or screenshotting any patient information
• Posting patient-related content on social media (even without full name)
• Discussing patients with staff who are not directly involved in their care
Correct RT Behavior
• Discuss patient information only in private clinical areas (patient room, nursing station, conference room)
• Access only the charts of patients you are actively treating
• Give shift reports only to authorized clinical staff who are assuming care
• When in doubt: do not share
• Written notes from clinical shifts: dispose securely (shred or use designated secure disposal)
• If you witness a violation: report it through your chain of command

Cultural Diversity & Communication

Professional Expectation
RTs provide care to patients from all backgrounds — different languages, cultural practices, health beliefs, and communication styles. Professional competency includes the ability to adapt communication and remain respectful across diverse patient populations.
Practical Best Practices
Use trained medical interpreter services (not family members for complex clinical discussions). Remain patient when language barriers slow communication. Avoid speaking loudly or slowly to non-English speakers — speak normally at a measured pace with an interpreter. Maintain respect for cultural health practices even if they differ from standard care.
What Not to Do
Do not express visible frustration with language barriers — it damages trust. Do not use untrained family members as primary medical interpreters for sensitive or complex discussions. Do not assume that cultural differences represent misunderstanding or non-compliance. Do not make derogatory comments — in any language you assume the patient doesn't understand.

Professional Image & Appearance

Patients form impressions quickly. A professional appearance establishes trust before you speak your first word. It also communicates respect for the environment and the people you serve.

Apparel & Identification
Clean, unwrinkled uniform without stains or tears · Badge must be visible and worn at all times · Closed-toe, non-slip, professional footwear · Stethoscope readily available · White lab coat or facility-specific attire per policy
Grooming Standards
Hair pulled back and secured (away from patient contact areas) · Nails short and clean · Minimal makeup and jewelry · Avoid strong fragrances — many patients have respiratory sensitivity or allergies · No visible piercings or tattoos per facility policy
Technology in the Clinical Setting: Personal phone use is restricted during patient care. Earbuds and social media are inappropriate during clinical rotations. Using your phone for personal purposes while in patient care areas communicates disinterest and unprofessionalism — even if you are "just checking something quickly." Use facility devices and approved communication tools for all clinical communication.

Pulmonary Case Study Documentation

HIGH-YIELD — STRUCTURE IS NBRC TESTED

A pulmonary case study is a structured clinical narrative that captures a patient's presentation, assessment, and care plan. RTs are expected to construct and document clinical cases as part of their professional practice.

CC

Chief Complaint — ONE primary complaint in the patient's own words

HPI

History of Present Illness — chronological symptom progression around the CC

PMH

Past Medical History — relevant prior diagnoses and hospitalizations

SH

Social History — occupation, exposures, smoking, living situation

PE

Physical Exam — objective assessment findings

Labs

ABGs, CBC, electrolytes, cultures, CXR, ECG

Plan

RT care plan: goals, oxygen targets, treatments, education, escalation

Follow-Up

Re-assessment plan, response documentation, physician collaboration
Chief Complaint Rules
ONE primary complaint, not a laundry list. Written in the patient's own words or a close summary. Answers: "Why is this patient seeking care today?" Wrong: "Shortness of breath, cough, fever, COPD, on home O₂." Right: "Shortness of breath worsening over 3 days."
HPI Structure
Chronological — start at the beginning of this episode. Centered on the chief complaint. Include: onset, duration, progression, associated symptoms, triggers, alleviating factors, prior similar episodes, treatment attempts before this visit.

RT Charting & Progress Notes

VERY HIGH-YIELD — DOCUMENTATION STANDARDS

The RT's clinical note is a legal document. It is part of the patient's permanent medical record, used for billing, continuity of care, legal protection, and clinical communication.

"If you didn't chart it, you didn't do it."

Good vs Poor Documentation — Side by Side

✓ Good Documentation

SpO₂ 88% on RA, RR 28/min, accessory muscle use noted. Expiratory wheezing bilateral. Patient alert, communicating in short sentences.
Albuterol 2.5 mg SVN administered per MD order. Pre-treatment HR 94, post-treatment HR 108. Breath sounds improved — expiratory wheeze decreased bilaterally. Patient reports dyspnea improved from 8/10 to 4/10.
Patient instructed in IS technique. Achieved 1,800 mL on 3rd attempt. Patient verbalized understanding of goal and technique. To continue Q1H while awake.

✗ Poor Documentation

Patient was breathing really hard and looked uncomfortable.
Gave treatment — patient better. Will do again next time.
This patient always complains. Completed IS as ordered — same as yesterday.
Good Documentation Is:Poor Documentation Is:
Objective — measurable findings (RR, SpO₂, HR, breath sounds)Subjective — emotions, opinions, character judgments about patients
Timely — charted at or near the time of the interventionDelayed — filled in from memory hours later
Accurate — reports what actually happenedGuessed or approximated — "about 2 treatments given"
Clear and specific — exact values, device names, patient responsesVague — "treatment done; patient okay"
Relevant — clinically meaningful information onlyExcessive or irrelevant — unrelated social commentary

End-of-Shift Report

VERY HIGH-YIELD — CONTINUITY OF CARE DEPENDS ON THIS

The shift report transfers clinical responsibility from one RT to the next. Incomplete or inaccurate handoffs are a major source of patient harm in healthcare. A good report is concise, organized, and focused on current status and pending needs.

What to Include
• Patient ID and current status (stable / unstable / improving)
• Current oxygen device, settings, and targets (SpO₂ goal)
• Current ventilator settings if applicable
• Recent significant changes (increased WOB, desaturation, new order)
• Last treatment time and patient response
• Pending procedures or tests (ABG due, scheduled bronchoscopy)
• Highest-priority patients for immediate attention
Reporting Tips
• Organize by priority — sickest first
• Keep it concise — relevant clinical details only
• Use objective language (same standards as charting)
• Include pending tasks clearly
• Do not skip patients even if you think they are "fine" — your successor needs the picture
• Dispose of any written notes securely after handoff (do not take PHI out of the facility)
What NOT to Include
• Personal opinions about patients or staff
• Social or family history unrelated to current clinical status
• Gossip about clinical incidents
• Speculative diagnoses or opinions not in the chart
• Irrelevant biographical details

Shift report is clinical communication — keep it professional, objective, and relevant.

Order Interpretation Example

Order: "Duoneb SVN Q4 W/A and Q4 PRN SOB"

Interpretation:
Scheduled every 4 hrs while awake
+
Additional Q4 PRN when patient has SOB
Key Rule: "While awake" means do NOT wake a sleeping patient for a scheduled W/A treatment unless there is a specific clinical reason or additional order. If the patient is sleeping comfortably with acceptable SpO₂, skip the scheduled W/A dose and document accordingly. PRN treatments are still available if the patient wakes and has SOB.

Common NBRC Traps — Professionalism & Documentation

  • Students should not perform unsupervised skills. Performing a skill before competency is signed off — even when asked by staff — is a professionalism and patient safety failure. The correct answer is always to request supervision.
  • "If you didn't chart it, you didn't do it." Undocumented treatments and assessments do not legally or medically exist. Always chart promptly and accurately. Do not assume someone else will document it.
  • W/A does not mean wake sleeping patients. "While awake" scheduled treatments are held when the patient is sleeping comfortably with acceptable clinical status. This is a clinical judgment call — document your assessment and reasoning.
  • Chief complaint = ONE primary complaint. The CC is not a list of symptoms. It answers: "Why is the patient seeking care today?" in the most concise, focused form possible.
  • Documentation must be objective. Clinical notes contain measurable findings — SpO₂, RR, HR, breath sounds, patient-reported scores. Opinion, emotion, and characterizations of patient behavior are inappropriate in medical records.
  • Do not access charts out of curiosity or personal interest. HIPAA violations have real consequences — termination, fines, criminal charges for egregious violations. Access only the charts of patients you are directly treating.
  • Professionalism includes online and social media behavior. Posting about patients — even without full names — is a HIPAA violation and grounds for dismissal from clinical rotations and employment. Your digital behavior is your professional behavior.
  • Gossip destroys trust and team cohesion. In clinical settings, gossip is not just unprofessional — it can affect patient care when teams dysfunction. The correct response to negative gossip is to redirect, not engage.

FAQ

Common Questions

If I made a clinical mistake, do I have to report it?

Yes — always. Reporting a clinical error is the professional, ethical, and legal standard. Errors that go unreported cannot be corrected, learned from, or addressed. Most clinical environments have formal incident reporting systems specifically designed for this. The instinct to hide a mistake is understandable — but it places patients at ongoing risk and, if discovered later, is far more damaging to your professional reputation than honest, prompt reporting. The professional response to a mistake: acknowledge it, report it, correct it if possible, document accurately, and learn from it.

What counts as a HIPAA violation that I should know about?

Common HIPAA violations in student and RT contexts include: accessing a patient's chart when you are not involved in their care (even out of curiosity or concern); discussing patient information in hallways, elevators, or public spaces where it can be overheard; sharing patient information — including test results, diagnosis, or even the fact that someone is admitted — with unauthorized individuals; photographing or screenshotting any patient information; and posting anything about a patient encounter on social media, even without using the patient's full name. The test is always: did the person I'm sharing this with have a clinical care reason to receive this information? If no — it's a violation.

How do I handle an end-of-shift report when I have many patients?

Prioritize — sickest or most complex patients first. For stable patients, a brief status update is sufficient: "Room 4 is stable on 2 L NC, SpO₂ 96%, all scheduled treatments completed." For unstable or high-acuity patients, give a more detailed handoff: current oxygen device and settings, SpO₂ trend, treatment response, last vital signs, pending tasks, and any specific concerns or anticipated changes. The goal is to give the oncoming RT the information they need to care for each patient safely from the moment they take over — not to recite everything in the chart.

How do I address a coworker who is spreading gossip?

You don't have to confront it dramatically. The simplest strategies: don't engage or add to the story; redirect the conversation with a neutral topic change; or ask a simple question like "Have you talked to them directly about this?" which often redirects the conversation naturally. If the gossip involves a patient (potential HIPAA violation), involves harassment or discrimination, or represents a pattern that's affecting your team, escalate through your chain of command. You are not obligated to participate in gossip to maintain workplace harmony — professionally withdrawing is a valid and respected choice.
