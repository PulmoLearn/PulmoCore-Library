Specialty Gases — Heliox & Inhaled Nitric Oxide Equipment

Specialty gases require more than knowing the indication. The RT must choose the correct gas system, verify the delivery setup, understand correction factors and analyzers, monitor response, and troubleshoot therapy before patient safety is compromised.

Clinical Goal: Choose the Gas Based on the Problem

Heliox
Primary goal: reduce airflow resistance and work of breathing in obstructive conditions. Helium is less dense than nitrogen, so the gas mixture flows more smoothly through narrowed airways. Best fit: asthma, COPD exacerbation, croup, and upper airway obstruction when oxygen demand is low enough for the available blend.
Inhaled Nitric Oxide (iNO)
Primary goal: improve oxygenation by selectively dilating pulmonary blood vessels in ventilated lung areas. Best fit: PPHN, pulmonary hypertension, and selected hypoxic respiratory failure situations such as ARDS when ordered as rescue/supportive therapy.
Do not confuse the endpoints: Heliox is judged mainly by lower work of breathing, less turbulence, improved airflow, and improved medication delivery. iNO is judged mainly by oxygenation response, pulmonary pressure response, and safe analyzer values.

Heliox Equipment & Setup

EQUIPMENT: HELIUM/OXYGEN BLEND

Heliox is a mixture of helium and oxygen used as a supportive gas in obstructive airway conditions. It does not cure the underlying obstruction; it reduces turbulence and airway resistance while other therapies work.

Common Blends
80/20: 80% helium, 20% oxygen. Best airway-resistance benefit; only for patients who can tolerate low FiO₂.

70/30: 70% helium, 30% oxygen. Less helium benefit but provides more oxygen.
Delivery Equipment
Heliox cylinder or source · compatible regulator/flowmeter · nonrebreather or specialty mask · nebulizer setup if ordered · oxygen analyzer if available · pulse oximetry · BVM/oxygen backup.
Do Not Use When
Patient needs high FiO₂ beyond available heliox blend · severe hypoxemia requires oxygen first · delivery equipment is not heliox compatible · no ability to monitor response or rapidly switch to oxygen support.

Setup Sequence

  1. Verify order, blend, delivery device, and target flow.
  2. Confirm cylinder label and blend before connecting equipment.
  3. Attach heliox-compatible regulator/flowmeter.
  4. Connect delivery interface: tight-fitting nonrebreather or specialty mask for spontaneous breathing; heliox-compatible nebulizer if ordered.
  5. Set the prescribed flow and calculate actual delivered flow using the correction factor.
  6. Assess baseline work of breathing, SpO₂, breath sounds, HR/RR, and patient comfort.
  7. Reassess within minutes for reduced work of breathing and adequate oxygenation.

Heliox Flow Correction

Standard flowmeters are calibrated for oxygen or air, not low-density heliox. The flowmeter setting may not equal actual delivered flow. Use the correction factor assigned to the blend.

HELIOX ACTUAL FLOW
Actual Flow = Flowmeter Setting × Correction Factor
80/20 factor = 1.8 · 70/30 factor = 1.6
Example: 80/20
Flowmeter = 10 L/min
Correction factor = 1.8
Actual flow = 18 L/min
Example: 70/30
Flowmeter = 10 L/min
Correction factor = 1.6
Actual flow = 16 L/min

Inhaled Nitric Oxide Equipment & Setup

EQUIPMENT: SELECTIVE PULMONARY VASODILATOR

Inhaled nitric oxide is delivered into the ventilator or respiratory circuit through a specialized delivery system. The goal is selective pulmonary vasodilation: improving blood flow to ventilated lung regions without producing the same degree of systemic vasodilation.

Common Indications
Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN) · pulmonary hypertension with right-heart strain · selected hypoxic respiratory failure/ARDS scenarios when ordered as rescue support.
Required Equipment
NO cylinder/source · iNO delivery module · injector module in ventilator circuit · NO/NO₂ analyzer · oxygen analyzer · backup cylinder/system · ventilator circuit and humidification · alarm response plan.
Major Hazards
NO₂ formation · methemoglobinemia · abrupt interruption causing rebound pulmonary hypertension/hypoxemia · delivery-device malfunction · failure to monitor analyzers.

Setup Sequence

  1. Verify provider order: initial iNO dose, patient circuit, and monitoring requirements.
  2. Confirm correct NO cylinder/source and delivery system readiness.
  3. Place injector module in the correct circuit location per device policy/manufacturer guidance.
  4. Connect sampling line/analyzer to monitor inspired NO and NO₂.
  5. Verify oxygen analyzer and ventilator alarms.
  6. Start ordered dose and confirm measured NO matches ordered dose.
  7. Document dose, measured NO/NO₂, FiO₂, SpO₂, hemodynamics, and patient response.

iNO Monitoring & Titration

iNO is not a set-and-forget therapy. Continuous analyzer values and patient response must be checked because the delivered dose can be affected by circuit changes, ventilator flow, leaks, calibration problems, or gas-source issues.

  • NBRC trap: Heliox is not an oxygenation rescue therapy when the patient requires high FiO₂. The more oxygen in the mixture, the less helium benefit remains.
  • NBRC trap: iNO should not be stopped abruptly without a plan because rebound pulmonary hypertension and hypoxemia can occur.
  • NBRC trap: Flowmeter values for heliox require correction factors. The number on the flowmeter is not the actual delivered flow.

Specialty Gas Troubleshooting Case

Stage 1: Heliox Selection

A 9-year-old with croup has moderate stridor, increased work of breathing, and SpO₂ 96% on room air. The provider orders heliox while other therapies are being prepared. Which blend is most appropriate if oxygenation remains adequate?

Stage 2: Escalation

Thirty minutes later, the patient tires, becomes less responsive, and SpO₂ falls to 84%. The team asks whether to increase heliox flow and continue.

Stage 3: iNO Monitoring

Later in the ICU, a different patient is receiving iNO for pulmonary hypertension. During a ventilator circuit change, the iNO measured value drops unexpectedly and SpO₂ worsens.

Case Summary: Heliox is best when airway resistance is the problem and oxygen needs are low enough. iNO is best when pulmonary vascular resistance/oxygenation is the problem. In both therapies, equipment delivery failure can rapidly become patient deterioration.

FAQ

Common Questions

Why does heliox help obstructive airway problems?

Helium is less dense than nitrogen, so a helium/oxygen mixture produces less turbulent flow through narrowed airways. This can reduce airway resistance and work of breathing. It is supportive therapy while medications or procedures address the cause of obstruction.

Why is 80/20 heliox not always better?

80/20 has the greatest helium content and therefore the greatest airflow benefit, but it provides only 20% oxygen. If the patient needs more oxygen, 70/30 may be chosen or heliox may no longer be appropriate. Oxygenation always takes priority.

What is the main equipment risk with inhaled nitric oxide?

The main risks are delivery interruption, incorrect measured NO dose, NO₂ formation, analyzer/sampling-line problems, and abrupt discontinuation. The RT must verify both the ordered dose and the measured values and respond quickly to alarms.

Why should iNO not be stopped suddenly?

Abrupt interruption can cause rebound pulmonary hypertension and hypoxemia. Weaning or discontinuation should follow provider order and facility protocol, with patient monitoring and backup support ready.

Summary & Board-Prep Reminders

Heliox
Low-density helium/oxygen mixture. Use for airflow obstruction when oxygen requirement is compatible with the blend. 80/20 = more helium benefit; 70/30 = more oxygen.
iNO
Selective pulmonary vasodilator. Use for pulmonary vascular/oxygenation problems such as PPHN, pulmonary hypertension, or selected hypoxic respiratory failure.
Flow Correction
Actual heliox flow = flowmeter × factor. 80/20 factor = 1.8. 70/30 factor = 1.6.
Safety
Do not let heliox delay airway/oxygen rescue. Do not ignore iNO analyzer alarms. Do not abruptly stop iNO without a plan.
