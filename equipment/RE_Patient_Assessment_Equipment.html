Why Equipment-First Assessment?

Assessment in respiratory care is not a passive process. Every technique requires a specific tool, a specific method, and a specific set of interpretive skills. Knowing that breath sounds exist is not enough — you need to know how to hear them, what they mean, and what to do when they are abnormal.

The RT Assessment Role
RTs conduct patient assessments independently before every treatment, during every clinical visit, and at every bedside response. Your assessment findings — not the chart — drive your immediate clinical decisions.
Equipment Is the Tool; Skill Is the Point
A stethoscope in the wrong hands is useless. A pulse oximeter misread can cause harm. The goal of this lesson is not to memorize devices — it is to develop clinical competence with each one.
Assessment Drives Escalation
The most important outcome of a thorough assessment is knowing when something is wrong. Every abnormal finding covered in this lesson has a corresponding escalation pathway that a practicing RT must know by reflex.
For Every Device in This Lesson: What is it? → Why does the RT use it? → How is it set up and used correctly? → What are common mistakes? → What do abnormal findings mean? → When do you escalate?

The Physical Assessment Framework: IPPA

Physical assessment follows a systematic, ordered approach. The four techniques are always performed in the same sequence — and that sequence matters. Auscultating before percussion, for example, changes what you hear.

I
Inspection
Eyes — observe before touching. Distress, color, posture, symmetry, breathing effort.
P
Palpation
Hands — feel chest expansion, tactile fremitus, crepitus, and tenderness.
P
Percussion
Fingers — tap to assess underlying tissue density and air vs. fluid.
A
Auscultation
Stethoscope — listen to breath sounds, quality, and symmetry.
Why This Order? Percussion and palpation can alter the sounds you hear. Inspection orients you before you touch anything. Auscultation comes last so earlier findings inform what you are listening for. The sequence is not arbitrary — it is optimized for accuracy.

Use both hands to assess chest wall movement, tactile fremitus, and any crepitus or tenderness
Systematically tap fingers over the chest wall, comparing side to side, to assess tissue density
Place stethoscope directly on skin and listen to breath sounds at each lung field, comparing side to side

Inspection — Your Eyes as Clinical Equipment

EQUIPMENT: Eyes · Clinical Observation

Inspection begins the moment you enter the room — before you open your bag or touch the patient. A skilled RT extracts significant clinical information from observation alone.

What to Assess During Inspection

General Appearance & Distress
Anxious? Comfortable? Alert vs. confused? Tripod position? Unable to speak in full sentences? These are immediate signs of severity.
Skin Color
Cyanosis (blue) = hypoxemia. Pallor = anemia or shock. Flushing = fever or CO₂ retention. Assess lips, fingertips, and mucous membranes.
Chest Symmetry & Shape
Barrel chest = air trapping (COPD, emphysema). Pectus excavatum/carinatum = congenital deformity. Kyphoscoliosis = restrictive impact. Check for equal rise bilaterally.
Breathing Effort & Pattern
Accessory muscle use (SCM, trapezius, intercostals) = increased work of breathing. Nasal flaring, retractions, pursed-lip breathing. Rate and rhythm — tachypnea, bradypnea, Cheyne-Stokes.
Posture & Position
Orthopnea (must sit upright) = CHF or severe COPD. Tripod position (leaning forward on hands) = severe respiratory distress. Patient preference often reveals disease severity.
Sputum & Airway Secretions
Note amount, color, consistency. Clear/white = benign. Yellow/green = possible infection. Rust-colored = pneumonia (pneumococcal). Pink/frothy = pulmonary edema. Blood = hemoptysis — always escalate.
NBRC Principle: Inspection findings that require immediate escalation include: central cyanosis, severe accessory muscle use, inability to speak, altered mental status, and paradoxical chest wall movement (flail chest).

Palpation — Hands as Assessment Tools

EQUIPMENT: Hands and Fingers

Palpation provides information that cannot be seen or heard. It confirms chest symmetry, detects abnormal masses or crepitus, and assesses the transmission of voice vibrations through lung tissue.

Key Palpation Assessments

Chest Expansion
Place hands symmetrically on the posterior chest with thumbs at the midline. Ask patient to inhale deeply. Thumbs should move apart equally. Unequal expansion = unilateral disease (pneumothorax, consolidation, effusion, mainstem intubation).
Tactile (Vocal) Fremitus
Place palm or ulnar edge of hand on chest. Ask patient to say "99" or "blue moon." Normal: gentle vibration transmitted through lung. Increased fremitus = consolidation. Decreased/absent fremitus = pleural effusion, pneumothorax, or emphysema.
Crepitus & Tenderness
Crepitus (subcutaneous emphysema): a crackling sensation felt under skin — indicates air escaping into tissue. Often means pneumothorax or barotrauma. Chest wall tenderness: palpate for rib fractures, especially post-trauma. Escalate both immediately.

Percussion — Mapping Tissue Density

EQUIPMENT: Dominant Middle Finger · Non-dominant Middle Finger as Strike Surface

Percussion uses sound produced by tapping to assess what lies beneath the chest wall. The sound quality reveals whether the underlying tissue contains air, fluid, or solid material.

Proper Percussion Technique

  1. Place the non-dominant middle finger flat against the chest wall (hyperextend it)
  2. Strike the distal interphalangeal joint (first joint below fingertip) with the dominant middle fingertip
  3. Use a quick, sharp wrist-flick — the strike, not a sustained press
  4. Move systematically from apex to base, comparing left and right at each level
  5. Percuss at least 4–6 levels bilaterally: posterior, lateral, and anterior
Comparison Is Everything: Always compare the same anatomical level on the left vs. right. A dull note on the right where the left is resonant is the finding — not the dull note alone.

Percussion Notes & Their Meanings

Resonance

Hollow, drum-like. Normal lung tissue. Low-pitched, moderate duration.

Hyperresonance

Loud, booming. Air-trapping (emphysema) or pneumothorax. Lower pitch, longer duration than resonance.

Dullness

Thud-like, muffled. Fluid (pleural effusion) or solid tissue (consolidation, tumor).

Flatness

Extremely muffled, no resonance. Massive atelectasis, dense consolidation, or large effusion.

The Stethoscope

HIGH-YIELD DEVICE
Purpose
Amplifies body sounds — breath sounds, heart sounds, and bowel sounds. The RT's most-used assessment tool. Should be on your person at all times in the lab and clinical setting.
Key Components
Diaphragm (flat side): high-pitched sounds — breath sounds, normal heart sounds.
Bell (concave side): low-pitched sounds — heart murmurs, certain adventitious sounds.
Tubing, binaurals, earpieces.
Infection Control
Clean the diaphragm and bell with an alcohol wipe between every patient. Stethoscopes are a known vector for cross-contamination, especially with MRSA. Never share without disinfecting first.

Proper Auscultation Technique

  1. Clean the diaphragm with an alcohol wipe before use
  2. Angle earpieces forward (toward your nose) — backward earpieces produce silence
  3. Use the diaphragm for lung sounds — press firmly but not painfully against bare skin
  4. Place directly on skin, never over clothing — clothing creates artifact noise
  5. Listen at each lung field systematically: apex, mid-lung, base — bilateral comparison at each level
  6. Ask patient to breathe slowly and deeply through the mouth during auscultation
  7. Note quality (clear, diminished, absent), pattern (equal bilaterally?), and any adventitious sounds

Cannot hear any soundsEarpieces angled backwardRotate earpieces forward (toward face)
Sounds muffled or weakStethoscope over clothing; poor sealPlace directly on skin; reposition earpieces
Crackling/rustling artifactTubing rubbing against gown, bed rail, or fingersHold tubing still; keep away from surfaces

Breath Sound Reference

Vesicular (Normal)

Soft, low-pitched, heard over peripheral lung fields. Inspiration > expiration. The baseline — any deviation is abnormal.

Bronchial (Normal over trachea)

Loud, high-pitched, hollow sound. Normal over trachea only. If heard over peripheral lung, indicates consolidation.

Bronchovesicular (Normal)

Combination of bronchial and vesicular. Normal over main bronchi (sternal angle, between scapulae). Equal I:E ratio.

Crackles (Rales)

Discontinuous, popping sounds. Fine = alveolar (CHF, fibrosis). Coarse = large airway secretions. Early inspiration = distal; late inspiration = alveoli.

Rhonchi

Coarse, low-pitched, continuous sounds from secretions in large airways. Often clears with coughing. Associated with COPD, bronchitis.

Wheeze

High-pitched, continuous, musical sound. Usually expiratory. Indicates airway narrowing — asthma, COPD, bronchospasm.

Stridor

Loud, high-pitched, monophonic inspiratory sound. Audible WITHOUT a stethoscope. Indicates upper airway obstruction — emergent finding.

Pleural Friction Rub

Coarse, scratching/grating sound on both inspiration and expiration. Inflamed pleural surfaces. Associated with pleuritis, PE.

Absent / Diminished

No breath sounds where they should be. Indicates severe obstruction, pneumothorax, large effusion, or mainstem intubation. Always escalate.

Clinical Check: Silent Chest

An RT is auscultating a patient in the emergency department with a known history of severe asthma who is in obvious respiratory distress. Despite widespread expiratory wheezing on arrival, the RT now hears no breath sounds at all bilaterally. What does this finding indicate, and what is the immediate action?

Pulse Oximetry

HIGH-YIELD DEVICE
What It Measures
SpO₂ — the percentage of hemoglobin saturated with oxygen, measured noninvasively. It also displays pulse rate and a waveform (plethysmograph) reflecting peripheral perfusion quality.
Probe Types
Finger probe: most common; standard for most patients. Forehead probe: better during hypoperfusion (vasoconstriction). Ear probe: used when fingers are unavailable or when faster response is needed.
Normal Values
SpO₂ ≥95% = normal in most adults. SpO₂ 90–94% = may require supplemental O₂. SpO₂ <90% = hypoxemia requiring intervention. SpO₂ <88% = significant hypoxemia requiring escalation.

Proper Setup and Use

  1. Select the appropriate probe type for the patient and clinical situation
  2. Ensure the probe site is clean — remove nail polish if present; assess skin perfusion
  3. Apply probe correctly: finger probe aligns with the LED/detector on opposite sides of the fingertip
  4. Verify waveform quality — a clean, regular plethysmographic waveform indicates reliable perfusion signal
  5. Wait for a stable reading (typically 5–10 seconds) before recording
  6. Correlate clinically: does the SpO₂ match the patient's appearance and symptoms?

Limitations — Critical for NBRC

Carbon Monoxide Poisoning
The most dangerous limitation. Pulse oximetry CANNOT distinguish carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) from oxyhemoglobin. A CO-poisoned patient may show SpO₂ 98–100% while actually being severely hypoxic. Always obtain ABG with CO-oximetry when CO exposure is suspected.
Dyshemoglobinemias
Methemoglobinemia causes SpO₂ to read approximately 85% regardless of true O₂ saturation. Sickle cell and other hemoglobin variants may also affect accuracy. Confirm with ABG when clinical picture doesn't match SpO₂.
Poor Peripheral Perfusion
Cold extremities, shock, vasoconstriction, and peripheral vascular disease reduce the signal. A weak or absent waveform = unreliable reading. Warm the hand, try a different probe site, or use forehead/ear probe.
Motion Artifact & Other Factors
Patient movement creates false waveforms. Bright ambient light (especially fluorescent) can interfere. Nail polish (especially dark colors) absorbs light and reduces accuracy. Remove polish or use the side of the finger.

Blood Pressure Assessment

EQUIPMENT: Sphygmomanometer · Stethoscope · Blood Pressure Cuff
Purpose in RT Practice
BP provides hemodynamic context for respiratory interventions. Hypotension during NIV, HFNC, or MV can indicate hemodynamic compromise. Hypertension may worsen pulmonary edema. The RT must know how to obtain and interpret BP accurately.
Manual vs. Automatic
Manual (auscultatory): RT listens for Korotkoff sounds with stethoscope. More accurate in arrhythmias and hypoperfusion states. Automatic (oscillometric): Convenient but inaccurate in irregular rhythms, movement, and certain low-flow states. Always confirm manual when automatic seems wrong.
Cuff Sizing — Critical
The bladder should encircle 80% of the arm circumference. Too-small cuff = falsely elevated reading. Too-large cuff = falsely lowered reading. Pediatric, standard adult, large adult, and thigh cuffs are available. Always select the correct size first.

Manual BP Procedure (Competency-Aligned)

  1. Position patient seated or supine with arm at heart level, palm up, supported
  2. Select correct cuff size — check that bladder covers 80% of arm circumference
  3. Wrap cuff snugly 2–3 cm above the antecubital fossa; cuff should fit two fingers underneath
  4. Locate brachial artery by palpation
  5. Place stethoscope diaphragm over brachial artery (not under cuff)
  6. Inflate cuff 20–30 mmHg above expected systolic pressure (or until pulse disappears)
  7. Deflate slowly at 2–3 mmHg per second
  8. Record first Korotkoff sound as systolic; muffling or disappearance as diastolic

Falsely high readingCuff too small for arm; deflating too quickly; patient talking or tenseReplace with larger cuff; repeat; ensure patient at rest
Falsely low readingCuff too large; arm above heart level; deflating too slowlyUse correct cuff size; reposition arm

Escalation Triggers: Systolic <90 mmHg (hypotension — possible shock); Systolic >180 or diastolic >120 (hypertensive crisis); any BP with concurrent respiratory distress, altered mental status, or diaphoresis.

Temperature Assessment

EQUIPMENT: Thermometer · Probe Cover
Oral
Most common. Place under tongue. Patient must be NPO for 15–20 min beforehand. Normal: 36.5–37.5°C (97.7–99.5°F).
Tympanic
Fast, convenient. Gently pull auricle upward and back (adults). Insert probe snugly. Affected by cerumen and technique.
Temporal Artery
Scan across forehead. Non-contact or soft contact. Widely used in inpatient settings. Accurate when technique is correct.
Axillary
Least accurate — approximately 0.5°C below core temperature. Use only when other routes are contraindicated. Hold arm firmly against body for full duration.

RT Clinical Significance

Temperature provides critical context for respiratory conditions. Fever drives increased oxygen demand and may worsen hypoxemia. Hypothermia depresses respiratory drive and can lead to bradypnea and CO₂ retention.

Fever (>38°C / 100.4°F)
Increases metabolic rate and O₂ demand. Suggests infection, inflammatory process, or drug reaction. Assess for sepsis in the RT patient with new hypoxemia + fever.
Hypothermia (<35°C / 95°F)
Depresses respiratory drive. May cause bradypnea, CO₂ retention. Masks sepsis (elderly patients can be septic without fever). Escalate.
Sepsis Screening
RTs should recognize the SIRS criteria: HR >90, RR >20, Temp >38 or <36, WBC abnormal. A patient meeting these criteria with an identified infection source needs urgent evaluation.

Pulse Assessment

EQUIPMENT: Index and Middle Fingers · Watch / Timer

Radial Pulse — Standard Technique

  1. Use index and middle fingers — never the thumb (thumb has its own pulse)
  2. Place fingers over the radial artery at the wrist (lateral aspect, just medial to the radial styloid)
  3. Apply gentle but firm pressure until the pulse is felt
  4. Count beats for a full 60 seconds if the rhythm is irregular; 30 seconds × 2 if regular; 15 seconds × 4 only in stable, regular patients
  5. Assess simultaneously: rate, rhythm, and strength (amplitude)
Rate
Normal: 60–100 bpm. Tachycardia (>100) = hypoxemia, fever, anxiety, bronchodilator overdose, pain. Bradycardia (<60) = vagal response, beta-blockers, severe hypoxia.
Rhythm
Regular vs. irregular. Regularly irregular (e.g., PVCs) vs. irregularly irregular (atrial fibrillation). An irregular pulse warrants cardiac monitoring and provider notification.
Strength (Amplitude)
Strong and bounding = fever, hyperdynamic states. Weak and thready = low cardiac output, shock, severe hypotension. Pulsus paradoxus (drops >10 mmHg on inspiration) = severe asthma, cardiac tamponade.
NBRC Trap: Counting a pulse for only 15 seconds in a patient with an irregular rhythm produces a significantly inaccurate rate. Always count for a full 60 seconds when rhythm is irregular.

Respiratory Rate Assessment

EQUIPMENT: Eyes · Watch / Timer

Respiratory rate is the vital sign most frequently measured inaccurately — and one of the most sensitive early indicators of clinical deterioration. An abnormal RR detected before SpO₂ drops can prevent a respiratory emergency.

Proper RR Assessment Technique

  1. Observe secretly when possible — patients who know their breathing is being watched will consciously alter their rate
  2. Continue holding the wrist after pulse assessment as a natural transition (patient thinks you are still counting the pulse)
  3. Count one full respiratory cycle (rise AND fall = 1 breath)
  4. Count for a full 60 seconds in unstable patients or when rate is irregular; 30 seconds × 2 minimum
  5. Observe breathing effort simultaneously: depth, use of accessory muscles, nasal flaring, pursed-lip breathing
Normal Adult
12–20 breaths/min at rest. Varies with age (children higher).
Tachypnea
>20 breaths/min. Early sign of hypoxemia, fever, sepsis, anxiety, acidosis, PE.
Bradypnea
<12 breaths/min. Opioid overdose, CNS depression, severe fatigue. Can lead to CO₂ retention and respiratory failure.
Apnea
Absence of breathing for >20 seconds. Requires immediate intervention — airway support and provider notification.
NBRC Trap: Counting RR for only 15 seconds in an unstable patient is a documented source of clinical error — especially with irregular breathing patterns. In a patient with Cheyne-Stokes respirations or irregular patterns, 15-second counts can be off by more than 50%. The NBRC expects 60-second counts for clinical accuracy in unstable patients.

Escalation — When Assessment Findings Demand Action

Assessment is only valuable if you act on what you find. These findings require immediate escalation — document as you notify, not before.

Immediate Escalation Triggers

  • SpO₂ <90% persistent despite repositioning probe — apply supplemental O₂ and notify provider immediately
  • Silent chest in an acute asthma exacerbation — emergent intervention, possible intubation
  • Stridor — upper airway obstruction; have airway equipment at bedside; notify immediately
  • Absent unilateral breath sounds + hyperresonance — pneumothorax until proven otherwise
  • Tracheal deviation — tension pneumothorax; emergent needle decompression may be needed
  • Crepitus (subcutaneous emphysema) — pneumothorax or barotrauma; escalate immediately
  • Central cyanosis — severe hypoxemia; airway and oxygenation support
  • Altered mental status + any respiratory abnormality — possible respiratory failure or CO₂ narcosis
  • Systolic BP <90 mmHg + respiratory distress — possible cardiogenic shock or tension pneumothorax
  • RR >30 breaths/min with distress — impending respiratory failure; prepare for escalation to NIV or intubation
Escalation Rule: When in doubt, notify first — then continue assessing. A brief call to the nurse or physician with "I found X and I'm concerned about Y — please come evaluate" is never wrong. Waiting for certainty can cost minutes you do not have.

Common NBRC Assessment Traps

  • Accepting a normal SpO₂ as proof of adequate oxygenation in a smoke-inhalation patient. CO poisoning produces normal SpO₂ on standard pulse oximetry. Always obtain ABG with CO-oximetry when CO exposure is suspected.
  • Interpreting bilateral wheezing that suddenly disappears as improvement. A silent chest in acute asthma is a life-threatening emergency — not a sign of relief. The wheezing stopped because airflow is insufficient to generate sound.
  • Counting respiratory rate for 15 seconds in an unstable patient. In irregular breathing patterns (Cheyne-Stokes, Biot's) or tachypneic patients, 15-second counts introduce significant error. Count for 60 seconds.
  • Using a too-small BP cuff and treating the artificially elevated result. Cuff too small = falsely high BP. Always select cuff by arm circumference before recording or acting on the reading.
  • Assuming unilateral absent breath sounds means equipment failure. Absent sounds unilaterally, especially with hyperresonance, suggest pneumothorax or mainstem intubation. Investigate clinically — do not just reposition the stethoscope.
  • Placing the stethoscope over clothing during auscultation. Clothing produces artifact that can completely mask real sounds and create false sounds. Always auscultate on bare skin.
  • Using the thumb to assess a pulse. The thumb has its own pulsation from the princeps pollicis artery. Use index and middle fingers only for pulse assessment.

FAQ

Common Questions

Why does the stethoscope need to be placed directly on skin?

Clothing creates friction artifact — a rustling noise that can mask true breath sounds and create false sounds that mimic crackles. A stethoscope placed over a hospital gown produces unreliable, sometimes completely uninterpretable auscultation. Always lift or open the gown and auscultate on bare skin. If a patient cannot be repositioned, at minimum open the gown anteriorly and posteriorly at each assessment site.

When should I trust the pulse oximeter and when should I not?

Trust the pulse ox when: the waveform is strong and regular, the clinical picture matches the reading, and there is no reason to suspect a dyshemoglobinemia. Do NOT trust the pulse ox when: the clinical picture contradicts the reading (patient looks cyanotic but reads 96%), carbon monoxide or methemoglobinemia is possible, the waveform is weak or irregular (poor perfusion), or the probe cannot be properly placed. When in doubt, obtain an ABG with co-oximetry — it is the gold standard.

Does percussion actually change what I do clinically?

Yes — particularly for differentiating pneumothorax from consolidation/effusion at the bedside before imaging. In an emergent situation (patient deteriorating rapidly), percussion takes 30 seconds and can give you enough information to escalate. On the NBRC, percussion findings are heavily tested in clinical scenario questions precisely because they differentiate conditions that look similar on inspection and auscultation. Hyperresonance + absent sounds = pneumothorax (not effusion). Dullness + absent sounds = effusion or consolidation (not pneumothorax). That distinction matters.

Why can't I use my thumb to check a pulse?

The thumb has its own arterial pulsation from the princeps pollicis artery — a branch of the radial artery. When you press your thumb against a patient's radial artery, you may feel your own thumb pulsation instead of the patient's pulse, producing a falsely present or misleadingly different rate. This is a real clinical error that has been documented in practice. Use the index and middle finger tips, which are more sensitive and do not have this interference.

How do I count respiratory rate without the patient noticing?

The most effective technique is to continue holding the patient's wrist after you finish counting their pulse, as if you are still counting. The patient believes you are still counting their heart rate. With your eyes on their chest (without staring obviously), count respiratory cycles for 30–60 seconds. Alternatively, you can observe chest movement while reviewing the patient's chart. Patients who know their breathing is being watched consistently alter their rate — the clinical finding becomes the watched rate, not the resting rate.

What is tactile fremitus and why does it matter?

Tactile (vocal) fremitus is the vibration felt on the chest wall when the patient speaks. When a patient says "99" or "blue moon," the vibration travels through the lung tissue and is transmitted through the chest wall. In normal lung tissue, you feel a gentle buzz. In consolidated lung (pneumonia), the vibration increases — solid tissue transmits sound better. In pleural effusion or pneumothorax, the vibration decreases or disappears — fluid and air block transmission. It provides the same information as egophony on auscultation, but is assessed by touch rather than sound.

Summary & High-Yield Reminders

Assessment is the starting point of every RT intervention. The equipment you carry and the techniques you master determine whether you catch problems early or late. The following are the highest-yield points from this lesson:

IPPA — Always in Order
Inspect → Palpate → Percuss → Auscultate. Never skip steps. The sequence protects the validity of your findings.
Stethoscope on Bare Skin
Forward earpieces. Diaphragm for lung sounds. Bilateral comparison at every level. Silent chest = emergency.
Pulse Ox Has Limits
CO poisoning produces falsely normal SpO₂. Clinical assessment always overrides the monitor. Weak waveform = unreliable reading.
Cuff Size Is Not Optional
Too small = falsely high. Too large = falsely low. Always size before recording. Incorrect BP readings drive incorrect treatment decisions.
