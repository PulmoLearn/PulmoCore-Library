Infection Control, PPE & Isolation Precautions

Learn how respiratory pathogens spread, how standard and transmission-based precautions interrupt that spread, how to select and remove PPE safely, and how to manage respiratory procedures and equipment without creating a new route of exposure.

Before you begin

Respiratory therapists work directly with coughing patients, artificial airways, nebulizers, suction systems, ventilator circuits, condensate, and respiratory secretions. That makes infection prevention an equipment skill as well as a patient-care skill.

For the 2027 Respiratory Therapy Examination (RTE), Breadth of Knowledge includes Standard Precautions, contact/droplet/airborne isolation, hand hygiene, PPE, disinfection, biohazard handling, and safe respiratory-equipment practices. Depth of Clinical Judgment means recognizing the likely transmission risk, acting before exposure occurs, and deciding when a contamination event or isolation failure requires immediate correction or escalation.

Big idea: Follow the chain source → route of transmission → susceptible host → precaution → PPE/equipment control → procedure → contamination check → correction → communication.

Core vocabulary: chain of infection, pathogen, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry, susceptible host, Standard Precautions, contact precautions, droplet precautions, airborne precautions, personal protective equipment (PPE), gown, gloves, eye protection, surgical mask, N95 respirator, hand hygiene, donning, doffing, aerosol-generating procedure (AGP), contamination, disinfection, biohazard, single-patient-use equipment, ventilator condensate.

Infection prevention works by breaking a link

The chain of infection includes an infectious organism, a reservoir, a portal of exit, a mode of transmission, a portal of entry, and a susceptible host. Respiratory-care procedures can affect several links at once because secretions leave the airway, contaminate hands/equipment, and may be dispersed into the environment.

Respiratory-care examples

  • Hand hygiene interrupts transmission from contaminated hands to another patient or device.
  • Cleaning/disinfection interrupts transmission through reusable respiratory equipment.
  • Appropriate masks/respirators reduce exposure to respiratory droplets or airborne particles.
  • Safe secretion and biohazard handling prevents body-fluid contamination.
  • Keeping contaminated condensate away from the patient's airway reduces the chance of inoculating the respiratory tract.
Clinical reasoning: Do not memorize infection-control actions as isolated rules. Ask which source or route each action is designed to interrupt.

Standard Precautions apply to every patient

Standard Precautions assume that blood, body fluids, respiratory secretions, mucous membranes, and contaminated equipment may transmit infection. Hand hygiene and exposure-based PPE are therefore used even when no specific isolation diagnosis is known.

The highest contamination risk often occurs during removal

PPE protects only when it is used and removed without transferring organisms from the contaminated surface to clothing, skin, eyes, mouth, equipment, or the environment.

Donning sequence taught in the course

  1. Hand hygiene.
  2. Gown.
  3. Mask or respirator.
  4. Eye protection/face shield when indicated.
  5. Gloves over the gown cuffs.

Doffing principle

Remove the most contaminated items without touching exposed skin or clean clothing, perform hand hygiene at the appropriate points, and avoid touching the front surfaces of masks, respirators, eye protection, or gowns unnecessarily. The course's doffing sequence emphasizes controlling contaminated gloves and gown first and cleaning hands before touching the face area.

Soap and water versus alcohol-based hand rub

Use the hand-hygiene method taught for the situation and facility policy. The course activity distinguishes occasions when visible contamination requires washing from routine decontamination that can be performed with an alcohol-based hand rub when appropriate.

Common mistake: Wearing gloves and then treating the gloved hands as clean. Gloves become contaminated surfaces and can spread organisms to ventilators, keyboards, doors, phones, and other patients.
Putting C3 together
Use hand hygiene before and after PPE, don from clean body protection toward gloves, remove contaminated items without contacting clean areas, and treat every used PPE surface as potentially contaminated.

Respiratory procedures can change the exposure risk

Airway suctioning, airway manipulation, aerosol therapy, manual ventilation, intubation-related care, and other procedures can place the therapist close to respiratory secretions and potentially increase environmental dispersion. The exact precautions follow the organism, procedure, and facility policy, but the planning principle is universal: identify the exposure before starting.

Procedure planning

  • Confirm the required isolation category.
  • Choose respiratory/eye/body protection appropriate to the exposure.
  • Bring only necessary equipment into the isolation area.
  • Plan how reusable equipment will be disinfected or contained afterward.
  • Minimize unnecessary circuit breaks and uncontrolled secretion exposure.
  • Perform hand hygiene and safe PPE removal after the procedure.
Clinical reasoning: PPE selection should account for both the suspected pathogen and what you are about to do at the bedside. A low-exposure interaction and airway instrumentation are not the same occupational risk.
Putting C4 together
Anticipate secretion and aerosol exposure, use the isolation/PPE level required by the risk, minimize unnecessary respiratory-system disruption, and control contaminated equipment after the procedure.

Treat contamination as a clinical problem

Common respiratory-care failures include taking contaminated equipment from one patient to another, touching clean equipment with contaminated gloves, using the wrong isolation PPE, allowing secretions or condensate to contaminate surfaces, and continuing a procedure after PPE integrity fails.

Correct in the right order

  1. Stop the contamination/exposure pathway.
  2. Protect the patient and staff member from further exposure.
  3. Remove or contain the contaminated equipment/PPE.
  4. Perform hand hygiene and replace PPE as needed.
  5. Disinfect or replace equipment according to policy.
  6. Notify/escalate the safety or isolation issue when required.

Ventilator condensate example

Condensate can contain microorganisms and respiratory secretions. Drain it away from the patient into the designated collection system using appropriate precautions. Do not allow contaminated liquid to flow toward the artificial airway or back into a humidifier reservoir.

Safety: If a PPE breach or unexpected secretion exposure occurs during patient care, correct the exposure immediately. Do not finish the task first and address contamination later.
Putting C5 together
Recognize the contaminated surface or failed barrier, interrupt spread immediately, replace/disinfect the affected equipment, and communicate the breach when additional action is required.

Education prevents the next contamination event

Patients and caregivers may handle cannulas, masks, nebulizers, humidification equipment, tracheostomy supplies, and other respiratory devices. Teaching should connect the action with the reason so the behavior is more likely to continue at home.

  • Perform hand hygiene before handling respiratory equipment.
  • Do not share single-patient respiratory devices.
  • Follow the cleaning/disinfection process taught for reusable home equipment.
  • Keep clean supplies separated from used/contaminated supplies.
  • Use cough/respiratory hygiene and follow isolation instructions when applicable.
  • Recognize when equipment is visibly contaminated, damaged, or cannot be safely cleaned and should be replaced.

Use teach-back

Instead of asking “Do you understand?” have the patient or caregiver show how they would clean the device, remove PPE, or separate clean from contaminated equipment. Demonstration reveals misunderstandings before they become home-care infection risks.

Putting C6 together
Teach the specific infection risk created by the patient's respiratory equipment and verify understanding through demonstration or teach-back rather than relying on verbal agreement alone.

Infection control: interrupt transmission before it reaches the next person

Respiratory equipment can support breathing or become a transmission pathway. The therapist must understand the suspected organism, route of spread, PPE and isolation requirements, procedure-related exposure risk, and how contaminated equipment will be handled after care.

source → route → precaution → PPE → procedure/equipment → contamination check → correction → communication

RTE Breadth of Knowledge: before moving on, you should be able to...

  • explain the chain of infection and identify infection-control actions that interrupt transmission;
  • apply Standard Precautions to respiratory secretions, body fluids, and contaminated respiratory equipment;
  • differentiate contact, droplet, and airborne transmission;
  • associate influenza/flu with droplet precautions and suspected pulmonary tuberculosis with airborne precautions as taught in the course;
  • select gown, gloves, surgical/procedure mask, eye protection, or N95 respirator according to the exposure and isolation risk;
  • perform the course donning sequence and remove PPE without contaminating skin or clean clothing;
  • use hand hygiene before and after PPE and recognize when visibly contaminated hands require washing;
  • recognize respiratory procedures that increase secretion/aerosol exposure and plan PPE before starting;
  • distinguish clean, single-patient-use, reusable/disinfected, contaminated, and biohazard respiratory equipment;
  • manage ventilator condensate away from the patient;
  • recognize PPE breaches, isolation failures, contaminated equipment, and unsafe secretion handling; and
  • teach patients/families respiratory-equipment hygiene and verify understanding with teach-back.

RTE Depth of Clinical Judgment: take the next step

  • Information Gathering: determine the suspected infection, symptoms, transmission route, planned respiratory procedure, secretion exposure, current isolation status, and equipment that will enter the room.
  • Information Gathering: identify what has already been contaminated after an unexpected exposure or PPE failure.
  • Decision Making: initiate airborne precautions for credible suspected pulmonary tuberculosis rather than waiting for final laboratory confirmation.
  • Decision Making: select droplet precautions for the flu pattern taught in the course and add PPE based on expected secretion/body-fluid exposure.
  • Decision Making: stop and correct a PPE or contamination breach immediately rather than completing the respiratory procedure first.
  • Decision Making: remove/disinfect contaminated respiratory equipment before it can become a transmission source for another patient.
  • Decision Making: manage contaminated ventilator condensate away from the artificial airway and designated clean equipment.
  • Reassessment: verify that isolation, replacement PPE, environmental controls, and equipment processing now match the actual risk.
  • Communication: escalate unresolved isolation or exposure concerns and clearly communicate the suspected transmission risk to the care team.
  • Education: adapt infection-control teaching to the respiratory equipment the patient or caregiver will handle independently.
National Board for Respiratory Care connection: The Respiratory Therapy Examination expects you to apply infection-prevention and control policies, use appropriate PPE and isolation, disinfect respiratory equipment, handle biohazardous material safely, recognize and correct exposure risks, and educate patients and families about respiratory-care safety.
