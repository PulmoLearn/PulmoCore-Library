Respiratory Equipment • Competency Guide

PFT, Spirometry, Peak Flow & Respiratory Pressure Devices

Learn how pulmonary function equipment measures flow, volume, lung size, and respiratory-muscle strength; how to coach technically valid maneuvers; and how to distinguish true physiologic abnormality from poor effort or equipment error.

Before you begin

Pulmonary function testing (PFT) is only as good as the equipment, calibration, coaching, and patient effort used to obtain it. A low forced vital capacity (FVC) can reflect true restriction—or a patient who stopped exhaling too early. A low peak flow can represent severe asthma—or an incomplete inhalation before the maneuver. Before interpreting disease, respiratory therapists must first decide whether the test is valid.

The 2027 Respiratory Therapy Examination (RTE) specifically includes performance and evaluation of spirometry, lung volumes, diffusing capacity, peak flow, and respiratory-muscle strength testing, as well as troubleshooting portable spirometers and pulmonary-function laboratory equipment.

Big idea: Follow the chain clinical question → correct test → calibrated equipment → coached maneuver → quality check → pattern → physiologic meaning → next test/action.

Core vocabulary: pulmonary function testing (PFT), spirometry, forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁), FEV₁/FVC ratio, peak expiratory flow (PEF), residual volume (RV), functional residual capacity (FRC), total lung capacity (TLC), body plethysmography, gas dilution, helium dilution, nitrogen washout, pneumotachograph, flow-volume loop, obstruction, restriction, calibration, reproducibility, maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP), negative inspiratory force (NIF), maximum expiratory pressure (MEP), maximum voluntary ventilation (MVV).

Flow sensors

A pneumotachograph measures flow from a pressure difference across a known resistance. The lesson describes Fleisch capillary-tube, Lilly/screen, and variable-orifice designs. It also introduces ultrasonic/vortex flow sensing, which uses signal transit changes and has no moving parts.

Residual volume is the key equipment distinction

Residual volume (RV) cannot be measured by spirometry because it is the air remaining after maximal exhalation. The lesson requires plethysmography or gas dilution to measure lung volumes that contain RV, including FRC and TLC.

Why the body box can outperform gas dilution in severe obstruction

Body plethysmography measures all compressible gas in the thorax, including trapped gas. Helium dilution and nitrogen washout depend on gas communicating with ventilated airways. In severe COPD/emphysema, trapped regions may not equilibrate, so dilution techniques can underestimate lung volume.

RTE trap: Do not choose repeat bedside spirometry when the clinical question is “How much trapped gas or RV does this patient have?” Spirometry cannot answer that question.

Spirometry is effort dependent: coaching is part of the test

The lesson describes FVC coaching as a high-yield RT competency. A calibrated spirometer cannot produce valid results when the patient does not inhale completely, delays the initial blast, leaks around the mouthpiece, coughs, or stops exhaling too soon.

FVC maneuver sequence

  1. Apply nose clips and ensure the patient is positioned safely.
  2. Have the patient inhale as deeply and completely as possible.
  3. Seal the lips tightly around the mouthpiece.
  4. Coach the patient to blast out immediately—hard and fast from the first instant.
  5. Continue active coaching: “Keep going! Keep blowing!” until the effort is complete.
  6. The lesson requires at least 6 seconds of exhalation or until a volume plateau of <25 mL over 1 second.
  7. Repeat acceptable maneuvers and evaluate reproducibility.

Calibration before patient testing

The lesson emphasizes a 3-L calibration syringe. Calibration must occur before patient testing. A calibration performed afterward cannot retroactively validate results that were already collected.

The course troubleshooting table identifies calibration outside ±3.5% as a problem requiring connection/leak checks and repeat calibration; remove the device from service if it continues to fail.

Peak flow measures speed, not total exhaled volume

Peak expiratory flow (PEF) is the maximum flow achieved during a short, forceful expiration. It is useful for asthma monitoring, emergency assessment, bronchodilator response, and tracking day-to-day variation. It is not a replacement for diagnostic spirometry.

Correct technique

  1. Reset the marker.
  2. Stand upright or sit erect.
  3. Take the largest breath possible.
  4. Seal the lips around the mouthpiece.
  5. Blast out as hard and fast as possible in one short effort.
  6. Repeat three times.
  7. Record the best/highest acceptable value.

Personal-best zone formula

% of personal best = Current PEF ÷ Personal Best PEF × 100
Green80–100%Course interpretation: lungs functioning well; continue regular plan
Yellow50–79%Caution; poor control; rescue therapy/provider contact per plan
Red<50%Emergency/severe obstruction; bronchodilator and emergency evaluation in lesson scenario

Example: 215 L/min ÷ personal best 500 L/min × 100 = 43%, which is in the red zone.

Common mistake: Comparing a peak flow only with a population-predicted value when the patient has an established personal best. The course zone system uses the patient's own best as the reference.

Lung volumes answer what spirometry cannot

Spirometry can measure air that moves through the mouth. It cannot measure the air that remains after maximal exhalation. Therefore RV cannot be measured by spirometry, and capacities containing RV—especially FRC and TLC—require another technique.

Body plethysmography

The patient sits in a sealed chamber and pants against a briefly closed shutter. Pressure changes in the box and at the mouth are used with Boyle's law to calculate lung volume. The lesson calls the body box the gold standard for comprehensive lung-volume measurement because it includes trapped gas.

Gas dilution

Helium dilution uses equilibration of a known helium concentration in a closed circuit. Nitrogen washout uses 100% oxygen to wash nitrogen from communicating lung units. Both can underestimate lung volume when severe obstruction traps gas behind poorly ventilated airways.

MIP/NIF: inspiratory muscle strength

Maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP), also called negative inspiratory force (NIF), is the most negative pressure a patient can generate against an occluded airway. The lesson gives a normal range of approximately −60 to −100 cmH₂O and identifies values less negative than about −20 to −25 cmH₂O as concerning for inability to sustain spontaneous ventilation or protect the airway.

  1. Apply mouthpiece and nose clips.
  2. Have the patient exhale to the instructed low lung volume.
  3. Occlude the airway using the device.
  4. Coach a maximal inspiratory effort sustained for at least 1 second.
  5. Repeat for reproducibility and record the best/most negative pressure.

MEP: expiratory muscle/cough strength

Maximum expiratory pressure (MEP) is the greatest positive pressure generated during a maximal expiratory effort against an occluded airway. The lesson gives approximately 80–100+ cmH₂O as a typical normal range and links a low MEP with weak cough, secretion retention, and atelectasis risk.

Clinical reasoning: Severe emphysema with known obstruction plus a question about air trapping requires lung-volume testing—preferably plethysmography in the lesson—not another peak-flow reading.
Putting C4 together
Use lung-volume equipment when the unanswered question involves RV, TLC, FRC, trapped gas, or confirmation of restriction. Use pressure measurements when the question is respiratory-muscle strength or ability to sustain ventilation/cough effectively.

Quality before pattern, pattern before diagnosis

Key spirometry values from the lesson

  • FVC: total volume expired during a maximal forced exhalation.
  • FEV₁: volume expired during the first second.
  • FEV₁/FVC: proportion of the FVC exhaled in the first second; the lesson's obstruction indicator.
  • PEF: highest expiratory flow achieved.

Obstructive pattern

The lesson teaches FEV₁/FVC <0.70 as the key obstructive pattern. FEV₁ falls disproportionately because narrowed/collapsible airways limit how quickly air can leave. The expiratory flow-volume loop develops a concave or “scooped” appearance. Course examples include COPD, asthma, bronchiectasis, and cystic fibrosis.

Restrictive pattern requires lung-volume confirmation

A normal or elevated FEV₁/FVC ratio with a reduced FVC can suggest restriction when the maneuver is valid, but the source lesson also pairs true restriction with a reduced TLC. The flow-volume loop is smaller but maintains a more normal shape. This distinction protects students from diagnosing restriction from low FVC alone.

When to escalate beyond bedside spirometry

  • Unexplained dyspnea with normal/near-normal bedside spirometry → comprehensive PFT evaluation.
  • Possible mixed obstruction/restriction → lung-volume testing.
  • Severe COPD/emphysema with suspected air trapping → body plethysmography for RV/TLC.
  • Suspected neuromuscular weakness → MIP/MEP plus spirometry and serial respiratory-strength assessment as indicated.
  • Asthma not responding as expected → pre/post bronchodilator spirometry when clinically appropriate.
Patient/data safety: Never label a patient with restriction from an invalid FVC maneuver. Poor effort is a data-quality problem that must be corrected before the physiologic pattern can be trusted.
Putting C5 together
Validate calibration and maneuver quality first. Then use FEV₁/FVC to identify obstruction, recognize low FVC with a preserved ratio as possible restriction rather than automatic proof, and use TLC/lung-volume testing to confirm restriction when needed.

Pulmonary function testing: prove the measurement before you interpret the disease

PFT equipment measures different pieces of respiratory function. The therapist's job is to select the correct test, produce technically valid data, recognize when the patient or equipment invalidated the maneuver, and only then interpret the physiologic pattern.

question → test → calibration → coaching → quality → pattern → mechanism → next test/action

RTE Breadth of Knowledge: before moving on, you should be able to...

  • match spirometry, peak flow, lung volumes, and respiratory-pressure devices with the measurements each produces;
  • explain how pneumotachographs and common flow sensors measure airflow;
  • state that RV cannot be measured by spirometry and identify plethysmography/gas dilution as lung-volume methods;
  • perform the complete FVC coaching sequence including immediate maximal blast and continued exhalation for at least 6 seconds or to the course plateau criterion;
  • calibrate before patient testing with the 3-L syringe and recognize the lesson's ±3.5% calibration limit;
  • recognize hesitation, cough, early termination, leaks, tongue obstruction, and inconsistent effort as invalidating problems;
  • apply the lesson's >150 mL trial-variation troubleshooting threshold;
  • identify FVC, FEV₁, FEV₁/FVC, and PEF;
  • recognize the lesson's obstructive pattern of FEV₁/FVC <0.70 and a scooped expiratory loop;
  • recognize that low FVC with a preserved ratio can suggest restriction but requires valid effort and reduced TLC for confirmation;
  • perform peak-flow testing and calculate percent of personal best;
  • apply green 80–100%, yellow 50–79%, and red <50% peak-flow zones;
  • explain why body plethysmography detects trapped gas better than gas dilution in severe obstruction;
  • describe MIP/NIF and MEP measurement and the lesson's normal/weakness ranges; and
  • identify when a patient needs comprehensive PFT or respiratory-muscle testing beyond bedside spirometry.

RTE Depth of Clinical Judgment: take the next step

  • Information Gathering: verify calibration, patient understanding, inspiratory effort, initial blast, exhalation duration, nose/mouth seal, cough, reproducibility, and loop quality before interpreting results.
  • Information Gathering: determine which missing measurement—TLC/RV, respiratory pressure, or another comprehensive PFT component—is needed to resolve an unclear bedside pattern.
  • Decision Making: repeat technically invalid spirometry rather than diagnosing obstruction or restriction from poor-quality data.
  • Decision Making: recognize a low ratio as the lesson's obstructive pattern and use lung volumes when restriction remains suspected.
  • Decision Making: choose body plethysmography when severe obstruction and trapped gas make dilution methods less reliable.
  • Decision Making: recognize a red-zone PEF together with severe symptoms as an urgent asthma pattern requiring rescue treatment/emergency evaluation according to the lesson plan.
  • Decision Making: use MIP/NIF and MEP when weakness and ability to sustain ventilation or cough are the clinical concerns.
  • Decision Making: remove a spirometer from service when calibration continues to fail rather than trying to interpret patient results from questionable equipment.
  • Reassessment: repeat valid measurements after rest, recoaching, bronchodilator therapy, equipment correction, or other indicated intervention and compare the trend.
  • Communication: report both the physiologic result and the quality of the maneuver so the team knows whether the interpretation is trustworthy.
