Respiratory Equipment • Competency Guide

Professionalism, Communication & Documentation

Learn how professional conduct, objective documentation, accurate order interpretation, concise handoff, appropriate escalation, and patient-centered communication protect patients and support safe respiratory care.

Before you begin

Professionalism is not separate from clinical skill. It determines whether the right treatment is performed safely, whether another clinician can understand what happened, whether patient information remains private, and whether unsafe situations are recognized and escalated.

The PulmoLearn lesson emphasizes several high-yield rules: students do not perform unsupervised skills before competency validation; chart objectively and promptly; access patient information only when directly involved in care; interpret orders exactly as written; give concise clinical handoff; and use the chain of command for ethical or safety concerns.

For the 2027 Respiratory Therapy Examination (RTE), Breadth of Knowledge includes documentation structure, order interpretation, confidentiality, handoff content, and professional conduct. Depth of Clinical Judgment requires you to decide what information matters, when to clarify an order, when to decline a task, when to escalate a concern, and how to communicate without delaying patient care.

Big idea: Follow the chain clinical responsibility → verify scope/order → perform care → reassess → document objectively → communicate priority → escalate when needed.

Core vocabulary: accountability, ethics, integrity, reliability, confidentiality, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), protected health information (PHI), scope of competency, chain of command, objective documentation, chief complaint (CC), history of present illness (HPI), past medical history (PMH), social history (SH), physical examination (PE), PRN, W/A, BID, TID, QID, Q4, Q12, STAT, handoff, escalation, teach-back, medical interpreter.

Professionalism is observable behavior

The lesson identifies eight core behaviors: accountability, respect, ethics, integrity, reliability, communication, positivity, and flexibility. These behaviors become clinically meaningful when something is difficult—when you make a mistake, receive correction, are asked to do something beyond your competency, or witness unsafe behavior.

The non-negotiable student rule

Students should never perform a skill independently before they are competency-validated for unsupervised performance.

If asked to perform a skill beyond your current competency, the professional response taught in the lesson is: “I'm not yet signed off on that—can you supervise me or perform it?” The patient need may be urgent, but urgency does not make an unvalidated student independently competent.

HIPAA and confidentiality

The source lesson teaches a simple operational rule: access or discuss patient health information only when you are directly involved in that patient's care and need the information for care. Being a hospital employee, student, coworker, friend, or relative does not create authorization.

  • Do not open charts out of curiosity.
  • Do not discuss patient details in hallways, elevators, cafeterias, or public spaces.
  • Do not photograph or screenshot patient information.
  • Do not post patient-related information on social media.
  • Dispose of written clinical notes containing PHI securely.
  • Report witnessed privacy violations through the appropriate chain of command.

Gossip affects patient care

The lesson treats workplace gossip as more than poor manners. It damages trust and team function. The professional response is to redirect rather than participate and to address genuine concerns through direct communication or appropriate supervisory channels.

RTE distinction: “A staff RT asked me to do it” does not make an unsupervised student skill appropriate. Patient safety and competency boundaries take priority over staffing pressure.
Putting C1 together
Act within competency, protect confidentiality, own errors, accept feedback, avoid gossip, and use appropriate channels when safety or ethics are threatened.
C2: Document respiratory assessment, therapy, education, and patient response objectively, accurately, clearly, and in a timely manner.
2027 NBRC alignment: I.A.1.
Breadth: know the characteristics of good documentation and the structure of the pulmonary case study taught in the lesson.
Depth: select clinically meaningful objective facts and document both intervention and response without speculation or character judgments.

“If you didn't chart it, you didn't do it.”

The lesson treats the RT note as a permanent clinical and legal record used for continuity of care, communication, billing, and legal protection. Good documentation is objective, timely, accurate, clear, specific, and relevant.

Objective versus subjective wording

Poor chartingBetter charting
“Patient looked really uncomfortable.”“RR 28/min, accessory muscle use present, bilateral expiratory wheeze, speaking in short sentences.”
“Treatment given; patient better.”Document medication/device, pre/post HR/SpO₂, breath sounds, symptoms, and response.
“Patient is always noncompliant.”Document the observable behavior or patient statement without labeling character.

Pulmonary case-study structure

  • CC: chief complaint—ONE primary reason for seeking care.
  • HPI: chronological progression centered on the chief complaint.
  • PMH: relevant prior diagnoses/hospitalizations.
  • SH: relevant smoking, occupation, exposures, living situation.
  • PE: objective physical-assessment findings.
  • Labs: relevant ABG, imaging, cultures, ECG, and other data.
  • Plan: RT goals, oxygen target, treatments, education, escalation.
  • Follow-up: response and reassessment plan.

Chief complaint = one complaint

The lesson repeatedly tests this. “Shortness of breath worsening over 3 days” is an appropriate CC. COPD, fever, sputum color, smoking history, and other symptoms belong elsewhere in the history rather than being turned into a diagnostic list under CC.

Common mistake: Documenting only that a treatment occurred. Respiratory charting should show what the patient was like before treatment, what was done, and what changed afterward.
Putting C2 together
Document measurable findings and specific therapy, include patient response, keep the note timely and relevant, and separate objective clinical facts from opinion.

W/A is a classic exam distinction

A patient sleeping comfortably with acceptable clinical status is not routinely awakened for an order written “while awake.” Assess the patient, follow the order as written, and document the assessment/reasoning according to the clinical workflow.

PRN is not automatic

PRN means there should be a clinical need consistent with the order. A therapist should assess the patient rather than automatically administering every PRN therapy because it is available.

Clarify rather than guess

If the order is incomplete, contradictory, outside expected parameters, or unclear enough that two reasonable clinicians could perform meaningfully different treatments, clarify before proceeding unless an immediate emergency requires stabilizing care first.

RTE trap: W/A does not mean “wake at the scheduled clock time.” It means administer during waking hours.
Putting C3 together
Interpret frequency and indication exactly, assess the patient when clinical judgment is required, and clarify ambiguity instead of inventing an order.
C4: Deliver concise, prioritized clinical handoff and communicate respiratory status, current support, response to therapy, and pending needs to the healthcare team.
2027 NBRC alignment: III.G.2, III.I.1.a–b.
Breadth: know the required elements of a respiratory handoff.
Depth: prioritize the information the next clinician needs now and omit irrelevant social detail, speculation, and gossip.

Handoff transfers clinical responsibility

The lesson emphasizes that incomplete handoffs are a patient-safety problem. A strong RT handoff is concise, objective, and prioritized by current risk.

Include

  • Patient identity/location and current stability.
  • Current oxygen device, FiO₂/flow, and saturation target.
  • Current ventilator settings when applicable.
  • Recent clinically meaningful changes.
  • Last respiratory treatment and patient response.
  • Pending tests/procedures such as ABG or bronchoscopy.
  • Immediate concerns and which patients need attention first.

Do not include

  • Personal opinions about the patient or staff.
  • Irrelevant biographical or family details.
  • Speculative diagnoses not established in the chart.
  • Gossip about clinical incidents.

Example of prioritized handoff

“Room 412: COPD exacerbation. Venturi 28%, SpO₂ goal 88–92%, currently 90%. Scheduled Duoneb given at 0600 and 1000 with improved wheeze and no tachycardia. ABG due at 1400. Patient is stable, but fatigue and work of breathing are increasing—reassess early.”

This report is useful because it tells the next RT the current support, target, recent treatment, response, pending task, and the reason the patient needs close attention.

Putting C4 together
Give the next clinician the current respiratory picture, what changed, what was done, what happened afterward, what is pending, and what requires attention first.

Patient safety changes the urgency

If a concern creates immediate risk, protect the patient first and escalate now. Do not postpone action to avoid conflict. For nonurgent interpersonal conflict, direct and private professional communication may be the best first step.

Patient-first safety: Never perform a procedure you are not competent to perform independently merely to avoid disappointing a staff member. The professional response is to obtain qualified supervision or assistance.
Putting C5 together
Address routine conflict directly when appropriate, but use the chain of command when safety, ethics, discrimination, confidentiality, or competency boundaries are involved.

2027 NBRC alignment: I.B.1.l–m, III.I.1.d–e, III.I.2.
Breadth: know the communication practices taught for language barriers, cultural differences, patient education, and respect.

Clear communication is a clinical intervention

Patients cannot safely use oxygen, inhalers, airway-clearance devices, or home respiratory equipment if they do not understand what to do. Respectful communication supports both trust and treatment accuracy.

Language and cultural communication

  • Use trained medical interpreter services for complex or sensitive clinical communication rather than relying on family members as the primary interpreter.
  • Speak at a normal, measured pace rather than shouting or speaking unnaturally slowly.
  • Allow extra time when interpretation is required.
  • Respect cultural health beliefs and practices even when they differ from your own.
  • Do not assume cultural difference means misunderstanding or nonadherence.
  • Avoid visible frustration or derogatory comments.

Teach what the patient must do

Education should be specific to the respiratory task: inhaler sequence, oxygen safety, incentive-spirometry goal, secretion-clearance technique, home equipment cleaning, or signs that should trigger help. Teach in small steps and use patient-friendly language.

Verify understanding

A patient saying “yes” does not prove understanding. Ask the patient or caregiver to explain the plan in their own words or demonstrate the equipment technique. Teach-back turns education into an assessment of whether the communication worked.

Common mistake: Equating agreement with comprehension. Effective education is demonstrated by accurate explanation or performance, not by a polite “I understand.”
Putting C6 together
Communicate respectfully, use qualified interpretation when needed, avoid cultural assumptions, teach the respiratory task clearly, and verify understanding through teach-back or return demonstration.

Professional respiratory care: safe action plus clear communication

Professionalism becomes visible in decisions: staying within competency, protecting privacy, documenting what actually happened, interpreting orders correctly, handing off priority information, escalating unsafe care, and adapting communication so the patient can participate safely.

verify responsibility → provide care → reassess → document → handoff → escalate/educate → close the loop

RTE Breadth of Knowledge: before moving on, you should be able to...

  • identify accountability, respect, ethics, integrity, reliability, communication, positivity, and flexibility as the lesson's core professional behaviors;
  • state that students should not perform unsupervised skills before competency validation;
  • apply HIPAA confidentiality principles to chart access, public discussion, photography/screenshots, social media, handoff, and written notes;
  • distinguish objective, timely, accurate, clear documentation from vague or judgmental charting;
  • document pre-treatment assessment, therapy performed, post-treatment response, and relevant patient education;
  • identify CC, HPI, PMH, SH, PE, Labs, Plan, and Follow-Up in the pulmonary case-study structure;
  • state that the chief complaint contains one primary complaint;
  • interpret Q4, Q12, BID, TID, QID, PRN, W/A, and STAT as taught in the lesson;
  • recognize that W/A does not mean waking a comfortably sleeping patient for routine therapy;
  • recognize that PRN therapy requires a clinical indication consistent with the order;
  • include current status, oxygen/ventilator support, treatment response, pending tasks, and priority concerns in handoff;
  • omit gossip, opinions, irrelevant personal details, and unsupported speculation from clinical handoff;
  • use the chain of command for unsafe clinical behavior, privacy violations, discrimination, or unresolved safety concerns;
  • use trained medical interpreters for complex clinical discussions rather than relying on family as the primary interpreter; and
  • verify patient education through teach-back or return demonstration.

RTE Depth of Clinical Judgment: take the next step

  • Information Gathering: determine the patient's current status, ordered therapy, therapist/student competency, confidentiality authorization, communication barriers, and pending clinical needs before acting.
  • Decision Making: decline an unsupervised procedure beyond competency and obtain qualified supervision rather than performing it because the unit is busy.
  • Decision Making: refuse unauthorized chart access or disclosure even when the requester is another healthcare worker or a personal friend.
  • Decision Making: document measurable clinical findings and patient response rather than opinion or character labels.
  • Decision Making: clarify an ambiguous or unsafe order rather than guessing what the prescriber intended.
  • Decision Making: hold a routine W/A treatment while the patient sleeps comfortably when that matches the order and clinical condition, documenting the assessment as appropriate.
  • Decision Making: prioritize the deteriorating or high-risk patient first during handoff rather than reporting in arbitrary room order.
  • Decision Making: escalate unsafe behavior or a privacy breach through the appropriate chain rather than ignoring it to preserve team harmony.
  • Decision Making: notify and protect the patient first when an urgent safety problem exists rather than completing routine documentation before escalation.
  • Communication/Education: adapt language and use interpreter services or teach-back so the patient can safely participate in the respiratory-care plan.
  • Reassessment: close the communication loop by confirming that handoff, escalation, order clarification, or education produced the intended safe next step.
National Board for Respiratory Care connection: The Respiratory Therapy Examination expects professional behavior to be integrated with clinical practice: evaluate orders and patient information, document care accurately, communicate with the healthcare team, escalate concerns, protect confidentiality, educate patients and families, and remain within safe professional competency. Primary alignment in this guide: I.A.1, I.B.1.l–m, II.B, III.G.2, and III.I.
