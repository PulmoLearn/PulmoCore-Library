Introduction to Aerosol Therapy

An aerosol is a suspension of solid or liquid particles in a gas. Aerosol drug therapy delivers medication in particle form directly to the respiratory tract, achieving high local drug concentrations at low total doses — with fewer systemic side effects than oral or IV routes.

Goals of Aerosol Therapy
Deliver medication directly to airway smooth muscle, mucosa, or alveoli. Higher local drug concentration at the target tissue. Lower systemic drug exposure compared to oral/IV. Faster onset for bronchodilators (minutes vs. 30+ minutes for oral).
Aerosol Generators
Atomizers — convert liquid to droplets by high-pressure gas (used for nebulizing local anesthetics, dye, etc.).
Nebulizers — generate fine therapeutic aerosol from liquid medication.
Inhalers (MDI, DPI, Respimat) — generate single-breath aerosolized doses from pressurized or powdered formulations.
Factors Affecting Delivery Efficiency
Particle size (MMAD) · Patient's inspiratory flow · Respiratory rate · Breath-hold duration · Airway anatomy (normal vs. obstructed) · Device assembly and setup · Patient technique and coordination

Aerosol Physics & Particle Deposition

VERY HIGH-YIELD — NBRC TESTED

Where an aerosol particle deposits in the airway is primarily determined by its size, measured as Mass Median Aerodynamic Diameter (MMAD). MMAD is the particle size at which half the aerosol mass consists of smaller particles and half consists of larger particles.

Deposition Mechanisms
Inertial impaction — large particles (≥5 µm) cannot follow airway curves; they impact on mucosa at bends. Sedimentation — medium particles (2–5 µm) settle under gravity in smaller airways. Diffusion (Brownian motion) — tiny particles (<1 µm) deposit by random motion in alveoli. Particles <0.5 µm may be exhaled without depositing.
Optimizing Deposition
Slow inhalation: reduces inertial impaction in upper airway, allows particles to travel deeper. Breath hold (10 sec): increases sedimentation time in lower airways — more drug deposits. Particle size selection: target 2–5 µm MMAD for lower airway bronchodilator therapy.

Aerosol Hazards & Infection Control

Patient Hazards
Adverse drug reactions (bronchospasm, tachycardia, systemic effects) · Bronchospasm from irritating aerosol (bland or medicated) · Eye irritation from aerosol cloud · Overhydration with bland aerosol · Reflex coughing and increased secretion production
Caregiver & Environmental Hazards
Secondary drug exposure — RTs, nurses, and family absorb aerosol medication. Particularly significant with mucolytics, antibiotics, and steroids. Always position patient appropriately and work in ventilated areas. Ribavirin (antiviral) requires full OSHA protection.
Infection Control
Single-patient-use equipment only. SVN and other nebulizers are recognized sources of nosocomial infection if improperly cleaned. Disinfect between patients. Negative pressure rooms and N95 masks required for aerosol-generating procedures in airborne isolation. Never share inhalers between patients.

Metered-Dose Inhaler (MDI)

HIGH-YIELD
Components
Pressurized canister (drug + propellant) · Actuator body (nozzle + mouthpiece) · Metering chamber (measures each dose) · HFA propellant (replaced CFC propellants) · Dose counter (on newer devices)
Best For
Patients with good coordination · Rescue and maintenance medication delivery · Home and outpatient use · Short-acting bronchodilators · ICS delivery (with spacer preferred) · Patients who can perform slow, coordinated inhalation
Maintenance
Prime new inhaler (4 actuations into air) · Clean mouthpiece weekly — rinse with warm water, air dry completely · Track doses — do not estimate by shaking · Discard after labeled dose count exhausted, even if it "feels" like there's still medication

MDI Technique — Step by Step

  1. Remove cap and inspect mouthpiece for obstructions
  2. Shake the inhaler for 5 seconds (mixes propellant and medication)
  3. Prime if new or unused for >3 days (actuate into air)
  4. Exhale fully and comfortably away from the mouthpiece
  5. Seal lips around mouthpiece (or hold 1–2 cm from open mouth for open-mouth technique)
  6. Begin a slow, deep inhalation — then press the canister once as you begin to breathe in
  7. Continue slow inhalation to full inspiration
  8. Hold breath for up to 10 seconds to maximize deposition
  9. Wait 30–60 seconds between puffs (allows medication to clear and propellant to dissipate)
  10. Replace cap; rinse mouth if steroid-containing

An RT observes a patient using their albuterol MDI. The patient removes the cap, shakes the inhaler, exhales fully, seals lips around the mouthpiece — then takes a fast, forceful breath while pressing the canister. What is the error, and what does it cause?

Spacer / Valved Holding Chamber

HIGH-YIELD

A spacer (or valved holding chamber, VHC) is a device placed between the MDI and the patient's mouth. It holds the aerosol cloud after actuation, giving the patient time to inhale — eliminating the need for perfect coordination between actuation and inhalation.

Benefits of Spacer Use
Eliminates coordination demand · Larger particles fall out in the spacer (not the oropharynx) → reduces thrush and systemic absorption of ICS · Smaller particles reach lower airways → improved lung deposition · Slows aerosol velocity, further reducing oropharyngeal impaction
Best Candidates for Spacer
Poor MDI coordination (common NBRC scenario) · Young children and older adults · Every patient using inhaled corticosteroids · Patients who consistently underdose because of timing errors · Patients with arthritis or hand-strength limitations
Troubleshooting & Maintenance
Whistle/squeaking sound during inhalation: patient inhaling too fast — slow down. Visible medication escaping: poor lip seal around mouthpiece. Static buildup (particles cling to walls): wash weekly in warm water with dish soap; air dry — do NOT wipe inside. Pediatric spacers use a mask instead of mouthpiece for blowby delivery.

Breath-Actuated MDI

The breath-actuated MDI automatically releases a dose when the patient creates adequate inspiratory flow — eliminating the coordination problem of traditional MDIs. The patient does not need to manually press the canister.

Advantages
No hand-breath coordination required · Less oropharyngeal deposition compared to poorly timed MDI use · Helpful for patients with arthritis, limited hand function, or poor timing · Automatically doses at the right moment of inhalation
Limitation
Requires the patient to generate adequate inspiratory flow to trigger the mechanism. Patients in severe acute asthma or with very weak respiratory effort may not generate sufficient flow to actuate the device — making it unreliable in acute distress. SVN is preferred in severe episodes.

Soft-Mist Inhaler — Respimat

The Respimat uses spring compression to generate a slow-moving, low-velocity aerosol mist without a propellant. The slow mist velocity dramatically reduces oropharyngeal impaction compared to traditional MDIs — even without a spacer.

Key Advantages
Long plume duration (1.5 seconds) vs MDI (<0.15 seconds) · Slow velocity reduces impaction · No propellant · Significantly less coordination demand than MDI · Better lung deposition than most MDIs
Technique
Turn base until it clicks (loads dose) · Open cap · Exhale fully away from device · Seal lips firmly — do not block the vents on either side of mouthpiece · Press dose-release button while inhaling slowly and deeply · Hold breath up to 10 seconds
Troubleshooting
No mist produced: device not loaded (base not turned) or device at end of life. Reduced delivery: patient blocked side vents during inhalation. Dose wasted: patient pressed button before starting to inhale — re-teach that pressing and inhaling must begin simultaneously.

Dry Powder Inhalers (DPI)

VERY HIGH-YIELD — FAST INHALATION REQUIRED

DPIs deliver powdered medication that must be aerosolized by the patient's own inspiratory flow — there is no propellant. This makes them breath-actuated by definition, but it also means that inspiratory flow rate is critical: too slow and the powder clumps; too fast and deposition shifts to the upper airway.

💨 MDI — Metered-Dose Inhaler

SLOW

Slow, controlled, deep inhalation while simultaneously pressing the canister. Propellant carries the drug — patient flow is not the aerosolizing force. Fast inhalation causes impaction.

  • Propellant-driven
  • Coordination required
  • Works in severe distress
  • Add spacer for poor coordination

🌬️ DPI — Dry Powder Inhaler

FAST

Fast, forceful, deep inhalation. The patient's inspiratory flow IS the aerosolizing force — it disaggregates the powder into respirable particles. Slow inhalation = clumped powder = wasted dose.

  • Patient-powered (no propellant)
  • Breath-actuated
  • NOT for severe distress
  • Cannot use spacer

DPI Device Types

Single-Dose Capsule

HandiHaler · Aerolizer
Load capsule → pierce → inhale. Capsule is inhaled THROUGH the device, not swallowed.

Multi-Unit Dose (Blister)

Diskhaler
Contains multiple pre-loaded blisters; advance to next dose by turning or clicking.

Multidose Reservoir

Twisthaler · Diskus · Flexhaler
Internal reservoir holds many doses; loading mechanism dispenses one dose per actuation cycle.
DPI Technique: Load dose (device-specific) → exhale fully and away from device → seal lips → inhale RAPIDLY and deeply → hold breath 10 seconds → close/replace cover → rinse mouth if steroid.

DPI Contraindications / Poor Candidates: Severe acute airway obstruction (cannot generate adequate flow) · Children <5 years · Patients with weak inspiratory effort (neuromuscular disease, etc.) · Patients unable to follow instructions for loading and inhaling

Small-Volume Nebulizers (SVN)

VERY HIGH-YIELD

SVNs convert liquid medication into aerosol using compressed gas. They are the workhorse device for acute bronchodilator therapy — they work without requiring any patient coordination and can be used even in severe respiratory distress.

Jet Nebulizer Types
Standard JN (T-piece): aerosol released continuously — medication wasted during exhalation.
JN with collection bag: captures exhaled aerosol for re-inhalation — reduces waste.
Breath-enhanced JN: one-way valves increase aerosol delivery during inspiration; reduces waste.
Breath-actuated JN: releases aerosol ONLY during inhalation — most efficient; requires adequate triggering flow.
SVN Technique
Verify order → add medication → connect gas source (O₂ or air) at 6–8 L/min → apply mouthpiece (or mask) → verify visible aerosol output → instruct slow tidal breathing with occasional deep breaths → keep cup upright → run until sputtering (treatment complete) → clean/disinfect nebulizer cup.
Troubleshooting
No mist: check gas flow, tubing connections, cup assembly. Weak mist: increase flow to minimum 6 L/min. Medication remaining at end: cup was tilted during treatment (tilting increases dead volume); keep upright. Treatment taking too long: increase driving flow to 8 L/min. Patient dizzy/tachycardic: stop treatment, reassess.

Ultrasonic Nebulizers (USN)

Ultrasonic nebulizers use a piezoelectric transducer that vibrates at ultrasonic frequency to generate aerosol from liquid placed directly on or above the transducer. No compressed gas is required — electrically powered.

Characteristics
Electrically powered (no gas required) · Quiet operation · Portable models available · Generates high volumes of aerosol · More expensive than jet nebulizers · Can overheat labile medications (heat-sensitive drugs should not be used in USN)
Limitations
Can overheat medication (heat from transducer) · Not appropriate for all medications · Expensive to purchase and maintain · Transducer requires careful cleaning · Less commonly used now compared to VMN for clinical applications · Not typically used for standard bronchodilator delivery in acute settings

Vibrating Mesh Nebulizer (VMN)

HIGH-YIELD — PREFERRED FOR VENTILATOR & HFNC

VMNs use a vibrating plate with thousands of tiny precision-engineered holes (the mesh) through which liquid medication is pumped, generating a fine aerosol. They are highly efficient, quiet, portable, and do not require added gas flow — making them ideal for ventilator and HFNC circuits.

Advantages
Highly efficient — minimal residual volume · Quiet — no compressed gas noise · Portable — battery-powered options · No added gas flow — does not alter FiO₂, flow, or ventilator settings when inline · Excellent particle size control · Drug formulation preserved (no heating)
Clinical Applications
HFNC aerosol delivery — VMN preferred because it does not change the HFNC gas flow or FiO₂. Ventilator inline nebulization — does not disturb trigger sensitivity or ventilator settings. Specialty medications (inhaled antibiotics, etc.). Home/portable nebulization for COPD and CF patients.
Limitations & Troubleshooting
Mesh can clog — especially with viscous medications or if dried medication is not cleaned promptly. Requires careful cleaning and maintenance. Higher initial cost than jet nebulizers. Not all medications are compatible. If no aerosol: clogged mesh → clean with sterile water per manufacturer protocol.

Nebulizer Variables — Residual Volume & Flow Effects

VERY HIGH-YIELD
Residual Volume ("Dead Volume")
The medication that remains in the SVN cup at end of treatment — it can never be nebulized because the fluid level drops below the siphon tube and jet orifice.

Residual volume is typically 0.5–1.5 mL. It represents wasted medication. Factors that increase it: tilting the nebulizer, low fill volume, poor assembly.
Effect of Increased Gas Flow
Higher driving gas flow:
• Decreases average particle size (more turbulence fragments droplets smaller)
• Decreases treatment time (higher flow aerosolizes liquid faster)
• Minimum: 6 L/min · Optimal: 6–8 L/min

Sub-optimal flow = larger particles + longer treatment time + less lower airway deposition.
Gas Density Effects
Lower-density gas (heliox) reduces turbulence in the aerosol stream, reducing upper airway impaction and improving penetration to lower airways. Used with heliox-driven SVNs in severe asthma.

Air vs. oxygen: both are commonly used; clinical choice based on patient oxygen requirements.
Minimizing Residual Volume: Fill the SVN with an adequate total volume (typically 3–5 mL total, diluting medication with NS to reach target volume) · Keep the nebulizer cup upright during treatment · Tap the sides gently toward the end when sputtering begins to knock remaining fluid down · Consider VMN for critical medications where residual loss is clinically significant.

Aerosol Delivery on HFNC & Mechanical Ventilators

HFNC Aerosol Delivery

VMN — Preferred Device
The VMN is the preferred aerosol device for HFNC because it adds no additional gas flow — the HFNC flow and FiO₂ remain unchanged. Place VMN at the gas inlet of the HFNC system. The HFNC flow carries aerosol to the patient. Efficiency depends on HFNC flow rate — higher flows dilute and sweep aerosol through more quickly.
Position & Efficiency
Nebulizer placed at the HFNC gas inlet (between humidifier and patient cannula). Higher HFNC flows (>40 L/min) may dilute aerosol and reduce lung deposition. Consider pausing HFNC briefly for critical medications in consultation with the provider — but maintain oxygenation safety first.

Mechanical Ventilator Aerosol Delivery

VMN — First Choice on Ventilator
No added gas flow — does not alter tidal volume, trigger sensitivity, or pressure limits. Place in the inspiratory limb 15 cm from patient Y-piece. Most efficient inline aerosol delivery available.
MDI with Adapter
MDI can be used inline with a special MDI adapter placed in the inspiratory circuit. Requires actuation timed with the ventilator breath. Effective when used correctly — adapter position and breath timing both matter. No gas flow added.
⭐ Critical Rule: Remove the HME Before Aerosol Therapy
The HME must be removed from the ventilator circuit before any inline aerosol treatment. Aerosol particles (both medication and water) will clog the HME hygroscopic filter element, dramatically increasing circuit resistance. After treatment is complete, the HME can be reinstalled. If aerosol therapy is frequent or ongoing, switch to a heated humidifier circuit instead.
  • Inspiratory flow pattern matters — a decelerating flow waveform (VC-AC) increases residence time and deposition compared to square waveform.
  • Slow respiratory rate and longer inspiratory time increase aerosol deposition in ventilated patients.
  • Humidity in the circuit affects aerosol — high humidity can cause aerosol particles to grow in size, shifting deposition more proximally.

Common NBRC Traps — Aerosol Drug Therapy

  • Confusing MDI and DPI inhalation technique. MDI requires SLOW, coordinated inhalation. DPI requires FAST, forceful inhalation. This is the single most commonly confused technical distinction in aerosol therapy. Know it cold.
  • Giving a DPI to a patient in severe respiratory distress. Patients with severe airway obstruction, high RR, and accessory muscle use cannot generate the sustained forceful inspiratory flow needed to aerosolize DPI powder. SVN is the correct choice for acutely distressed patients.
  • Not removing the HME before administering inline aerosol therapy. The HME filter element becomes obstructed by aerosol particles, increasing circuit resistance. Always remove the HME first — the NBRC tests this frequently.
  • Thinking VMN adds gas flow to HFNC or ventilator circuits. The VMN does NOT add gas flow — this is one of its primary advantages. Jet nebulizers add 6–8 L/min of driving gas; VMNs add zero. This makes VMN the preferred device when altering circuit flow is unacceptable.
  • Forgetting that small particles (2–5 µm) target the lower airways. Large particles (>5 µm) deposit in the upper airway by impaction. Small particles (2–5 µm) deposit in the lower airways by sedimentation. The therapeutic target for bronchodilators is the lower airways — which is why spacers (removing large fast particles) improve lower airway delivery.
  • Forgetting that fast inhalation through an MDI causes upper airway impaction. High flow = turbulence = large particles impact on oropharynx = wasted dose. Slow inhalation allows particles to travel past the upper airway and deposit in the lower airways.
  • Using "blowby" technique (holding mask near face without sealing) in children. Blowby is ineffective — aerosol particles disperse rapidly in open air. A sealed spacer with mask or proper SVN mask seal is required for effective pediatric delivery.
  • Tilting the SVN during treatment. SVN cups must be kept upright. Tilting the cup moves the medication away from the siphon tube and jet orifice, dramatically increasing residual (dead) volume and wasting medication.

FAQ

Common Questions

Why does the MDI require slow inhalation but the DPI requires fast inhalation?

The mechanisms are completely different. In an MDI, the propellant does the work of aerosolizing the medication — the drug is already in particle form when it leaves the nozzle. The patient just needs to deliver it to the lower airways. A slow inhalation creates laminar (smooth) airflow that allows particles to follow the airway curves and deposit in the lower airways by sedimentation, rather than impacting on the oropharyngeal walls. In a DPI, there is no propellant — the patient's own inspiratory airflow is what disaggregates the powder from its bulk carrier into respirable particles. High-velocity airflow through the device creates turbulence that breaks apart powder clusters into small particles. Without fast, forceful inhalation, DPI powder delivers incompletely aerosolized clumps that deposit in the upper airway.

What is MMAD and how does it predict where medication deposits?

MMAD stands for Mass Median Aerodynamic Diameter — the particle size at which half the aerosol mass consists of particles smaller than this diameter and half consists of particles larger. Larger particles (≥5 µm) deposit in the upper airway and oropharynx by inertial impaction — they cannot follow the curved airway walls and simply impact on mucosa. Medium particles (2–5 µm) penetrate into the lower airways and deposit in the bronchi and bronchioles by sedimentation (settling under gravity during slow inhalation and breath hold). Very small particles (1–3 µm) reach the alveoli and deposit by diffusion. Particles smaller than ~0.5 µm may be exhaled without depositing at all. The therapeutic target for bronchodilators is the lower airways (2–5 µm), which is why proper technique and particle size selection matter clinically.

Why can't you use a spacer with a DPI?

Spacers are designed for MDI use — they hold the aerosol cloud from the MDI propellant blast, giving the patient time to inhale. DPIs produce powder, not aerosol — there is no slow-moving aerosol cloud to hold. If you tried to actuate a DPI into a spacer, the powder would just settle in the spacer and be lost. More fundamentally, the DPI's aerosolization depends on the patient's inspiratory flow passing through the device to disaggregate the powder. If the powder were somehow delivered into a spacer first, there would be no airflow-driven turbulence to break up the powder clusters.

What is residual (dead) volume in an SVN?

Residual volume (also called dead volume) is the medication left in the nebulizer cup at the end of treatment — the fluid that remains because the liquid level has dropped below the siphon tube and jet orifice, so it cannot be aerosolized. Typical SVN residual volume is 0.5–1.5 mL. This medication is wasted. To minimize residual volume: fill the SVN to an adequate volume (typically 3–5 mL total), keep the cup upright during treatment (tilting moves fluid away from the siphon tube), and gently tap the sides when sputtering begins. VMNs have significantly smaller residual volumes because they pump fluid through a mesh plate rather than relying on a siphon tube.

When should I choose SVN over MDI with spacer?

MDI with spacer is preferred for outpatient and stable patients — it is more portable, efficient in terms of medication delivery, and convenient. SVN is preferred when: (1) the patient is in acute severe distress and cannot coordinate any inhalation technique; (2) the patient is very young or cognitively impaired and cannot perform even spacer-assisted MDI; (3) the ordered dose is larger than can be conveniently delivered by MDI; (4) the medication is only available in liquid form for nebulization; (5) continuous aerosol delivery is needed for ongoing bronchospasm management.
