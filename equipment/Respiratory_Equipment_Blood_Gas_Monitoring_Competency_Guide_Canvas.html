Blood Gas Monitoring, ABG Sampling & Analyzers

Learn how to obtain trustworthy arterial blood gas data, recognize preanalytic error, operate and evaluate analyzers, choose the correct monitoring technology, and decide when a number reflects the patient—or when the number itself should not be trusted.

Before you begin

The most important distinction in blood gas monitoring is that oxygenation and ventilation are not the same thing. Peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) may look acceptable while carbon dioxide rises and pH falls. An arterial blood gas (ABG) can provide information about oxygenation, ventilation, and acid-base balance—but only when the sample is valid and the analyzer is functioning correctly.

For the 2027 Respiratory Therapy Examination (RTE), Breadth of Knowledge includes ABG collection, normal values, sample errors, analyzer operation, co-oximetry, pulse oximetry, and transcutaneous monitoring. Depth of Clinical Judgment means deciding whether the result can be trusted, whether another test is needed, whether the patient is deteriorating, and what should happen before treatment decisions are made.

Big idea: Follow the chain clinical question → patient preparation → correct sample → specimen quality → analyzer quality → result → clinical fit → intervention → reassessment.

Core vocabulary: arterial blood gas (ABG), partial pressure of arterial oxygen (PaO₂), partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO₂), arterial oxygen saturation (SaO₂), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂), bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), oxygenation, ventilation, acid-base balance, radial artery, Modified Allen test, collateral circulation, preheparinized syringe, preanalytic error, calibration, quality control (QC), point-of-care analyzer, co-oximetry, carboxyhemoglobin (COHb), methemoglobin (MetHb), plethysmographic waveform, transcutaneous oxygen (TcPO₂), transcutaneous carbon dioxide (TcPCO₂).

First decide what information the ABG must answer

An ABG can evaluate ventilation through PaCO₂ and pH, oxygenation through PaO₂ and SaO₂, and acid-base balance through pH, PaCO₂, and HCO₃⁻. It is especially important when ventilation status cannot be determined from pulse oximetry.

Pre-sampling checks

  • Verify the order and identify the patient with two approved identifiers.
  • Document the oxygen device and exact flow or FiO₂ at the time of sampling.
  • Note recent changes in oxygen or ventilator settings.
  • Assess anticoagulant use and bleeding risk.
  • Confirm a palpable pulse at the intended site.
  • Gather a preheparinized ABG syringe, needle, antiseptic, gauze, label, biohazard bag, and sharps container.

Modified Allen test before radial puncture

The Modified Allen test checks whether the ulnar artery can provide collateral circulation to the hand if radial flow is compromised.

  1. Have the patient clench the hand.
  2. Compress both the radial and ulnar arteries.
  3. Have the patient open the hand; the palm should appear blanched.
  4. Release the ulnar artery while maintaining radial compression.
  5. Observe for return of color.

The lesson defines color return within 5–7 seconds as adequate collateral circulation. If color does not return or takes more than 15 seconds, do not use that radial site.

Arterial puncture and post-procedure care

For a true arterial puncture, arterial pressure should fill the syringe; do not pull forcefully on the plunger. After collection, remove the needle safely, apply firm direct pressure for at least 5 minutes and longer for anticoagulated patients, check for hematoma and distal perfusion, remove air bubbles, cap the syringe, gently roll to mix with heparin, label at the bedside, and transport appropriately.

Common mistake: Calling an ABG “room air” when the patient is still receiving oxygen. PaO₂ cannot be interpreted correctly without knowing the oxygen conditions at the moment of collection.

Most ABG errors happen before the analyzer

A precise analyzer cannot rescue a bad specimen. Preanalytic error can create results that look physiologically plausible but lead to the wrong treatment.

Why delay changes the blood

Blood cells remain metabolically active after collection. They consume oxygen and produce carbon dioxide. That means delay tends to make PaO₂ fall, PaCO₂ rise, and pH fall. Chilling slows this process when delayed analysis is unavoidable under the lesson procedure.

A result must fit both physiology and specimen history

If a hypoventilating patient unexpectedly has a very low PaCO₂ and high pH, ask whether an air bubble contaminated the sample. If an old room-temperature sample shows worsening CO₂ and acidosis, do not assume the patient's ventilation suddenly failed without checking specimen timing.

Safety: Do not change oxygen or ventilator support based on a clearly clotted, significantly air-contaminated, mislabeled, or improperly stored sample. Obtain valid data while supporting any clinically unstable patient.

The analyzer is another clinical instrument that must earn your trust

Blood gas analyzers directly measure pH, PaCO₂, and PaO₂ using parameter-specific electrochemical sensors. Derived values such as HCO₃⁻ and base excess are then calculated. The instrument must be calibrated and pass quality control before its numbers are used for patient decisions.

Calibration and QC answer different questions

Calibration adjusts the instrument against known reference values. The lesson introduces 1-point, 2-point, and 3-point calibration concepts. Quality control tests whether the analyzer can correctly measure a control sample with a known expected range. A calibration that completes is not a license to ignore failed QC.

QC failure means stop patient testing

If QC fails, remove the analyzer from patient service, troubleshoot according to the analyzer process, repeat QC, and use another analyzer or the central lab while the problem is being corrected. Do not run a patient sample “just to see if it looks right.” Clinical plausibility cannot validate an instrument that has failed its quality check.

Examples of troubleshooting targets

The lesson directs attention to calibration-gas connections, control expiration, electrode condition, cartridge status, and other manufacturer-defined causes of failure. The exact device-specific service process may differ, but the safety rule does not: no patient results from an analyzer that has not demonstrated acceptable performance.

Safety: Analyzer QC failure is not an inconvenience to work around. It is a reason to stop using the instrument for patient testing until reliability is restored.

Pulse oximetry is not a ventilation monitor

Pulse oximetry can show a normal SpO₂ while the patient develops severe hypercapnia and respiratory acidosis. If the clinical question is “Is this patient ventilating adequately?” you need information about PaCO₂ and pH—not just saturation.

CO poisoning requires co-oximetry

The lesson identifies co-oximetry as the test that directly measures carboxyhemoglobin. Standard pulse oximetry can appear falsely reassuring because it cannot reliably distinguish COHb from oxyhemoglobin. Smoke exposure, neurologic symptoms, or a clinical picture inconsistent with SpO₂ should trigger this distinction.

Pulse-ox limitations

Motion, poor peripheral perfusion, dark or artificial nail coverings, and dyshemoglobins can reduce reliability. Check the pleth waveform and compare pulse-ox heart rate with the patient's actual heart rate before trusting an unexpected value.

Transcutaneous monitoring

The transcutaneous sensor heats the skin to approximately 42–45°C to increase local perfusion and allow gas diffusion. The lesson emphasizes neonatal/pediatric use and trending rather than exact equivalence with arterial values. Reposition sensors every 4–6 hours in adults and more frequently, about 2–4 hours in neonates, to reduce thermal injury risk.

Common mistake: Choosing the easiest monitor instead of the monitor that answers the question. SpO₂ answers “How saturated is hemoglobin?” It does not answer “What is the PaCO₂?” or “What is the pH?”
Putting C4 together
Name the clinical question first. Use ABG for ventilation/acid-base and direct arterial oxygenation data, co-oximetry for dyshemoglobins, pulse oximetry for continuous saturation trending, and transcutaneous monitoring when continuous noninvasive gas trending is useful and its limitations are acceptable.

Ask: does the number fit the patient?

A respiratory therapist should neither ignore an abnormal monitor nor obey it blindly. Compare the result with respiratory rate, mental status, work of breathing, perfusion, oxygen device, pleth waveform, recent changes, and prior data.

Mismatch examples

  • SpO₂ 99% after smoke exposure + confusion: do not conclude oxygenation is normal; obtain co-oximetry for suspected COHb.
  • SpO₂ fluctuates wildly during movement: evaluate motion artifact and waveform quality before escalating oxygen solely from the displayed number.
  • Cold mottled fingers + absent pleth: peripheral perfusion is inadequate; choose a better site or different measurement method.
  • ABG shows unexpectedly low PaCO₂/high pH in a hypoventilating patient: inspect for air-bubble contamination.
  • ABG result from a delayed room-temperature sample: suspect metabolic drift before attributing the change entirely to the patient.

Urgent patterns taught in the lesson

  • Critically low PaO₂: apply/increase oxygen and consider escalation to HFNC or NIV while notifying the provider.
  • Rising PaCO₂ with somnolence or distress: recognize impending ventilatory failure and escalate to NIV or prepare for intubation.
  • Severe acidosis, pH <7.20: emergent intervention and ventilatory-support preparation.
  • QC failure: remove analyzer from service; use an alternate analyzer or central lab.
  • Questionable sample quality: do not report or act on it as valid; recollect while managing any obvious patient instability.

Case: normal SpO₂ can hide hypercapnic failure

A COPD patient becomes somnolent with a respiratory rate of 8/min while SpO₂ remains 94% on supplemental oxygen. The clinical problem is possible hypoventilation. Pulse oximetry cannot measure PaCO₂ or pH. An ABG is required to evaluate ventilation.

The lesson's case then shows pH 7.26, PaCO₂ 71 mmHg, PaO₂ 74 mmHg, and HCO₃⁻ 31 mEq/L—an acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory-failure pattern. Continuous SpO₂ remains useful, but it cannot replace serial assessment of ventilation when PaCO₂ and pH are driving the treatment decision.

Patient-first safety: Data validation should never become a reason to delay treatment of obvious deterioration. Support the unstable patient while simultaneously verifying whether the measurement, specimen, or analyzer is trustworthy.
Putting C5 together
Compare every result with the clinical picture, verify signal/sample/analyzer quality, identify the most likely source of disagreement, obtain better data when needed, and escalate immediately when the patient's physiology—not merely the number—shows danger.

Blood gas monitoring: trustworthy data before treatment decisions

The ABG, pulse oximeter, co-oximeter, and transcutaneous monitor answer different questions. The RTE expects you to know their roles, but also to recognize bad samples, poor signal quality, analyzer failures, and dangerous patient-monitor mismatches.

patient → clinical question → specimen/monitor → data quality → result → clinical fit → action → reassessment

RTE Breadth of Knowledge: before moving on, you should be able to...

  • differentiate oxygenation from ventilation and identify which ABG parameters reflect each;
  • state the lesson normal ranges for pH, PaCO₂, PaO₂, HCO₃⁻, and SaO₂;
  • identify common indications, hazards, supplies, and sample-rejection criteria for ABG analysis;
  • perform the Modified Allen test in sequence and apply the lesson's color-return criteria;
  • describe radial puncture preparation, arterial self-filling, post-puncture pressure, and distal-perfusion checks;
  • document the oxygen device and flow/FiO₂ at the exact time of the draw;
  • predict the lesson effects of an air bubble: PaCO₂ decreases, pH increases, and PaO₂ is variable;
  • predict the lesson effects of room-temperature delay: PaCO₂ rises, pH falls, and PaO₂ falls;
  • recognize a clotted, mislabeled, significantly air-contaminated, or improperly stored specimen as unreliable;
  • differentiate stationary and point-of-care blood gas analyzers;
  • explain calibration versus QC and the requirement to remove an analyzer from patient service after QC failure;
  • identify co-oximetry as the required technology for direct COHb/MetHb measurement;
  • state what pulse oximetry measures and what it cannot measure;
  • recognize motion, poor perfusion, nail covering, and dyshemoglobin limitations of pulse oximetry;
  • explain transcutaneous monitoring, its 42–45°C heating principle, trending role, perfusion limitations, and burn risk; and
  • apply the lesson's escalation patterns for severe hypoxemia, rising PaCO₂ with somnolence/distress, severe acidosis, QC failure, and questionable samples.

RTE Depth of Clinical Judgment: take the next step

  • Information Gathering: determine the clinical question, oxygen device/FiO₂, recent respiratory-support changes, patient temperature when relevant, site safety, bleeding risk, specimen timing, and signal/analyzer quality.
  • Information Gathering: distinguish oxygenation failure from ventilation failure rather than assuming a normal SpO₂ excludes respiratory failure.
  • Decision Making: reject/recollect a clotted or significantly contaminated sample instead of changing treatment from unreliable results.
  • Decision Making: obtain co-oximetry when smoke/CO exposure makes pulse oximetry unreliable.
  • Decision Making: remove an analyzer from service after QC failure and use another validated testing pathway.
  • Decision Making: use ABG data rather than pulse oximetry alone when PaCO₂ and pH determine whether NIV is correcting hypercapnic respiratory failure.
  • Decision Making: escalate respiratory support when rising PaCO₂, somnolence, severe acidosis, or worsening oxygenation indicates true physiologic deterioration.
  • Reassessment: repeat or trend the appropriate measurement after intervention and confirm that the patient's clinical status changes in the expected direction.
  • Communication: report the result together with sample source, oxygen/ventilator conditions, specimen-quality concerns, analyzer status, and the patient's clinical response.
