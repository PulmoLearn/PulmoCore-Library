This lesson focuses on the bedside procedure side of ventilator liberation: choosing the correct equipment, setting up spontaneous breathing trials, monitoring tolerance, recognizing failure, preparing for extubation, troubleshooting post-extubation airway problems, and supporting withdrawal of ventilatory support.

Learning Objectives

  1. Differentiate ventilator weaning, spontaneous breathing trial, extubation, and liberation from mechanical ventilation.
  2. Select and set up appropriate SBT equipment for CPAP/PSV trials, T-piece trials, and tracheostomy collar trials.
  3. Gather baseline data before initiating a spontaneous breathing trial.
  4. Monitor SBT tolerance and identify stop criteria requiring return to ventilatory support.
  5. Recognize that successful SBT completion does not automatically equal extubation readiness.
  6. Prepare and verify required equipment before extubation, including suction, oxygen, BVM, cuff syringe, and reintubation readiness.
  7. Support the extubation procedure and monitor for post-extubation complications such as stridor, respiratory distress, and secretion retention.
  8. Assist with withdrawal of ventilatory support using an equipment-focused, comfort-centered bedside workflow.
  9. Document SBT results, extubation procedure details, patient response, complications, and team communication.
Lesson boundary: This is not a deep ventilator physiology lesson. It is the equipment/procedure lesson for setting up, monitoring, troubleshooting, and documenting ventilator liberation procedures.

Ventilator Liberation: What Starts the Process?

PROCEDURE FRAMEWORK

Ventilator liberation begins when the patient is stable enough to attempt reducing or discontinuing ventilatory support. Weaning is the overall process. A spontaneous breathing trial is a timed test of whether the patient can breathe with minimal or no assistance. Extubation is the separate airway-removal decision.

Weaning
The process of reducing ventilator support or assessing whether support can be discontinued. It may occur rapidly or gradually depending on patient condition and facility protocol.
SBT
A procedure used to test spontaneous breathing tolerance, commonly using CPAP, PSV, T-piece, or tracheostomy collar. It collects patient response data.
Extubation
Removal of the endotracheal tube after ventilatory support and airway readiness have been assessed. Passing an SBT does not automatically mean the airway is safe for extubation.
  • Too soon: premature extubation can lead to respiratory failure, reintubation, airway trauma, and longer ICU stay.
  • Too long: unnecessary ventilator days increase risk for VAP, airway injury, deconditioning, and other complications.
  • RT role: prepare the equipment, run the trial safely, monitor trends, identify failure, and communicate findings clearly.

SBT Equipment & Bedside Readiness

EQUIPMENT SETUP

Before an SBT, verify that the patient, ventilator, oxygen delivery, monitoring, suction, and rescue equipment are ready. The exact setup depends on the SBT method and the airway type.

Trial Equipment
Ventilator with CPAP/PSV or spontaneous mode · T-piece setup · Tracheostomy collar setup · Oxygen source and flowmeter · Heated humidification or aerosol source if indicated · Large-bore tubing for aerosol or trach collar trials.
Monitoring Equipment
Pulse oximeter · ECG monitoring · Blood pressure monitoring · ETCO₂ monitoring if available · Timer/stopwatch · Ventilator waveform/parameter display · Documentation tool or flowsheet.
Airway & Rescue Equipment
Suction setup · Yankauer · Inline or sterile suction catheter · BVM connected to oxygen · Reintubation equipment available per policy · Oxygen delivery device ready for post-extubation if applicable.
Before You Start
Verify the order/protocol · Verify patient identity · Review current settings and readiness criteria · Explain procedure · Position patient upright if tolerated · Ensure alarms are active and audible.

SBT Setup Methods

The method should match the patient’s airway, provider order, facility protocol, and equipment available. CPAP/PSV keeps the patient on the ventilator for monitoring. T-piece and trach collar trials disconnect the ventilator and provide supplemental oxygen through the artificial airway.

SBT MethodEquipment NeededSetup FocusCommon Troubleshooting
CPAP TrialVentilator in CPAP/spontaneous modeSet minimal PEEP/CPAP, appropriate FiO₂, active alarms, monitor VT/RR/VELow VT or high RR may indicate fatigue; return to support if stop criteria appear
PSV TrialVentilator in PSV/spontaneous modeSet low pressure support, minimal PEEP, appropriate FiO₂; confirm spontaneous triggerExcessive PS can mask weakness; too little PS can overburden patient
T-Piece TrialT-piece, oxygen source, flowmeter, humidity/aerosol setup, large-bore tubingDisconnect ventilator, provide oxygen and humidity through the tube, monitor continuouslyFlow too low can increase work of breathing or allow room-air entrainment
Trach Collar TrialTrach collar, aerosol source or humidifier, oxygen source, large-bore tubingPlace collar over trach, deliver humidified/aerosolized oxygen, monitor toleranceCondensate can block tubing; drain away from patient and verify mist/flow

Baseline Data Before Starting an SBT

Before the trial begins, gather enough information to compare the patient’s response during the trial. Baseline values provide the “before” picture and help identify deterioration early.

Ventilator Baseline
Current mode · FiO₂ · PEEP · VT · RR · minute ventilation · pressure support · recent alarm pattern · waveform concerns · spontaneous triggering.
Patient Baseline
SpO₂ · RR · HR · rhythm · BP · temperature if relevant · mental status · comfort/anxiety · work of breathing · cough strength · secretion burden.
Optional/Ordered Parameters
RSBI · NIF/MIP · MEP · spontaneous VT · ABG trend · ETCO₂ trend · cuff leak if extubation may follow.
Board-style trap: Do not start the SBT and then look for baseline values. Baseline status must be known before the trial so that changes can be interpreted.

Monitoring During the SBT

During the trial, monitor both numbers and patient appearance. Trend changes matter more than a single isolated number.

Oxygenation
SpO₂ trend · FiO₂ requirement · color · cyanosis · ability to maintain target saturation.
Ventilation
RR · VT · minute ventilation · ETCO₂ trend if available · breathing pattern · shallow rapid breathing.
Cardiovascular Response
HR · rhythm · BP · new dysrhythmia · chest pain · diaphoresis.
Clinical Appearance
Accessory muscles · nasal flaring · paradoxical breathing · anxiety · agitation · decreased LOC · inability to clear secretions.

SBT Stop Criteria and Immediate Actions

HIGH-YIELD DECISION POINT

If failure signs appear, stop the trial, return to previous support or a comfortable support level, reassess, and notify the provider according to protocol.

Finding During SBTWhat It SuggestsRT Action
SpO₂ < 90% or falling below ordered targetInadequate oxygenationStop SBT, return to support, increase oxygen/support as needed
RR > 35/min or marked increase from baselineIncreased work of breathing or fatigueStop SBT and return to previous settings or comfortable support
HR > 140/min, significant HR change, or new dysrhythmiaCardiac intoleranceStop SBT, monitor rhythm, notify provider
SBP > 180 or < 90, or unstable BPHemodynamic intoleranceStop SBT and reassess
Diaphoresis, accessory muscles, anxiety, agitationRespiratory distressStop SBT, return support, assess airway and secretion burden
Rising ETCO₂ or decreasing mental statusVentilatory failure or fatigueStop SBT; consider ABG and provider notification

Passing an SBT Is Not the Same as Extubation Readiness

A successful SBT suggests the patient may tolerate breathing without ventilatory assistance. Extubation also requires airway readiness: the ability to protect the airway, clear secretions, and maintain patency after the tube is removed.

Ventilation Readiness
Passed SBT · stable RR and VT · acceptable oxygenation · stable HR/BP · no distress · acceptable ABG or ETCO₂ trend if obtained.
Airway Readiness
Awake enough to protect airway · adequate cough · manageable secretions · suction needs not excessive · no high-risk airway swelling signs · cuff leak if indicated · reintubation plan available.
  • High-risk airway: traumatic or prolonged intubation, absent cuff leak, prior stridor, upper airway edema, difficult airway history, or large ETT relative to airway size.
  • Action if uncertain: do not treat “passed SBT” as automatic extubation. Communicate findings and prepare for additional airway evaluation or treatment per protocol.

Extubation Procedure Workflow

Follow facility policy, provider order, and local protocol. The general procedural flow is consistent: verify readiness, prepare equipment, suction, deflate cuff, remove tube, apply support, and reassess.

  1. Verify order and readiness: confirm provider order/protocol, SBT outcome, airway readiness, and post-extubation oxygen plan.
  2. Explain procedure: coach patient that they may cough and will receive oxygen immediately after removal.
  3. Position patient: upright or semi-Fowler if tolerated.
  4. Preoxygenate if ordered/protocolized: increase FiO₂ or oxygen support as directed.
  5. Suction: suction ETT and oral cavity to reduce secretion aspiration risk.
  6. Deflate cuff: use syringe to remove air from pilot balloon/cuff fully.
  7. Remove tube smoothly: remove according to facility procedure, often coordinated with patient inspiration or cough.
  8. Encourage cough and clear secretions: suction oral cavity as needed.
  9. Apply oxygen device: nasal cannula, mask, HFNC, NIV, or aerosol as ordered.
  10. Reassess immediately: SpO₂, RR, breath sounds, stridor, WOB, voice quality, mental status.
  11. Document: time, procedure, cuff leak if performed, suctioning, device applied, patient response, complications, provider communication.
Safety note: Extubation must occur with rescue equipment ready. The best time to prepare for reintubation is before the tube is removed.

Post-Extubation Monitoring & Troubleshooting

The first minutes after extubation are high-risk. Monitor airway patency, oxygenation, ventilation, and secretion clearance.

FindingLikely ConcernRT Action
Inspiratory stridorUpper airway swelling / post-extubation edemaAssess severity; apply ordered aerosol/medication support; notify provider; prepare for reintubation if worsening
Rising RR and accessory musclesRespiratory distress or fatigueEscalate oxygen/ventilatory support per order/protocol; consider NIV/HFNC; notify provider
SpO₂ falling despite oxygenHypoxemia or airway failureIncrease support; assess breath sounds and airway; prepare for reintubation if unstable
Weak cough and retained secretionsAirway clearance failureSuction, coach cough, consider airway clearance support, notify provider if worsening
Decreased mental statusVentilatory failure, fatigue, or hypoxemiaAssess airway/ventilation, consider ABG/ETCO₂, prepare for advanced support
Post-extubation stridor: Mild/moderate stridor with stable vital signs may be managed with ordered aerosol/medication therapy such as racemic epinephrine per protocol. Severe distress, worsening oxygenation, or declining mental status requires immediate escalation and reintubation readiness.

Withdrawal of Ventilatory Support / Terminal Extubation Support

PROCEDURE ASSIST

This section focuses on the RT’s equipment and procedure support role when withdrawal of ventilatory support is ordered. Follow facility policy, provider orders, and the established goals of care.

Confirm & Coordinate
Confirm provider order · Review plan with RN/provider · Clarify whether the plan is terminal extubation or ventilator withdrawal with airway left in place · Ensure family/support persons are considered per policy.
Prepare Equipment
Suction available · Oxygen or humidified support if ordered for comfort · BVM available per policy · Supplies for secretion management · Tissues/gauze · Medication administration handled by appropriate team members.
Monitor & Document
Adjust alarms or monitoring per policy · Provide comfort-focused airway support · Document ventilator settings before withdrawal, time of withdrawal/extubation, patient response, secretion management, and team communication.

Summary & NBRC Reminders

Weaning vs SBT vs Extubation
Weaning is the process. SBT is the breathing test. Extubation is the airway-removal procedure. Do not collapse these into one decision.
SBT Stop Criteria
Stop for SpO₂ <90%, RR >35, HR >140 or major change, unstable BP, distress, diaphoresis, accessory muscles, agitation, or declining mental status.
Extubation Readiness
Passing SBT does not guarantee safe extubation. Check airway protection, cough, secretion burden, mental status, cuff leak if indicated, and reintubation readiness.
Withdrawal of Support
Confirm order and plan, prepare suction and comfort-focused equipment, coordinate with the team, adjust monitoring per policy, and document carefully.
  • Common trap: Choosing extubation when the patient is actively failing the SBT.
  • Common trap: Ignoring absent cuff leak in a high-risk airway because the SBT was successful.
  • Common trap: Treating post-extubation stridor as lower-airway wheezing or secretion retention.
  • Common trap: Focusing only on oxygen saturation during SBT and missing RR, WOB, HR, BP, ETCO₂, or mental status deterioration.
