VAP Prevention, Oral Care, Cuff Pressure, Skin Care & Artificial Airway Maintenance

This lesson focuses on the bedside equipment and procedures that reduce ventilator-associated pneumonia risk and prevent artificial-airway injury: oral care supplies, suction systems, cuff manometers, subglottic suction, securement devices, skin checks, and circuit-condensate safety.

Learning Objectives

  1. Select the correct equipment for VAP prevention, oral care, cuff pressure monitoring, subglottic suction, and skin protection.
  2. Perform a bedside VAP-prevention workflow without contaminating the airway, circuit, or oral-care supplies.
  3. Use cuff pressure monitoring to reduce aspiration risk while preventing tracheal injury.
  4. Manage ventilator circuit condensate safely and explain why it must never be drained toward the patient.
  5. Assess and troubleshoot artificial airway maintenance problems, including tube migration, low cuff pressure, skin breakdown, pooled secretions, and contaminated equipment.
  6. Document VAP-prevention procedures, patient response, tube position, cuff pressure, secretion findings, and escalation needs.
Equipment-course boundary: This lesson does not teach pneumonia pathophysiology in depth. It focuses on what the RT chooses, sets up, checks, performs, troubleshoots, and documents at the bedside.

VAP Risk Workflow — What the RT Controls

PROCEDURE FOCUS

Ventilator-associated pneumonia prevention is a system of repeated bedside actions. The RT cannot eliminate every risk, but the RT directly influences aspiration risk, secretion pooling, circuit contamination, airway trauma, and artificial-airway stability.

Reduce Aspiration Risk
Maintain appropriate cuff pressure, keep oral secretions controlled, use subglottic suction when available, and coordinate with nursing on head-of-bed elevation.
Reduce Contamination Risk
Avoid unnecessary circuit breaks, keep sterile/clean supplies separated, drain condensate away from the patient, and replace contaminated equipment per policy.
Reduce Airway Injury
Reposition the ETT, inspect lips/skin, avoid overinflated cuffs, protect pressure points, and verify tube depth after care.
Board-style trap: VAP prevention is not just “give oral care.” It is an airway maintenance workflow: cuff pressure + oral secretion management + circuit safety + tube security + skin protection + documentation.

Oral Care Setup and Procedure Flow

EQUIPMENT: Oral care kit · suction toothbrush · Yankauer
  1. Review order/policy for oral care frequency and solution selection.
  2. Perform hand hygiene and apply PPE.
  3. Verify ETT/trach tube position and securement before moving equipment.
  4. Prepare suction and test function before beginning oral care.
  5. Use suction toothbrush/swabs to clean oral surfaces while continuously removing pooled secretions.
  6. Suction above and around the cuff area without pulling on the tube.
  7. Reassess tube position, lip/skin condition, cuff pressure if indicated, and patient tolerance.
  8. Document oral care, suctioning, secretion appearance, tube position, and complications.
Procedure priority: Keep the airway secure. Oral care is not successful if the ETT migrates, the cuff pressure changes, or the patient becomes unstable during the procedure.

Cuff Pressure Monitoring

EQUIPMENT: Cuff manometer
Why Check It?
Low cuff pressure can allow secretions to leak past the cuff. High cuff pressure can reduce tracheal mucosal perfusion and cause injury.
Target Range
Common target: 20–30 cmH₂O. Follow facility policy and provider orders. Recheck after tube movement, repositioning, or major ventilator changes.
Do Not Guess
Pilot balloon palpation is not accurate. Use a cuff manometer and document the measured pressure.

Clinical Check: Cuff Pressure

An intubated patient has audible gurgling above the cuff and repeated low-volume alarms. The cuff pilot balloon feels soft. What is the best next action?

Subglottic Suctioning

Subglottic suction systems remove secretions pooled above the cuff before they can leak into the lower airway. Not every ETT has this feature, so students must identify the port and understand the facility suction method.

Continuous or Intermittent
Follow facility policy and manufacturer guidance. Continuous suction can injure mucosa if set too high; intermittent suction may be used to reduce trauma.
Patency Check
If the port is clogged, pooled secretions remain above the cuff. Check connections, suction source, and tubing; notify provider/team if the port cannot be cleared per policy.
Before Repositioning
Suction oral/subglottic secretions before moving the ETT or changing securement so pooled secretions are not displaced toward the cuff.
Document
Chart secretion amount/character, suction method, patient tolerance, and any port obstruction or bleeding.

Ventilator Circuit Handling and Condensate Safety

HIGH-YIELD SAFETY POINT

Condensate in ventilator tubing can contain patient secretions and microorganisms. It can obstruct flow, trigger alarms, and create infection risk if drained toward the patient or back into the humidifier.

  • Drain away from the patient. Position tubing so water moves to a collection point, not toward the airway.
  • Never drain back into the humidifier. Condensate is contaminated once it has traveled through the circuit.
  • Avoid unnecessary circuit breaks. Each break increases contamination risk and may cause derecruitment in unstable patients.
  • Use heated-wire circuits when appropriate. Heated-wire circuits reduce, but do not eliminate, rainout.

Tube Securement, Repositioning, and Skin Protection

EQUIPMENT: securement device · barrier dressing · bite block
Before Moving the Tube
Verify depth marking, suction oral/subglottic secretions, assess sedation/biting risk, and ensure at least one person maintains tube control.
During Repositioning
Move the tube to the opposite side of the mouth per policy, inspect lips/cheeks, replace wet or loose securement, and avoid excessive tension.
After Repositioning
Confirm tube depth, breath sounds, exhaled volumes, cuff pressure, SpO₂, and ventilator alarms. Document new position.

Tracheostomy-Specific Maintenance Considerations

Stoma and Neck Skin
Assess for redness, drainage, pressure injury, bleeding, odor, and moisture. Replace wet dressings or ties per policy while maintaining airway security.
Inner Cannula
Clean or replace per facility protocol. A blocked inner cannula can mimic bronchospasm or worsening lung disease.
Humidification
Trach patients bypass the upper airway and often require humidification or aerosol. Dry secretions increase obstruction risk.
Emergency Supplies
Keep replacement trach tubes, obturator, suction, BVM with trach adapter/mask, and oxygen source available according to facility policy.

Summary & Board-Prep Reminders

VAP Prevention Is a Workflow
Oral care, cuff pressure, subglottic suction, HOB elevation, circuit handling, securement, skin care, and documentation all work together.
Cuff Pressure
Measure with a manometer. Common target is 20–30 cmH₂O unless policy differs. Low pressure increases aspiration risk; high pressure increases tracheal injury risk.
Condensate
Drain away from the patient and discard. Never drain toward the airway or back into the humidifier.
Tube Safety
Before and after oral care or repositioning, verify tube depth, securement, skin condition, breath sounds, exhaled volumes, SpO₂, and cuff pressure if indicated.
