Airway Pharmacology — Inhaled Medication Classes

Learn how to recognize major inhaled medication classes, distinguish rescue from controller therapy, connect each class with its clinical purpose, identify important side effects, and determine when treatment should be continued, modified, stopped, or escalated.

Before you begin

Respiratory therapists frequently administer inhaled medications, teach device technique, monitor response, and recognize adverse effects. This lesson does not require memorizing every drug dose or brand name. Instead, it focuses on the patterns that matter most clinically: What class is this medication? Is it rescue or controller therapy? What effect should occur? What side effect is important? What should the respiratory therapist do if the response is unsafe or ineffective?

For the 2027 Respiratory Therapy Examination (RTE), Breadth of Knowledge means recognizing the major inhaled medication classes, examples, mechanisms, clinical roles, and side effects. Depth of Clinical Judgment means selecting the appropriate class in a patient scenario, interpreting the patient's response, and deciding when medication therapy or respiratory support must change.

Big idea: Follow the chain patient problem → medication class → rescue/controller role → expected effect → observed response → adverse effect → modify/stop/escalate → reassess.

Core vocabulary: bronchodilator, short-acting beta-2 agonist (SABA), long-acting beta-2 agonist (LABA), short-acting muscarinic antagonist (SAMA), long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA), anticholinergic, inhaled corticosteroid (ICS), mucolytic, topical airway anesthetic, rescue medication, controller medication, beta-2 receptor, muscarinic receptor, bronchospasm, airway inflammation, mucus viscosity, oral candidiasis, dysphonia, tachycardia, tremor, urinary retention, aspiration, methemoglobinemia.

Rescue and controller medications solve different problems

Rescue medications act quickly to relieve acute bronchospasm. Controller medications are taken on a schedule to reduce future symptoms or maintain bronchodilation. They are not interchangeable.

SABA: rapid rescue

Short-acting beta-2 agonists stimulate beta-2 receptors in airway smooth muscle, causing relaxation and bronchodilation. The lesson uses albuterol and levalbuterol for acute asthma or COPD bronchospasm, sudden dyspnea, exercise-induced bronchospasm, and pretreatment before selected mucolytic therapy.

LABA: maintenance, not acute rescue

LABAs act at the same beta-2 receptor but are designed for prolonged bronchodilation. The key course distinction is that a patient in acute distress should not substitute a LABA controller for a SABA rescue medication.

ICS: control inflammation, not immediate bronchospasm

Inhaled corticosteroids reduce airway inflammation, hyperresponsiveness, and mucus production over time. They do not rapidly relax bronchial smooth muscle. A patient with severe acute wheezing who repeatedly uses fluticasone is using the wrong pharmacologic tool for the immediate problem.

RTE distinction: The class name is only the starting point. Be able to say whether the drug is intended to relieve symptoms now, prevent symptoms later, thin secretions, reduce inflammation, or facilitate a procedure.

Choose the class that treats the actual physiologic problem

Acute bronchospasm

When the immediate problem is acute bronchospasm with wheezing and dyspnea, the lesson identifies a SABA such as albuterol as the primary rescue class. In acute COPD exacerbation, ipratropium may be added because it provides bronchodilation through muscarinic blockade.

Long-term bronchodilator maintenance

LABAs and long-acting anticholinergics such as tiotropium are controller therapies. Their role is to maintain bronchodilation over time rather than reverse sudden severe symptoms.

Persistent airway inflammation

The lesson identifies ICS therapy for persistent asthma and selected COPD patients with frequent exacerbations, often in combination with a LABA. The expected benefit develops over time. The patient must understand that daily adherence matters even when symptoms are controlled.

Thick secretions

Acetylcysteine breaks disulfide bonds in mucoproteins and reduces mucus viscosity. The lesson associates it with thick, retained secretions and warns that it can trigger bronchospasm, especially in reactive airways. A bronchodilator is often available or used before treatment.

Dornase alfa breaks down DNA in mucus from dead neutrophils and is specific in this lesson to cystic fibrosis. It is not presented as a general mucolytic for non-CF conditions.

Before airway procedures

Topical lidocaine or benzocaine may be used to reduce gagging, coughing, and airway irritation before bronchoscopy, nasotracheal suctioning, intubation, or other airway instrumentation. The benefit—suppressed reflexes—also creates the major safety risk: temporarily reduced airway protection.

Clinical reasoning: “The patient is short of breath” is not enough information to choose a drug. Determine whether the problem is bronchospasm, chronic airflow limitation, airway inflammation, retained secretions, or procedural discomfort.

Therapeutic response versus adverse effect

After a bronchodilator, less wheezing, easier breathing, reduced work of breathing, and improved airflow are expected. A modest heart-rate increase may occur, but marked tachycardia with dizziness or palpitations changes the risk-benefit balance. The therapist should not continue simply because the medication was ordered.

Paradoxical bronchospasm

Paradoxical bronchospasm means the airway narrows rather than improves after inhaled therapy. New or worsening wheeze and respiratory distress during treatment require the treatment to stop and the patient to be reassessed promptly.

No response may mean the medication is wrong for the problem

An ICS will not relieve severe acute bronchospasm in minutes. A mucolytic will not substitute for bronchodilation. A correctly delivered medication can still appear to “fail” when the wrong class was chosen for the immediate physiologic problem.

Patient-first safety: If respiratory distress worsens, airway protection is lost, or a severe medication reaction develops, stabilize and escalate the patient rather than finishing the scheduled treatment or documentation first.

Some inhaled therapies depend heavily on specialized delivery systems

The core PulmoLearn pharmacology lesson concentrates on SABA, LABA, anticholinergic, ICS, mucolytic, and topical anesthetic therapy. The 2027 NBRC outline also places aerosolized antimicrobials and pulmonary vasodilators within inhaled medication management. For this competency, the required connection is primarily equipment-focused: know that these therapies must be paired with an appropriate respiratory delivery system rather than treated like interchangeable standard inhalers.

Aerosolized antimicrobial therapy

When an antimicrobial is ordered by inhalation, the respiratory therapist's equipment responsibilities include verifying the correct medication and prescribed delivery method, using the appropriate aerosol device, minimizing unintended environmental exposure according to policy, and assessing tolerance and respiratory response.

Pulmonary vasodilator therapy

Pulmonary vasodilator therapy may use aerosol or specialty-gas systems. The equipment lesson connection is that delivery may require dedicated devices, blending, monitoring, or circuit placement. The therapist must verify that the equipment is designed for the prescribed therapy and that respiratory support is not disrupted during administration.

Stay within the scope of what this lesson teaches

This guide does not introduce detailed drug dosing or disease-specific pulmonary-vasodilator protocols that are not taught in the source lesson. The competency expectation here is to recognize the therapy category, connect it to appropriate inhaled/specialty delivery equipment, and apply the same safety pattern used throughout the course: verify the system, monitor the patient, and reassess response.

RTE distinction: For specialty inhaled therapy, medication knowledge and equipment knowledge meet. A correct drug delivered through an inappropriate system is still ineffective or unsafe therapy.

RTE Depth of Clinical Judgment: take the next step

  • Information Gathering: identify the current respiratory problem, medication class, rescue/controller role, pre-treatment breath sounds, work of breathing, respiratory rate, heart rate, oxygenation, secretion burden, and medication history.
  • Information Gathering: determine whether apparent treatment failure reflects the wrong medication class, inadequate delivery technique, worsening disease, or an adverse reaction.
  • Decision Making: select SABA therapy for acute bronchospasm rather than relying on LABA or ICS controller therapy for immediate relief.
  • Decision Making: add/recognize anticholinergic therapy in the acute COPD pattern taught in the lesson while reserving long-acting agents for maintenance.
  • Decision Making: stop and reassess a bronchodilator treatment when significant tachycardia or other important adverse symptoms develop.
  • Decision Making: stop acetylcysteine and address bronchoconstriction when the mucolytic triggers worsening wheeze.
  • Decision Making: maintain aspiration precautions after topical airway anesthesia until protective sensation returns.
  • Decision Making: recognize that a correctly delivered controller drug is still the wrong immediate therapy for severe acute bronchospasm.
  • Reassessment: compare the patient's post-treatment respiratory status with the pre-treatment baseline and determine whether the intended effect occurred.
  • Communication/Education: teach rescue-versus-controller use, rinse-and-spit technique for ICS, relevant side-effect precautions, and when the patient should seek further help.
National Board for Respiratory Care connection: The Respiratory Therapy Examination expects you to identify respiratory medication classes, understand what each is intended to accomplish, administer or support appropriate inhaled therapy, recognize adverse responses, and recommend changes when therapy is ineffective or unsafe.
