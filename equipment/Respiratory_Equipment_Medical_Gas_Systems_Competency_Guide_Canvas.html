Respiratory Equipment • Competency Guide

Medical Gas Storage, Cylinders, Regulators, Flowmeters & Oxygen Supply Systems

Learn how respiratory therapists identify medical gases, choose and connect gas-supply equipment, calculate portable oxygen duration, plan safe transport, recognize system limitations, and troubleshoot failures before they become patient emergencies.

Before you begin

Medical gas equipment can look simple because many components are familiar: a cylinder, regulator, flowmeter, wall outlet, or home oxygen concentrator. The clinical responsibility is more complex. The respiratory therapist must know what gas is being supplied, where it comes from, whether the connection is correct, whether the measured flow can be trusted, how long the supply will last, and what backup plan protects the patient if the system fails.

The 2027 Respiratory Therapy Examination (RTE) tests both Breadth of Knowledge and Depth of Clinical Judgment. For this lesson, breadth means knowing the equipment, safety systems, calculations, properties, and operating principles well enough to apply them. Depth means recognizing what matters in a transport, home-care, or gas-delivery failure scenario and deciding what should be checked, changed, replaced, or escalated.

Big idea: Follow the chain gas identity → source → connection → pressure reduction → flow control → patient delivery → reserve → troubleshooting → backup → reassessment.

Core vocabulary: compressed gas, liquefied gas, cylinder factor, pounds per square inch gauge (psig), regulator, reducing valve, flowmeter, Thorpe tube, Bourdon gauge, compensated flowmeter, Pin Index Safety System (PISS), Diameter Index Safety System (DISS), pressure relief device, hydrostatic testing, bulk oxygen system, zone valve, oxygen concentrator, molecular sieve, zeolite, liquid oxygen (LOX), Dewar flask, cryogenic injury, oxygen enrichment, reserve supply, transport duration.

Start with the gas, not the color

Medical gases are drugs and therapeutic resources. The respiratory therapist must identify the gas from the cylinder label or approved source identification, understand how it is stored, and know what the supply system can and cannot provide. Color coding can help as a secondary check, but the lesson emphasizes that color alone is not enough because colors may fade, labeling conventions may differ, and a cylinder may be incorrectly placed.

Compressed gases versus liquefied gases

Compressed nonliquefied gases are stored entirely as gas under high pressure. As gas leaves the cylinder, pressure falls. For gases such as oxygen, medical air, and helium, cylinder pressure therefore helps estimate the remaining supply.

Liquefied gases are stored partly as liquid. Their pressure may remain relatively stable while liquid remains in the cylinder, so pressure alone does not reliably tell you how much product is left. The lesson uses nitrous oxide and carbon dioxide to illustrate this distinction.

Cylinder markings and safety features

The shoulder of a cylinder carries information about construction, service pressure, and testing. Hydrostatic testing evaluates whether a compressed-gas cylinder can continue to safely withstand pressure. Pressure-relief devices are designed to reduce the risk of catastrophic rupture if pressure becomes excessive.

PISS and DISS prevent wrong-gas connections

The Pin Index Safety System (PISS) uses gas-specific pin positions on small cylinder yokes. The Diameter Index Safety System (DISS) uses gas-specific threaded connections and is associated in this lesson with large cylinders and wall-source connections. The purpose is the same: make an incorrect gas connection physically difficult or impossible.

Common beginner mistake: Treating PISS and DISS as memorization-only trivia. On the RTE, the more important question is whether you can recognize a mismatch and prevent the wrong gas or wrong connection from reaching the patient.

Clinical example: choosing a home source

A patient needs continuous low-flow oxygen at home and experiences occasional power outages. An oxygen concentrator may provide the routine supply, but electricity is a system dependency. A backup compressed-gas cylinder provides an alternate source when the concentrator cannot operate. The decision is not simply “which device is cheapest?” It is “which system can reliably supply the ordered therapy in the patient's real environment?”

A regulator changes pressure; a flowmeter controls or measures flow

Compressed cylinders contain gas at pressures far above what respiratory equipment or patients can safely receive. The regulator reduces cylinder pressure to a usable working pressure. A flowmeter then controls or indicates the amount of gas moving through the system, usually in liters per minute (L/min).

Thorpe tube flowmeter

A Thorpe tube is a variable-orifice flowmeter with a float inside a tapered transparent tube. The lesson emphasizes one critical operating rule: it must remain vertical. Gravity is part of the measurement system. If the tube is tilted, the float position no longer corresponds reliably to the calibrated scale.

Read the float at the correct reference point for its shape according to the device markings. Do not “correct” a questionable flow by eye. Return the meter to its proper orientation and verify.

Bourdon gauge flowmeter

A Bourdon-type flow device estimates flow from pressure acting on a mechanical element. Because it is pressure-based, downstream resistance can make the indicated value misleading. A kinked tube or obstructed cannula can create back pressure, so the gauge may appear to show flow that is not actually reaching the patient.

Safe cylinder setup

  1. Confirm the gas label and cylinder condition.
  2. Secure the cylinder. A compressed-gas cylinder is stored energy and can become a projectile if the valve is damaged.
  3. Inspect the valve and connection. Keep oxygen connections free of oil, grease, and hydrocarbon contamination.
  4. Use the correct gas-specific regulator or connection.
  5. Follow the lesson/facility procedure for briefly clearing the valve before regulator attachment when applicable.
  6. Attach and tighten the regulator correctly.
  7. Open the cylinder and check pressure.
  8. Set the ordered flow and verify the flowmeter is positioned/read correctly.
  9. Check tubing and the patient interface for leaks, kinks, or obstruction.
  10. Reassess the patient response.

Duration is a planning calculation, not an afterthought

A portable cylinder may look “mostly full” but still be inadequate for a long transport, procedure, or delay away from wall oxygen. The therapist should calculate before leaving the reliable source.

Cylinder duration (minutes) = Cylinder pressure (psig) × Cylinder factor ÷ Flow (L/min)

The lesson uses these cylinder factors: D = 0.16, E = 0.28, G = 2.41, H/K = 3.14. First identify the cylinder size. A correct formula with the wrong factor still gives a wrong answer.

Worked example

An E cylinder reads 1,600 psig. Oxygen is flowing at 4 L/min.

1,600 psig × 0.28 L/psig ÷ 4 L/min = 112 minutes

The calculation means that under the assumptions of the formula, the cylinder provides approximately 112 minutes at that flow. The clinical question is not “Did I get 112?” It is “Is 112 minutes safely longer than the entire time I expect to be away from a dependable oxygen source?”

Transport requires a time budget

Include travel to the destination, waiting time, the procedure itself, return travel, and a reserve for delays or increased oxygen need. A cylinder whose calculated duration barely equals the planned trip is not a robust plan.

RTE scenario reasoning

An E cylinder reads 1,100 psig and the patient needs 4 L/min. The calculated duration is:

1,100 × 0.28 ÷ 4 = 77 minutes

If the planned time away from wall oxygen is approximately 90 minutes, the supply is inadequate. The correct clinical action is to obtain a fuller or additional appropriate supply before leaving.

Common mistakes: using the wrong cylinder factor, leaving the factor out entirely, forgetting the flow is in L/min, or comparing duration only with travel time while ignoring the procedure and return trip.

Troubleshoot the system, but stabilize the patient first

Equipment troubleshooting is clinical reasoning. A useful sequence is patient → source → pressure → regulator → flowmeter → tubing → interface → response. If the patient is unstable, restore a known working supply first and continue the diagnostic sequence after oxygen delivery is protected.

Common system failures

  • Hissing regulator connection: suspect a leak, loose connection, washer, or O-ring problem.
  • Pressure falling faster than expected: suspect a significant leak or regulator/system failure.
  • Flowmeter reads zero: verify source pressure, open controls, tubing, and regulator function.
  • Wall outlet has no flow: consider central-supply, zone-valve, or outlet failure and move the patient to a portable source.
  • Oxygen concentrator alarm/no power: switch to backup cylinder supply immediately when the patient depends on oxygen.
  • LOX connection frosting during fill: consider liquid oxygen leakage; disconnect safely, avoid contact with frosted surfaces, and evaluate the equipment before retrying.

Central supply failure changes the scale of the problem

A wall outlet problem may affect one outlet, one zone, or a larger system. If a central supply alarm activates or wall gas is unavailable, the therapist may need to place affected patients on portable cylinders while the facility response proceeds. The clinical priority is continuity of respiratory support, not waiting for engineering to finish diagnosing the pipeline.

Emergency pattern: During transport, a regulator begins hissing, delivered flow drops, and the patient's SpO2 falls. Do not “turn the flow higher” to compensate for a leaking system. Move to the nearest reliable oxygen source, support the patient, notify the team, and replace the defective equipment before continuing.

Do not trust the equipment more than the patient

A gauge can fail. A line can kink. A connection can leak. If a patient on “adequate” indicated flow becomes cyanotic, confused, tachypneic, or hypoxemic, verify actual delivery and the patient's condition. A normal-looking gauge does not prove oxygen is reaching the airway.

Specialty gases require the right system, not just the right cylinder

The lesson introduces gases such as helium/oxygen mixtures, nitric oxide, nitrous oxide, and carbon dioxide to show that medical gases differ in physical properties, clinical purpose, storage behavior, and delivery requirements. A gas can be correctly identified and still be unsafe if the wrong delivery system is used.

Helium and heliox

Helium is much less dense than air or oxygen. In a helium-oxygen mixture, lower density can reduce turbulent resistance in selected severe airway-obstruction situations. Equipment readings may require correction because devices calibrated for oxygen or air may not indicate heliox flow accurately.

Nitric oxide

Inhaled nitric oxide is a selective pulmonary vasodilator delivered through a dedicated blending/monitoring system. The source gas, delivered concentration, oxygen delivery, and nitrogen dioxide byproduct monitoring are part of safe administration.

Nitrous oxide and carbon dioxide

The lesson uses nitrous oxide to reinforce the concept of a liquefied gas: cylinder pressure does not fall in direct proportion to remaining content while liquid remains. Nitrous oxide also should not be delivered without oxygen. Carbon dioxide is used in selected procedural or testing applications and can displace oxygen in confined spaces.

RTE distinction: “Which gas is ordered?” is only the first question. The deeper question is whether the equipment can blend, measure, monitor, and safely deliver that gas under the patient's current conditions.
Putting C5 together
Identify the specialty gas and its purpose, understand how its physical properties affect storage and measurement, verify gas-specific delivery and monitoring equipment, and ensure the therapy can be safely supported before administration begins.

Medical gas systems: make the invisible supply chain visible

When oxygen reaches a patient, several things had to go right first: the correct gas was selected, the source was adequate, the connection matched, pressure was reduced safely, flow was controlled accurately, tubing remained patent, and sufficient reserve was available. The RTE can test any link in that chain.

patient need → gas identity → source → connector → regulator → flowmeter → duration → patient delivery → response → backup/escalation

RTE Depth of Clinical Judgment: take the next step

Depth questions are not satisfied by naming a cylinder or repeating a formula. Be prepared to work through a patient/equipment problem.

  • Information Gathering: identify the patient's prescribed flow, current oxygenation, cylinder size, pressure, source type, anticipated transport time, backup supply, tubing condition, connection status, and reliability of the displayed flow.
  • Information Gathering: recognize which additional observation will distinguish a depleted source from a regulator failure, leak, downstream obstruction, or central-supply problem.
  • Decision Making: decide whether a cylinder has enough reserve for a transport before leaving wall oxygen.
  • Decision Making: switch to a known working source when a patient deteriorates during a gas-delivery failure rather than continuing to troubleshoot a compromised system in place.
  • Decision Making: determine when a home concentrator needs backup cylinder support because electrical failure would interrupt therapy.
  • Decision Making: recognize when a specialty gas requires dedicated blending, monitoring, or measurement considerations before administration.
  • Reassessment: verify that the correction restored actual gas delivery and improved the patient's oxygenation/clinical status.
  • Communication: report the failed component, patient effect, replacement/backup action, and any need to delay transport or escalate facility response.
