CPAP vs BiPAP — Equipment and Settings

HIGH-YIELD EQUIPMENT DISTINCTION
CPAP
Continuous positive airway pressure. One pressure level is maintained throughout inspiration and exhalation. Equipment goal: splint open the airway or alveoli, improve oxygenation, and reduce preload/afterload in pulmonary edema. Settings usually include CPAP pressure and FiO2 or oxygen bleed-in.
BiPAP / Bilevel
Two pressure levels: IPAP during inspiration and EPAP during exhalation. Pressure support equals IPAP − EPAP. Equipment goal: improve ventilation by assisting inspiration while also supporting oxygenation with EPAP. Settings may include IPAP, EPAP, FiO2, backup rate, rise time, and alarms.
Quick Rule: CPAP mainly supports oxygenation/airway splinting. BiPAP supports oxygenation and ventilation because IPAP creates pressure support.

When to Use NIV

BiPAP-Focused Indications
COPD exacerbation with hypercapnia · hypoventilation · increased work of breathing with ventilatory failure · selected neuromuscular or obesity hypoventilation situations when appropriate.
CPAP-Focused Indications
Obstructive sleep apnea · acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema · oxygenation support when a single continuous pressure is sufficient · alveolar recruitment without needing inspiratory pressure support.
Post-Extubation or Prevention Use
Planned post-extubation support for selected high-risk patients · bridge therapy while avoiding intubation when contraindications are absent · adjunct to improve oxygenation while closely monitoring for failure.

Contraindications and Precautions

STOP BEFORE SETUP
  • Cannot protect airway: decreased consciousness, absent gag/cough, active vomiting, or high aspiration risk.
  • Facial trauma, burns, or recent facial/upper airway surgery: mask cannot seal safely or may worsen injury.
  • Cardiac or respiratory arrest: patient needs immediate invasive airway/resuscitation support, not NIV trial.
  • Severe hemodynamic instability: positive pressure may worsen venous return and instability.
  • Untreated pneumothorax: positive pressure can worsen air leak/tension risk.
  • Copious secretions or inability to clear secretions: mask prevents effective clearance and increases aspiration risk.
  • Severe agitation or intolerance: repeated mask removal prevents therapy and delays escalation.

NIV Interface Selection

Oronasal / Full-Face Mask
Most common for acute care NIV. Covers nose and mouth, helpful for mouth breathers and higher pressures. Risks: claustrophobia, skin breakdown at nasal bridge, aspiration risk if vomiting.
Nasal Mask
Useful for stable patients, OSA, chronic support, or lower pressures. Requires patient to keep mouth closed or use chin support. Less useful during acute distress with mouth breathing.
Total Face Mask
Covers entire face and can reduce pressure on nasal bridge. Useful when nasal bridge breakdown occurs or seal is difficult. Watch dead space, comfort, and eye irritation.
Helmet NIV
Not available everywhere. Can improve tolerance and reduce facial pressure injury. Requires correct setup, adequate flow, and careful CO2 washout monitoring.

Circuit Safety: Exhalation Ports and Leak Pathways

CRITICAL SAFETY CHECK

Many NIV circuits are single-limb circuits. Exhaled gas must leave the system through an intentional leak or exhalation port. If that pathway is missing, blocked, taped over, or connected incorrectly, the patient can rebreathe CO2.

Required Components
NIV device · single-limb or dual-limb circuit · correct mask/interface · exhalation port or leak valve · oxygen adapter/bleed-in if needed · filter per policy · humidification if ordered.
Safety Verification
Confirm exhalation port is present and open · do not cover intentional leak · confirm mask type matches device/circuit · confirm oxygen source and flow · check alarms and backup battery if available.
Unsafe Setup
No exhalation port · anti-asphyxia valve blocked · oxygen tubing connected to wrong port · mask strapped too tightly · excessive water in tubing · filter saturated or occluded.

NIV Setup Workflow

  1. Verify order, patient identity, indication, and contraindications.
  2. Select device and mode: CPAP, BiPAP S, BiPAP S/T, or facility-specific NIV mode.
  3. Choose interface based on acuity, mouth breathing, skin condition, and tolerance.
  4. Assemble circuit and verify exhalation port or leak valve is present and unobstructed.
  5. Connect oxygen source or set FiO2 as ordered; add humidification if ordered or needed.
  6. Enter initial settings: CPAP or IPAP/EPAP, backup rate if ordered, FiO2/O2 flow, alarms.
  7. Place mask gently, start flow/pressure, then adjust straps only enough to control leak.
  8. Coach patient: breathe with the machine, use call light, do not remove mask without notifying staff.
  9. Assess leak, exhaled VT, RR, SpO2, comfort, skin pressure points, and synchrony.
  10. Reassess within minutes and again after ordered interval; obtain ABG/ETCO2 data if indicated.

Monitoring During NIV

Respiratory Response
RR should decrease · work of breathing should improve · accessory muscle use should lessen · patient should appear less distressed · exhaled VT should be adequate for size/condition.
Oxygenation/Ventilation Data
SpO2 trend · FiO2/O2 bleed-in requirement · ETCO2 if used · ABG trend, especially pH and PaCO2 for BiPAP/hypercapnic failure.
Equipment Performance
Mask leak · delivered pressures · exhaled tidal volume · alarm frequency · circuit condensation · filter resistance · skin pressure areas.
Safety and Tolerance
Mental status · agitation · vomiting/nausea · abdominal distention · hypotension · facial pressure injury · ability to remove mask or call for help.

Troubleshooting Common NIV Problems

  • Mask leak: Reposition mask first. Avoid overtightening because it worsens discomfort and skin injury. Check size, cushion seal, facial hair, dentures, and tubing pull.
  • Persistent hypoxemia: Check O2 connection/FiO2, mask seal, EPAP/CPAP, patient position, secretions, and underlying disease progression.
  • Persistent hypercapnia: Check leak, exhaled VT, IPAP/pressure support, respiratory rate, mental status, and ABG trend. Escalate if CO2 or pH worsens.
  • Gastric insufflation: Pressure may be too high or patient may be swallowing air. Watch for distention/vomiting/aspiration risk.
  • Dryness or thick secretions: Consider heated humidification if compatible with device and ordered/policy-supported.
  • Condensation: Drain away from patient and device. Check humidifier temperature and circuit position.
  • Alarm fatigue: Do not silence alarms without correcting cause. Check leak, disconnects, apnea events, and pressure delivery.

Recognizing NIV Failure

DO NOT DELAY ESCALATION
Clinical Failure Signs
Worsening distress · RR remains very high or rising · accessory muscles/diaphoresis · worsening mental status · inability to tolerate mask · vomiting/aspiration risk.
Gas Exchange Failure
SpO2 remains low despite oxygen/pressure adjustments · PaCO2 rising · pH worsening · ETCO2 rising with fatigue · poor exhaled VT despite adequate seal.
RT Action
Assess and troubleshoot quickly. If failure persists, notify provider, prepare for escalation, and ensure intubation/BVM equipment is available. NIV should not delay needed intubation.

Summary & Board-Prep Reminders

CPAP vs BiPAP
CPAP = one pressure, mainly oxygenation/airway splinting. BiPAP = IPAP and EPAP; pressure support = IPAP − EPAP and supports ventilation.
Circuit Safety
Single-limb NIV circuits require an exhalation port or intentional leak. Missing or blocked leak pathways can cause CO2 rebreathing.
Troubleshooting
Correct mask leak before changing settings. Reposition and resize before overtightening. Monitor exhaled VT, RR, WOB, comfort, and skin.
Failure Signs
Worsening mental status, rising CO2, worsening pH, persistent severe distress, vomiting, or inability to tolerate mask means escalate. NIV must not delay needed intubation.
