Airway Management Equipment & Procedures

Airway management is one of the most urgent and high-stakes skills in respiratory care. From the jaw thrust to capnography confirmation to recognizing right mainstem intubation — this lesson covers every device, maneuver, and decision point the RT encounters at the bedside and on the NBRC.

Upper Airway Anatomy — Clinical Pearls

VERY HIGH-YIELD — ADULT VS PEDIATRIC DIFFERENCE IS NBRC TESTED
ADULTS
Narrowest airway point = GLOTTIS (vocal cords)

Natural airway: nasopharynx or oropharynx → pharynx → larynx → glottis → trachea → mainstem bronchi

Key laryngeal structures: thyroid cartilage (Adam's apple) · cricoid cartilage (ring below thyroid) · epiglottis (guards glottis)
PEDIATRICS
Narrowest airway point = CRICOID CARTILAGE

Clinical impact: traditionally, uncuffed ETTs were used in young children because the snug fit at the cricoid provides a natural seal. This distinction affects ETT selection and sizing in pediatrics.

In infants, the airway is also more anterior and cephalad — important for visualization during intubation.
Anatomy Key Terms: Glottis — the space between the vocal cords; the opening the ETT must pass through. Epiglottis — leaf-shaped cartilage that deflects food away from the airway during swallowing; the landmark for laryngoscope blade placement. Cricothyroid membrane — the soft tissue between the thyroid and cricoid cartilages; the site for emergency surgical airway access (cricothyrotomy).

Airway Assessment & Mallampati Classification

VERY HIGH-YIELD — MALLAMPATI IS NBRC TESTED

Difficult Airway Predictors

Anatomic Predictors
Small mouth opening · Micrognathia (small jaw) · Overbite / buck teeth · Short neck · Neck immobility · Thyromental distance <6 cm · Mallampati Class III–IV
History Predictors
Prior documented difficult intubation (most predictive) · Obstructive sleep apnea · Radiation to neck/airway · Prior airway surgery or trauma · Rheumatoid arthritis (cervical instability) · Morbid obesity
Pathologic Predictors
Airway edema (anaphylaxis, angioedema, burns, LTB) · Epiglottitis · Airway tumor or mass · Trauma with blood/swelling · Ludwig's angina · Airway foreign body

Mallampati Classification

Performed with patient sitting upright, mouth wide open, tongue fully protruded, NO phonation. Grades the visibility of oropharyngeal structures and predicts intubation difficulty.

Class I
Full soft palate, uvula, tonsillar pillars, and fauces all clearly visible
Easy Intubation
Class II
Soft palate and upper portion of uvula visible; lower tonsillar pillars obstructed by tongue
Mild Difficulty
Class III
Only base of uvula and soft palate visible; uvula tip and tonsillar pillars NOT seen
Difficult
Class IV
Only hard palate visible; soft palate completely obstructed by tongue
Very Difficult

Airway Opening Maneuvers

VERY HIGH-YIELD — JAW THRUST FOR C-SPINE IS NBRC ESSENTIAL

The most basic airway management is a manual maneuver to open the airway by aligning the mouth, pharynx, and laryngeal axes — eliminating the soft-tissue obstruction created by a relaxed tongue falling posteriorly.

Head-Tilt Chin-Lift

Use when: Unconscious patient with NO suspected cervical spine injury. Most common first-line airway opening maneuver in non-trauma scenarios.

Technique: Place one hand on the patient's forehead and tilt the head back. Place two fingers of the other hand under the bony part of the chin and lift upward. This extends the neck, lifting the tongue off the posterior pharynx.

Contraindicated: Any suspected cervical spine injury — extension can cause spinal cord damage.

Jaw Thrust

Use when: Suspected or confirmed cervical spine injury (trauma, fall, MVA, diving injury). Maintains cervical neutrality while still opening the airway.

Technique: Place fingers under both sides of the posterior mandible (jaw angles). Without tilting or extending the neck, thrust/lift the jaw forward. The mandible moves anteriorly, pulling the tongue forward with it — opening the airway WITHOUT neck movement.

Safe for C-spine: No neck extension required.

Sniffing Position: Used to optimize visualization during intubation — not for basic rescue airway opening. Involves neck flexion (towel under occiput) with head extension, aligning the oral, pharyngeal, and laryngeal axes for the best laryngoscopic view. Contraindicated with unstable cervical spine.

Mask Ventilation — E-C Clamp Technique

HIGH-YIELD — E-C CLAMP IS CORE SKILL
E-C Clamp Technique
C-grip: Thumb and index finger form a "C" shape, holding the mask to the face. E-grip: Remaining three fingers (3, 4, 5) form an "E" along the bony mandible — avoiding soft tissue to prevent airway compression. This technique creates a mask seal with one hand while the other squeezes the bag.
Proper Mask Fit
The mask should cover: bridge of nose (superior seal point) to the space between lower lip and chin (inferior seal). A properly sized mask minimizes dead space while maintaining a complete seal. Mask should NOT compress the nose bridge — this obstructs nares.
Troubleshooting
Poor chest rise → improve mask seal (two-person technique helps). Gastric distention → reduce squeeze force; ventilate gently. Beard or facial hair → gel or additional seal pressure. Facial trauma/anatomy → try different mask size; consider LMA. Always use two-person technique when seal is difficult.

Manual Resuscitators

VERY HIGH-YIELD — T-PIECE FOR NEONATES IS NBRC TESTED
Self-Inflating Bag (BVM)
Reinflates automatically after each squeeze without a gas source — can deliver room air if O₂ disconnected. With O₂ and reservoir: delivers near-100% FiO₂. Components: bag · one-way patient valve · reservoir bag · O₂ inlet · pop-off valve (limits pressure). Most common manual resuscitator in clinical use.
Flow-Inflating Bag (Anesthesia Bag)
Inflates ONLY with continuous gas flow — collapses if disconnected from gas source. Requires constant flow and a seal to stay inflated. More sensitive feedback for the operator — allows estimation of compliance by feel. No gas flow = no bag = cannot ventilate. Used in OR and by experienced practitioners.
T-Piece Resuscitator (Neonatal)
Preferred for neonatal resuscitation. Delivers controlled PIP (peak inspiratory pressure) and PEEP via a T-piece with adjustable controls — the operator simply occludes/releases the vent hole. Provides consistent pressures unlike squeezing a bag (which varies). Set PIP and PEEP precisely. Do NOT squeeze — just occlude/release.

Oropharyngeal Airway (OPA)

VERY HIGH-YIELD — CONTRAINDICATION IS MOST TESTED FACT
Indications
Unconscious patient with absent gag reflex · Assists mask ventilation by maintaining tongue position · Bite block for intubated patients (prevents biting on ETT) · During CPR to facilitate mask ventilation
⚠️ Contraindications
Gag reflex intact — stimulating the OPA in a conscious or semi-conscious patient causes gagging and vomiting → aspiration risk. Conscious patient — will not tolerate and will expel OPA. If patient responds to placement by gagging: remove immediately.

Sizing & Insertion

Sizing
Measure from: corner of the mouth → angle of the jaw (earlobe). Too small: OPA does not reach the base of tongue — ineffective. Too large: tip may push tongue back further or cause laryngospasm at the laryngeal inlet. Correct size: flange sits at the lips.
Insertion Technique
1. Open mouth with chin-lift or cross-fingers technique. 2. Insert OPA UPSIDE DOWN (curved end toward palate). 3. Advance until soft palate resistance is felt. 4. Rotate 180° (now curved end faces tongue). 5. Advance until flange rests against patient's lips. The rotation avoids dragging the tongue backward during insertion.

Clinical Check: OPA vs NPA Selection

A 44-year-old male is found unresponsive after a suspected opioid overdose. He is breathing spontaneously with loud snoring respirations. When the RT sternal rubs the patient, he moans and withdraws — he has a positive gag reflex. Which airway adjunct is most appropriate?

Nasopharyngeal Airway (NPA) — "Nasal Trumpet"

Indications
Conscious or semi-conscious patient with INTACT gag reflex · Soft-tissue upper airway obstruction when OPA is contraindicated · Frequent nasotracheal suctioning (prevents nasal trauma) · Trismus (patient cannot open mouth for OPA) · Facial injury where oral route is impractical
Contraindications
Severe epistaxis or coagulopathy (bleeding into airway) · Mid-face or nasal fractures · Basilar skull fracture (NPA could enter cranial vault) · Posterior pharyngeal injury or known obstruction
Sizing & Insertion
Measure from: tip of nose → earlobe. Lubricate generously before insertion. Select the LARGER nare. Insert parallel to the floor of the nasal cavity (NOT upward toward the brain). Advance gently with a rotating motion. Confirm flange rests at the nare. If resistance: do NOT force — redirect or try other nare.

Supraglottic Airways — LMA & Combitube

HIGH-YIELD — EMERGENCY RESCUE DEVICES
Laryngeal Mask Airway (LMA)
Blind insertion — sits in the hypopharynx and covers the laryngeal inlet with an inflatable cuff. Does not require laryngoscopy or direct vocal cord visualization.

Advantages: Rapid insertion · Minimal manipulation · Works when direct laryngoscopy fails · Used in "can't intubate, can't ventilate" scenarios as rescue bridge.

Contraindications: Conscious patient with gag reflex · Non-fasted patient (aspiration risk) · Poor pulmonary compliance (LMA seals only to ~20 cmH₂O pressure). NOT a definitive airway — does not protect against aspiration.

Fastrach (Intubating LMA): Specialized LMA that facilitates blind or guided ETT placement through the LMA — used in difficult airway scenarios.
Combitube
Double-lumen, double-cuff tube inserted blindly. Two balloon cuffs seal the pharynx and either the esophagus or trachea. The operator then ventilates through the correct lumen based on which position the tube landed (esophageal vs tracheal placement).

Advantages: Works whether tube is in trachea OR esophagus · Used in EMS/emergency situations where laryngoscopy is impossible or trained personnel are limited.

Contraindications: Intact gag reflex · Esophageal disease or trauma · Height <4 ft (sizing issue). Like LMA, does NOT fully protect against aspiration.

Endotracheal Tube (ETT)

VERY HIGH-YIELD — GOLD STANDARD DEFINITIVE AIRWAY

The ETT is the gold standard for airway protection — it sits in the trachea with a cuffed seal, protecting the lungs from aspiration and enabling full mechanical ventilation. It is the definitive airway for patients who require prolonged ventilatory support.

ETT Components
Cuff — inflatable balloon that seals the trachea; prevents aspiration and air leak. Pilot balloon — external balloon connected to cuff; indicates inflation status. Murphy eye — side port near the tip; provides backup airflow if tip is occluded against carina or airway wall. Adapter — 15mm connector to ventilator or BVM. Centimeter markings — indicate depth at lips; adult placement: 21–23 cm at teeth (females), 23–25 cm (males).
Indications for Intubation
Respiratory failure requiring ventilatory support · Airway protection (loss of consciousness, aspiration risk) · Secretion removal (cannot clear independently) · Bronchoscopy access · Anticipated clinical deterioration requiring definitive airway before loss of protective reflexes
Complications
Traumatic: Tooth damage · Vocal cord injury · Lip/tongue laceration · Laryngotracheal injury · Esophageal intubation. Cardiopulmonary: Hypoxia during attempt · Tachycardia/bradycardia (vagal stimulation) · Bronchospasm · Right mainstem intubation (most common misplacement).

ETT Cuff Pressure — Critical Range

Too Low (<20 cmH₂O)

Air leak around cuff during ventilation · Inadequate tidal volume delivered · Risk of aspiration of oral/gastric secretions into lungs · Cuff may herniate past cords if severely underinflated

TARGET RANGE

20–30
cmH₂O

Check every 8–12 hours. Use a cuff manometer — never inflate by feel alone.

Too High (>30 cmH₂O)

Mucosal ischemia — impairs tracheal blood flow · Progressive tracheal injury (ulceration, necrosis) · Long-term risk: tracheal stenosis · May cause pressure necrosis of tracheal cartilage · Reduce to target range immediately

Intubation Equipment

HIGH-YIELD — BLADE TYPES ARE TESTED

Laryngoscope Blades

Miller Blade (Straight)

The blade tip is placed DIRECTLY UNDER the epiglottis, lifting it upward to reveal the glottis. Provides direct epiglottic manipulation. Often preferred in infants/children and patients with floppy epiglottis or anterior airway. Provides direct visualization when anatomy makes vallecula placement difficult.

Macintosh Blade (Curved)

The blade tip is placed in the VALLECULA (the space between the tongue base and epiglottis). The blade pushes the hyoepiglottic ligament, which lifts the epiglottis INDIRECTLY. Most commonly used in adults. The curve provides more space in the oral cavity for the ETT to pass around the blade.

Stylets & Bougie
Standard stylet: Rigid wire inside ETT to shape it for intubation. Must not protrude beyond ETT tip. Bougie (Eschmann): Thin, flexible, semi-rigid guide that is passed into the trachea first when glottic view is poor, then the ETT is railroad over it. "Tactile" clicks confirm tracheal placement as it bounces along tracheal rings.
Video Laryngoscopy — GlideScope
Camera at the blade tip provides a video image of the glottis on a screen — dramatically improves view in difficult airways without requiring direct line-of-sight. The GlideScope and similar devices (C-MAC, McGrath) are now standard in most emergency airway kits. Particularly useful when standard laryngoscopy fails.
Fiberoptic Bronchoscopy
Gold standard for difficult airway management — particularly for patients with unstable cervical spine (awake fiberoptic intubation with topical anesthesia) or known difficult anatomy. The bronchoscope is passed through the ETT and used to navigate to the trachea under direct vision. Also the most definitive visual confirmation of tracheal placement.

Confirmation of Endotracheal Tube Placement

VERY HIGH-YIELD — FASTEST VS MOST RELIABLE IS NBRC TESTED
⭐ Capnography — FASTEST
Real-time, breath-by-breath CO₂ waveform from the exhaled gas. Tracheal placement: CO₂ detected with each breath. Esophageal placement: CO₂ waveform absent (no CO₂ in the esophagus). Most sensitive and specific for immediate confirmation. Continuous monitoring maintains ongoing confirmation throughout ventilation.
📋 Chest X-ray — MOST RELIABLE
Confirms exact tip position relative to anatomy. Ideal position: 2–5 cm above the carina. Detects right mainstem intubation, too-high position (above cords), and other misplacements. NOT a real-time method — requires time to obtain and interpret. Most comprehensive and reliable overall.

⭐ Right Mainstem Intubation — Most Common Misplacement
The right mainstem bronchus branches off the carina at a less acute angle than the left — ETTs advanced too far almost always enter the right mainstem. Clinical signs: decreased breath sounds on the LEFT side only (right lung ventilated, left lung not). Correction: deflate cuff, pull ETT back 1–2 cm, re-auscultate bilaterally, confirm with CXR. This is the most common post-intubation complication on the NBRC.

Specialized Airway Tubes

Double-Lumen Tube (DLT)
Two separate lumens allow independent ventilation of each lung (lung isolation). Indications: thoracic surgery (one-lung ventilation) · wedge resection · lung transplant · bronchopleural fistula · massive hemoptysis isolation. Requires careful positioning and confirmation — usually by bronchoscopy.
Hi-Lo Evac Tube
Standard cuffed ETT with an additional suction lumen above the cuff opening in the subglottic space. Continuously or intermittently suctions pooled secretions above the cuff — dramatically reduces micro-aspiration. Evidence-based VAP prevention measure. Indicated in patients expected to be intubated >48–72 hours.
Tube Exchanger / Changer
A long, thin, flexible guide used to safely exchange one ETT for another. Procedure: insert tube exchanger through existing ETT → remove old ETT over the exchanger → railroad new ETT over the exchanger → confirm placement → remove exchanger. Used when replacing damaged tubes or upsizing/downsizing without losing airway access.

Surgical Airways — Tracheostomy & Cricothyrotomy

VERY HIGH-YIELD — CRICOTHYROTOMY IS EMERGENCY LAST RESORT
🚨 Cricothyrotomy
Emergency surgical access through the cricothyroid membrane — the soft tissue between the thyroid and cricoid cartilages. Indicated in the "cannot intubate, cannot ventilate" emergency scenario after all other airway methods have failed.

A scalpel incision is made through the membrane and a tube is inserted to establish a temporary airway. This is the definitive emergency fallback when all non-surgical airway options have been exhausted.
🔵 Tracheostomy
Surgical opening in the anterior trachea (below the cricoid) for long-term artificial airway access. Performed when prolonged mechanical ventilation is anticipated (typically >10–14 days) or when the upper airway cannot be maintained.

Advantages over ETT: More comfortable for the patient · Less sedation needed · Enables oral communication with speaking valve · Lower VAP risk · Easier mouth care · Patient may be able to eat.

Tracheostomy Tube Components

Outer Cannula + Flange
The main tube body that sits in the tracheostomy stoma. The flange rests against the patient's neck and is secured with neck ties or holder. Never remove the outer cannula without guidance — the stoma may close rapidly.
Inner Cannula + Obturator
Inner cannula: removable liner inside the outer cannula — removed for cleaning and secretion clearing without removing the whole tube. Obturator: blunt-tipped guide used ONLY during insertion to prevent tracheal injury — REMOVED immediately after placement.
Fenestrated & Speaking Valves
Fenestrated trach: has holes in the posterior curve allowing airflow through the upper airway when the tube is plugged — enables speech and weaning trials. Tracheostomy button: maintains stoma patency after decannulation while allowing normal upper airway breathing. Passy-Muir valve (speaking valve): one-way valve allowing speech without tube removal.

Common NBRC Traps — Airway Management

  • OPA is contraindicated when the gag reflex is intact. The single most tested airway adjunct rule. Inserting an OPA in a conscious or semi-conscious patient causes gagging and vomiting → aspiration. If you're not sure, use NPA. Remove OPA immediately if gagging occurs.
  • Jaw thrust for suspected cervical spine injury — not head-tilt chin-lift. Head-tilt chin-lift extends the neck. Any neck extension with an unstable cervical spine risks spinal cord injury. Jaw thrust achieves airway opening without neck movement.
  • Capnography is the fastest confirmation method; chest X-ray is the most reliable. These two facts are tested as a pair constantly. Capnography confirms tracheal vs esophageal placement breath by breath. CXR tells you exactly where the tip is relative to the carina — but it's not real-time.
  • Right mainstem intubation is the most common ETT misplacement. Signs: decreased or absent breath sounds on the LEFT, while the right lung is ventilated normally. Action: pull back the ETT 1–2 cm, re-auscultate, confirm with CXR.
  • T-piece resuscitator is preferred for neonatal resuscitation. A self-inflating bag delivers variable pressures based on how hard the operator squeezes — dangerous in neonates where precise PIP and PEEP are critical. T-piece delivers preset PIP and PEEP consistently.
  • ETT cuff pressure target is 20–30 cmH₂O. Below 20: air leak and aspiration risk. Above 30: mucosal ischemia and tracheal stenosis. Check with a calibrated cuff manometer — not by feel.
  • LMA does not protect against aspiration. The LMA seals around the laryngeal inlet but does not enter the trachea — gastric contents can still reflux past the LMA into the lungs. It is a rescue device, not a definitive airway. ETT provides true aspiration protection.
  • Mallampati class III–IV predicts difficult intubation. Class I = easy (full uvula visible). Class IV = very difficult (hard palate only). Performed with patient upright, mouth open, tongue out, no phonation.

FAQ

Common Questions

When is the OPA safe to use vs. when must I use the NPA?

Use OPA when the patient has NO gag reflex — typically deeply unconscious, in cardiac arrest, or deeply sedated. The rule is simple: if the patient will tolerate an OPA without gagging, it can be used. If the patient has ANY gag reflex (responds to the device, coughs, retches), remove the OPA immediately and substitute the NPA. The NPA passes through the nose and nasopharynx — it bypasses the oropharyngeal gag reflex triggers and is well tolerated in semi-conscious patients. Clinical shortcut: if you're not certain — use NPA. It's safer.

Why is right mainstem intubation so common?

The anatomy of the tracheobronchial tree explains it. The carina (where the trachea divides into left and right mainstem bronchi) has an asymmetric branching angle. The right mainstem bronchus branches at approximately 25° from the tracheal axis, while the left branches at a steeper 45° angle. When an ETT is advanced past the carina — whether during initial placement or after a patient position change — it almost always continues straight into the right mainstem because it's the more direct pathway. Clinical signs are always the same: decreased or absent left breath sounds while the right side is being fully ventilated. Correction: pull back 1–2 cm and re-confirm bilaterally.

Why is T-piece preferred over BVM for neonatal resuscitation?

Neonatal lungs require very precise, controlled pressures. The self-inflating bag delivers pressure that varies directly with how hard the operator squeezes — this varies enormously between practitioners, between breaths, and with fatigue. Delivering too much pressure in a premature infant can cause pneumothorax or bronchopulmonary dysplasia; too little is ineffective. The T-piece resuscitator has preset PIP and PEEP controls — the operator simply covers and uncovers a hole with their thumb to deliver consistent, reproducible breaths at the preset pressures. This removes operator variability and provides the consistent pressures that fragile neonatal airways require.

What's the difference between the Miller and Macintosh laryngoscope blades?

Both blades are designed to displace the tongue and visualize the glottis, but they interact with the epiglottis differently. The Miller blade is straight — the tip is placed directly UNDER the epiglottis and lifts it straight up, providing direct view of the cords. The Mac blade is curved — the tip is placed in the vallecula (the groove between the tongue base and epiglottis) and the blade indirectly lifts the epiglottis by pulling on the hyoepiglottic ligament. Mac is the most commonly used blade in adult intubation; Miller is often preferred in infants and young children (whose epiglottis is more floppy and U-shaped) and patients with a flaccid or anterior epiglottis that falls back over the cords.

Summary & Board-Prep Reminders

Airway Adjunct Rules
OPA = no gag reflex · NPA = intact gag reflex · OPA sizing: corner of mouth → earlobe · NPA sizing: tip of nose → earlobe · OPA inserted upside down, rotate 180°
Mallampati Quick Reference
Class I = full uvula/pillars (easy) · Class II = partial uvula (mild) · Class III = base of uvula only (difficult) · Class IV = hard palate only (very difficult) · Exam: upright, mouth open, tongue out, no phonation
Confirmation Rules
Fastest = Capnography · Most reliable = CXR · Right mainstem = most common misplacement · Signs = absent LEFT breath sounds · Fix = pull back 1–2 cm
Critical Numbers
ETT cuff = 20–30 cmH₂O · Too low = leak + aspiration · Too high = mucosal ischemia · T-piece for neonates · Jaw thrust = C-spine precaution
NBRC Must-Know List: OPA = no gag · NPA = intact gag · Jaw thrust = C-spine · Head-tilt = no C-spine injury only · T-piece = neonates · Capnography = fastest confirmation · CXR = most reliable · Right mainstem = common complication · Cuff 20–30 cmH₂O · LMA = rescue, NOT definitive airway · Class IV Mallampati = very difficult intubation · Miller = straight, lifts epiglottis directly · Mac = curved, vallecula, indirect
