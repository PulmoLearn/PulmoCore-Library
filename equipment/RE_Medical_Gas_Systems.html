Medical Gases in Respiratory Care

Respiratory therapists are responsible for every aspect of gas delivery — from selecting the correct supply source to calculating how long a portable cylinder will last during a 45-minute transport. Oxygen is a drug: it requires an order, correct dosing (flow rate), and the right delivery system.

Central Supply vs. Portable
Most hospitals run oxygen through a centralized bulk liquid oxygen system piped to wall outlets throughout the building. Portable cylinders are used for transport, emergencies, and home oxygen. The RT must understand both.
Oxygen as a Medication
Oxygen requires a physician order, correct flow rate, delivery device, and monitoring. Giving too little causes hypoxemia. Giving too much in hypercapnic COPD patients can blunt respiratory drive. Dose matters.
Safety Is Non-Negotiable
Oxygen supports combustion — it doesn't burn itself, but accelerates burning of everything around it. Compressed cylinders store enormous energy. One unsecured H cylinder falling and shearing a valve can become a deadly projectile.

Medical Gas Types & Properties

Compressed (Nonliquefied) Gases

Stored entirely in gaseous form under high pressure. The cylinder pressure decreases proportionally as gas is used — so pressure is a direct indicator of remaining volume.

Oxygen (O₂)
Colorless, odorless, tasteless. Supports combustion — does not itself burn. 21% of atmospheric air. Medical grade ≥99%. Used for hypoxemia, aerosol therapy, HFNC, NIV, MV.
Medical Air
Compressed filtered room air — 21% oxygen, 78% nitrogen. Used when high FiO₂ is not desired (premature neonates) and to power ventilators and nebulizers without increasing O₂.
Helium (He)
Colorless, odorless, inert. Much less dense than air or O₂. Used in Heliox (He/O₂ blend) for severe airway obstruction to reduce turbulence and work of breathing. Also used in PFT testing.

Liquefied Gases

These gases have critical temperatures above room temperature, allowing them to be stored as liquids under moderate pressure. As liquid is released, it vaporizes. Cylinder pressure alone does not indicate remaining volume.

Nitric Oxide (NO)
Selective pulmonary vasodilator. Used for PPHN, hypoxic respiratory failure. Stored in compressed form; delivered by blending system. Must be monitored for NO₂ (toxic byproduct).
Nitrous Oxide (N₂O)
Anesthetic/analgesic gas. Light blue cylinder. Must always be administered with oxygen — never alone; risks hypoxia. Stored as liquid; pressure does not drop until liquid is depleted.
Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)
Heavier than air; gray cylinder. Used in pulmonary function calibration gases and some bronchoscopy procedures. Asphyxiation risk in confined spaces — ventilate area before use.
Key Distinction: For compressed gases (O₂, medical air, helium), cylinder pressure directly indicates remaining volume — a cylinder at 1,100 psig has exactly half the gas of one at 2,200 psig. For liquefied gases (N₂O, CO₂), pressure stays constant until all liquid is consumed. Duration calculations using the standard formula only apply to nonliquefied gases.

Oxygen Cylinder Equipment Overview

EQUIPMENT: Compressed Gas Cylinder
Cylinder Body
Seamless steel or aluminum construction. Must withstand service pressures up to 2,200+ psig. DOT-specification marked on shoulder. Aluminum is lighter — preferred for portable home oxygen units.
Cylinder Valve
Controls gas release. Has a hand wheel or post valve (small cylinders). Never force the valve — if it won't turn, do not use that cylinder. Protective cap must remain on when not in use.
Pressure Relief Devices
Prevent catastrophic failure by releasing gas when pressure exceeds safe limits. Two types: Frangible disk — ruptures once and cannot be reset. Fusible plug — melts at high temperatures (fires) to release pressure safely.

Cylinder Sizes & Cylinder Factors

MEMORIZE FOR NBRC CALCULATIONS

Cylinder size determines both portability and gas capacity. The cylinder factor converts cylinder pressure (psig) into liters of gas — it is essential for duration calculations.

CylinderCylinder FactorTypical UsePortabilityApprox. Full Pressure
D0.16Small portable; ambulance; neonatal transportPortable~2,000 psig
E0.28Standard hospital transport; most common portablePortable~2,000 psig
G2.41Home oxygen; large portable supplySemi-portable~2,000 psig
H / K3.14Hospital stationary backup; bulk departmental supplyNon-portable (cart required)~2,200 psig
E Cylinder — Know This One Cold
The E cylinder is the workhorse of hospital transport oxygen. Factor = 0.28. Full pressure ≈ 2,000 psig. Full cylinder holds ≈ 622 liters at atmospheric pressure. At 4 L/min, a full E cylinder lasts approximately 155 minutes.
H/K Cylinders — Stationary Backup
Factor = 3.14. These are the large, tall cylinders you see in oxygen storage areas. They hold approximately 6,900 liters at full pressure (2,200 psig). These are not portable and require a wheeled cart.
Memory Hook: D = 0.16 (D is smallest, smallest factor). E = 0.28 (next size up). G = 2.41 (big jump for G). H/K = 3.14 (think π — the largest factor for the largest common cylinder).

Bulk Oxygen Systems & Wall Outlets

Most hospital oxygen is supplied from a centralized bulk system — not from individual cylinders. Understanding this system helps the RT recognize supply failures and respond appropriately.

Bulk Liquid Oxygen System
A large insulated storage tank outside the hospital holds oxygen as a supercooled liquid. Vaporizers convert it to gas, which is distributed through the piped system throughout the building. Typically holds 7–14 days of supply.
Piped Distribution System
Zone valves control oxygen flow to sections of the hospital. Each zone can be shut off independently for emergencies. RTs must know the location of zone valves on their units — a failed zone valve can cut oxygen to an entire floor.
Alarm Systems
Pressure alarms alert when supply pressure falls below safe operating levels. A low-pressure alarm at the bedside or on the gas panel requires immediate investigation. Always have a backup cylinder available during supply alarms.

Wall Outlets

Wall outlets deliver piped oxygen, medical air, and vacuum (suction) to patient rooms. Each gas has a color-coded, gas-specific outlet that only accepts matching connectors — preventing wrong-gas errors.

🟢 Oxygen outlet
Green connector. DISS threaded connection. Delivers at ~50 psi working pressure.
🟡 Medical Air outlet
Yellow connector. Gas-specific DISS connection. Also ~50 psi. Cannot be used for oxygen equipment.
⚫ Vacuum / Suction outlet
White connector in most facilities. Powers bedside suction units. Connected to a central vacuum system.

Flowmeters

HIGH-YIELD EQUIPMENT SECTION

Flowmeters regulate and display the rate of gas delivery in liters per minute (L/min). There are three main types — each with different operating principles, advantages, and clinical applications. Selecting the wrong type for the situation leads to inaccurate flow delivery.

① Flow Restrictor
Fixed orifice creates a set resistance; flow rate is determined by inlet pressure (usually 50 psi from wall). No visible float or gauge — preset to a specific flow. Simple, inexpensive, disposable. Commonly bundled with disposable nebulizer kits.

Limitation: Cannot be adjusted after manufacture. One flow rate only.
② Bourdon Gauge Flowmeter
Pressure-based; reads flow indirectly from back-pressure. Does NOT depend on gravity — works accurately in any orientation (upright, horizontal, tilted). Ideal for transport and use on cylinder regulators.

Critical Limitation: Back pressure from the patient circuit (nasal cannula kinking, secretions in mask) causes the Bourdon gauge to read HIGHER than actual flow. An increased back pressure reading is a false high.
③ Thorpe Tube Flowmeter
A calibrated vertical glass or plastic tube with a floating ball. Flow rate is read at the center of the ball. Gravity-dependent: must remain perfectly vertical to read accurately. The most accurate flowmeter — standard for all wall oxygen outlets.

Critical Rule: A tilted Thorpe tube produces an inaccurate reading. The ball position is affected by gravity — tilt changes the reading even if flow hasn't changed.

Thorpe Tube Setup Procedure

  1. Connect flowmeter inlet to wall oxygen outlet (green DISS connector) — press and lock
  2. Verify the green indicator confirms oxygen source
  3. Attach patient delivery tubing to flowmeter outlet
  4. Turn the flow control valve counterclockwise to increase flow
  5. Read flow at the center of the float ball — not top, not bottom
  6. Verify the prescribed flow rate matches the order and the patient's response

Gas Cylinder Safety Systems

VERY HIGH-YIELD — PISS vs DISS

Safety systems prevent the wrong gas from being connected to the wrong equipment — a potentially fatal error if nitrogen or nitrous oxide were mistakenly connected to a patient's oxygen supply. Three systems work together to create multiple layers of protection.

🔵 PISS — Pin Index Safety System

Used with: Small cylinders — sizes A through E

How it works: Two pins on the yoke of the regulator align with specific holes drilled in the cylinder valve. Each gas has a unique pin arrangement — oxygen pins are in a different position than nitrous oxide, helium, or air. Mismatched cylinders simply will not seat on the wrong regulator.

Connection type: Yoke connection (no threading)

Clinical memory: PISS = Petite (small) cylinders = Pins in yoke

🟢 DISS — Diameter Index Safety System

Used with: Large cylinders (H, K) and all wall outlet connections

How it works: Threaded connections with gas-specific diameters. The body of the connector and the nipple diameter vary for each gas — physically preventing the wrong connection. A green oxygen DISS connector will not thread onto a yellow medical air outlet.

Connection type: Threaded — requires hand-tightening

Clinical memory: DISS = Big/Diameter = Large cylinders + wall outlets
ASSS (American Standard Safety System): The broader category that encompasses PISS and DISS. When you see "ASSS" on the NBRC, it refers to the overall framework — but the specific system you'll be asked to identify is either PISS (small cylinders) or DISS (large cylinders/walls).

Regulators

EQUIPMENT: Pressure-Reducing Regulator

A regulator reduces the high pressure inside a cylinder (up to 2,200 psig) to a safe, usable working pressure (~50 psig). Without a regulator, the raw cylinder pressure would be dangerous and impossible to meter accurately.

Pressure Gauge
Displays remaining cylinder pressure in psig. This is the number used in your duration calculations.
Inlet Connection
Connects to cylinder valve using PISS (small) or DISS (large). Gas-specific — will not fit wrong cylinder type.
Reducing Valve
Internal mechanism that drops high cylinder pressure to working pressure (~50 psi). The core function of the regulator.
Flow Outlet
Where the flowmeter or delivery device attaches. Delivers gas at 50 psi to the flowmeter, which then meters it to the patient.

Regulator Setup — Competency-Aligned Sequence

Assign each step its correct position in the sequence below.

Secure the cylinder in an upright position; verify the gas label matches the intended use
Remove the protective cap; inspect the valve opening for dirt, oil, or damage — do not use if oil is present
Crack the cylinder: briefly open the valve one quarter turn (pointed away from people) to clear debris, then close
Attach the regulator firmly to the cylinder valve; tighten securely — never use oil or grease on any connection
Open the cylinder valve slowly — one full turn, then continue gradually; listen for leaks
Verify cylinder pressure on the gauge; set prescribed flow rate; confirm delivery to patient device
Put all six setup steps in the correct order to continue.
NEVER: Use oil, grease, or petroleum-based lubricants on any oxygen equipment. Oxygen under pressure with hydrocarbon lubricants creates an extremely high risk of fire or explosion. Only approved oxygen-compatible materials may contact oxygen equipment.

Cylinder Transport Safety

An unsecured compressed gas cylinder is one of the most dangerous objects in a hospital. If a cylinder falls and the valve shears off, the cylinder becomes a high-velocity projectile capable of penetrating walls. Transport and storage safety is not optional.

Always Use Approved Carts
E cylinders travel on wheeled carts with cylinder chains or straps. Cylinders must be restrained at all times — even when parked briefly at a bedside. Never lean a cylinder against a wall unattended.
Never Drag or Roll Cylinders
Dragging damages the bottom of the cylinder and creates dangerous sparks. Cylinders are always transported upright on approved wheeled carts, never dragged, dropped, or carried unsecured.
Keep Away from Heat Sources
High temperatures increase cylinder pressure and can trigger pressure relief devices. Never store cylinders near heaters, steam pipes, or direct sunlight. Storage areas must be ventilated.
Protective Cap When Not in Use
The protective valve cap must be in place any time a regulator is not attached. The cap is the last line of defense against valve damage during storage and transport.
Clinical Pearl: When entering a patient room with a cylinder on a cart, always secure the cart brake first. An unsecured cart on a sloped floor can result in a fallen cylinder — one of the most preventable serious incidents in respiratory care.

Cylinder Duration Calculations

VERY HIGH-YIELD — NBRC CALCULATION SECTION

The most frequently tested math skill in respiratory care is calculating how long an oxygen cylinder will last. Every transport requires this calculation. Running a cylinder empty during transport is a sentinel event.

CYLINDER DURATION FORMULA
Duration (min) = Pressure (psig) × Cylinder Factor ÷ Flow Rate (L/min)
D (min) = P × F ÷ Q

Worked Examples

E Cylinder — Transport Planning
Pressure: 1,100 psig | Flow: 3 L/min
1,100 × 0.28 ÷ 3 = 102.7 min
Approximately 1 hour 42 minutes remaining.
H Cylinder — Backup Planning
Pressure: 2,000 psig | Flow: 10 L/min
2,000 × 3.14 ÷ 10 = 628 min
Approximately 10 hours 28 minutes remaining.
Safe Reserve Rule: Never plan a transport that uses more than 2/3 of the cylinder's remaining time. If the calculation shows 90 minutes, plan for a maximum 60-minute transport to maintain a safe reserve. Unexpected delays (elevator waits, patient deterioration, procedure extensions) are common.

Oxygen Concentrators

EQUIPMENT: Molecular Sieve Oxygen Concentrator

Oxygen concentrators extract oxygen from room air, eliminating the need for cylinder refills. They are the standard home oxygen delivery device for patients requiring long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT).

How They Work
Room air is drawn in by a compressor and passed through zeolite molecular sieve beds. The sieve selectively adsorbs nitrogen, allowing oxygen to pass through. The process alternates between two beds, providing continuous flow. Output: typically 90–95% oxygen purity.
Indications & Advantages
Home oxygen for COPD, pulmonary fibrosis, other chronic hypoxemia. Continuous oxygen supply without refills. No explosion/fire risk of compressed cylinders. Cost-effective over time for stable home patients requiring ≤5 L/min.
Limitations — Critical for NBRC
Requires continuous electricity — fails during power outages. Maximum flow typically limited to 5–6 L/min (concentrators can't meet high-flow needs). Purity decreases at higher flow settings. Patient must have backup cylinder supply for power outages.

Liquid Oxygen Systems

EQUIPMENT: Liquid Oxygen Reservoir & Portable Unit

Liquid oxygen (LOX) stores oxygen in supercooled liquid form at approximately -183°C (-297°F). Because liquid takes up far less space than gas, LOX systems store enormous volumes of oxygen in relatively small containers — making them ideal for active home patients and hospital bulk supply.

Stationary Reservoir
A large insulated thermos-like container (Dewar flask) that holds liquid oxygen. Kept at the patient's home. As the patient draws oxygen, liquid converts to gas through a vaporizer coil. Typical home reservoir holds the equivalent of 15,000–20,000 liters of gaseous oxygen.
Portable Refillable Unit
A smaller insulated unit the patient carries. Connected to the stationary reservoir to refill by liquid transfer — takes approximately 1–2 minutes. Much lighter than an equivalently sized compressed gas cylinder. Ideal for active patients who spend time outside the home.

Portable Unit Filling Procedure

  1. Verify the portable and stationary systems are compatible (same manufacturer preferred)
  2. Inspect fill port connectors — both must be clean and dry; no ice or frost on connections
  3. Connect the portable unit to the stationary reservoir fill port — align and engage
  4. Open the fill valve; liquid oxygen will transfer from stationary to portable
  5. Monitor the fill indicator — stop when the portable unit reaches capacity or the indicator shows full
  6. Close the fill valve; disconnect the portable unit carefully — expect brief venting of gas
  7. Check portable unit for frost buildup at the connection; inspect for leaks; allow brief equilibration

LOX Safety Hazards

  • Cryogenic burn (frost injury): Direct skin contact with LOX or LOX-frosted surfaces causes immediate freeze injury. Always handle with insulated gloves. If skin contact occurs, flush immediately with warm water and seek medical attention.
  • Oxygen enrichment and fire risk: Liquid oxygen vaporizes rapidly when spilled. Enriched oxygen concentrations in the surrounding air dramatically increase fire risk — any ignition source in the area becomes extremely dangerous. Ventilate immediately.
  • Pressure venting: LOX systems vent gas when the internal pressure rises (normal process). The vent gas is 100% oxygen — ensure the vent is directed away from ignition sources and people.

Escalation & RT Clinical Pearls

When to Escalate Oxygen Supply

  • Cylinder pressure dropping below safe transport reserve — switch cylinders before pressure reaches unsafe levels; never run to empty
  • Patient's oxygen demand increasing during transport — reassess; return to room if demand may exceed cylinder duration; notify provider
  • Concentrator failure or power outage — switch to backup cylinder immediately; notify home oxygen provider
  • Central supply alarm activation — go to backup cylinder for all patients on wall oxygen; evacuate if directed by facility protocol
  • Confirmed gas delivery failure (leak, broken regulator) — replace immediately; use alternate supply; assess patient immediately for hypoxemia

RT Clinical Pearls

  • Always crack the cylinder before attaching the regulator — this clears valve debris that could damage the regulator's inlet filter or cause it to seat improperly.
  • Oxygen supports combustion but does not itself burn — it dramatically accelerates burning of everything around it. Even brief oxygen enrichment near an ignition source is dangerous.
  • Never estimate cylinder duration — always calculate. A 30-minute underestimate during a long CT scan transport can strand a patient without oxygen.
  • Verify cylinder labels, not just color — color is a secondary check. A mislabeled or color-faded cylinder must be verified by direct label reading before use.
  • Thorpe tubes must remain vertical — even a slight tilt produces an inaccurate reading. On a cluttered bedside, verify position before reading flow.
  • The Bourdon gauge reads falsely high with back pressure — a kinked nasal cannula tubing increases back pressure, making the gauge appear to show higher flow than is actually reaching the patient.

Common NBRC Traps — Medical Gas Systems

  • Confusing PISS and DISS. PISS = Pin Index = Small cylinders (A–E). DISS = Diameter Index = large cylinders (H, K) and wall outlets. Mixing these up on the NBRC is the most common safety system error.
  • Using the wrong cylinder factor in a duration calculation. E = 0.28, not 0.16 (D) or 3.14 (H/K). Always identify the cylinder size first, then apply the matching factor. A wrong factor produces a wildly incorrect duration.
  • Forgetting to apply the cylinder factor entirely. Pressure ÷ Flow with no factor is a common shortcut error. The formula is always: Pressure × Factor ÷ Flow.
  • Relying on color coding alone to identify a gas. Color is a secondary identifier. Always read the label. Colors can fade, cylinders can be mislabeled, and international standards differ from US standards.
  • Using a tilted Thorpe tube and recording the reading. A tilted Thorpe tube gives an inaccurate flow reading. The correct reading requires a perfectly vertical tube.
  • Trusting a Bourdon gauge reading with back pressure present. If the delivery tubing is kinked or the patient's cannula is blocked, the Bourdon gauge over-reads flow. The patient may be receiving less oxygen than the gauge shows.
  • Allowing oil or grease contact with oxygen equipment. Any hydrocarbon contamination on oxygen equipment creates explosion risk. Even hand lotion on a connection is a hazard.
  • Planning a transport without calculating cylinder duration first. Running oxygen out during a transport is a sentinel event. The NBRC will present scenarios where the RT must calculate before committing to a transport plan.

FAQ

Common Questions

Why do we crack the cylinder before attaching the regulator?

Cracking the cylinder — briefly opening the valve one quarter turn before attaching the regulator — clears any dust, debris, or small particles that have accumulated in the valve opening. If these particles were drawn into the regulator during first use, they could damage the internal reducing valve, clog the inlet filter, or in extreme cases, cause ignition in an oxygen-enriched environment (particulates under high pressure can ignite). The crack is brief — open, then immediately close — and must be directed away from any people or ignition sources.

What is the difference between PISS and DISS, and how do I remember it?

PISS (Pin Index Safety System) uses two small pins on the regulator yoke that fit into gas-specific holes in the cylinder valve. It is used for small cylinders — sizes A through E. DISS (Diameter Index Safety System) uses gas-specific threaded connectors with different diameters — physically impossible to attach the wrong gas. DISS is used for large cylinders (H, K) and all wall outlet connections. Memory: PISS = Petite (small) = Pins. DISS = Diameter = big cylinders + walls.

Why must Thorpe tube flowmeters remain vertical?

The Thorpe tube works by balancing two opposing forces on the float ball: upward gas pressure pushing the ball up, and downward gravity pulling it down. The scale on the tube is calibrated assuming perfect vertical alignment. When tilted, gravity no longer acts straight down on the ball relative to the tube — the ball position no longer reflects actual gas flow. Even a 10–15° tilt can produce a meaningfully inaccurate reading. The Bourdon gauge flowmeter, by contrast, is pressure-based and works accurately in any position, making it the choice for transport.

Why can't oxygen equipment use oil or grease?

Oxygen under high pressure is a powerful oxidizer. When it contacts hydrocarbon-based lubricants (petroleum jelly, WD-40, machine grease, even some hand lotions) under pressure, the combination can ignite spontaneously — a phenomenon called pressure-initiated combustion or oil-oxygen fire. Even a small amount of lubricant at a high-pressure oxygen connection can create an explosion or flash fire. Only oxygen-compatible lubricants (specific silicone-based products approved for oxygen service) may be used, and only where specifically indicated. In most cases, no lubricant at all is required.

How does an oxygen concentrator work?

An oxygen concentrator uses a compressor to push room air through beds of zeolite — a material that selectively adsorbs nitrogen molecules. As nitrogen is captured by the zeolite, the gas passing through is increasingly enriched with oxygen. Most concentrators alternate between two zeolite beds: while one is adsorbing nitrogen (producing oxygen-enriched output), the other is purging (releasing the captured nitrogen back to room air and regenerating). The result is a continuous stream of 90–95% oxygen at flows up to 5–6 L/min, without any tank refills required.

When would I use liquid oxygen instead of a concentrator or cylinder?

Liquid oxygen is most useful for patients who: (1) require higher flows than a concentrator can reliably deliver at adequate purity, (2) are highly active and need a lightweight portable unit for extended time outside the home, or (3) are in locations where electricity is unreliable (liquid oxygen doesn't need power). The portable LOX unit is significantly lighter than an equivalently sized compressed cylinder, and refilling from the stationary reservoir takes under 2 minutes — making it practical for active patients. Its primary drawbacks are the cryogenic safety hazards, required supplier deliveries for the stationary reservoir, and the continuous slow evaporation loss (liquid slowly vents off even unused).

Summary & Board-Prep Reminders

Color Codes — Know All 7
O₂ = Green · Air = Yellow · N₂O = Light Blue · He = Brown · CO₂ = Gray · N₂ = Black · Heliox = Brown/Green. Color is secondary to label — always read the label.
Cylinder Factors — Memorize
D = 0.16 · E = 0.28 · G = 2.41 · H/K = 3.14 (π). Formula: Duration = Pressure × Factor ÷ Flow. Never estimate. Always apply the safe reserve rule.
PISS vs DISS
PISS = small cylinders (A–E) = pins in yoke. DISS = large cylinders (H, K) + all wall outlets = threaded connections. Both prevent wrong-gas connections.
Flowmeter Rules
Thorpe tube = vertical only = most accurate. Bourdon gauge = any position = transport choice (reads high with back pressure). Flow restrictor = fixed flow only.
NBRC High-Yield Checklist: Crack cylinder before regulator · No oil/grease ever · Verify label before color · Calculate duration before every transport · Thorpe tube must be vertical · PISS = E cylinder, DISS = H cylinder and wall outlets · Concentrators fail in power outages — always have backup cylinders
