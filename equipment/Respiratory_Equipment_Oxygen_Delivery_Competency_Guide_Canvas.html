Respiratory Equipment • Competency Guide

Medical Gas Therapy & Oxygen Delivery Devices

Learn how to select, set up, calculate, troubleshoot, and escalate oxygen-delivery therapy from low-flow devices through Venturi systems, high-flow nasal cannula, and heliox while matching the device to the patient's oxygenation and inspiratory demand.

Before you begin

Oxygen is a respiratory medication delivered through equipment. Choosing the device requires more than looking at the liters-per-minute setting. You must ask how much oxygen the patient needs, whether the device can meet the patient's inspiratory flow demand, whether the delivered fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) is stable or variable, and whether work of breathing or ventilatory failure requires something beyond oxygen alone.

The 2027 Respiratory Therapy Examination (RTE) can test this lesson at two levels. Breadth of Knowledge includes device ranges, operating principles, formulas, setup rules, indications, and hazards. Depth of Clinical Judgment asks whether you can gather the right patient and equipment information, recognize why oxygen therapy is failing, select the next device, and decide when oxygen must be escalated to noninvasive or invasive ventilatory support.

Big idea: Follow the chain patient oxygen need → inspiratory demand → device capability → FiO2/flow → setup → response → troubleshooting → escalation → reassessment.

Core vocabulary: fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂), low-flow system, high-flow system, inspiratory demand, nasal cannula, reservoir cannula, simple mask, partial rebreather mask (PRB), nonrebreather mask (NRB), reservoir bag, one-way valve, Venturi mask, air entrainment, air-to-oxygen ratio, total output flow, oxygen blender, high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC), heated humidification, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), work of breathing (WOB), heliox, hyperbaric oxygen, inhaled nitric oxide (iNO), noninvasive ventilation (NIV).

Low-flow and high-flow describe demand matching, not the number on the flowmeter

A low-flow system supplies less total gas flow than the patient's peak inspiratory demand. The patient therefore draws additional room air around the device. That room-air mixing makes the actual FiO₂ variable. A standard nasal cannula remains a low-flow device even when it is set at 6 L/min.

A high-flow system provides enough total flow to meet or exceed the patient's inspiratory demand. Because the patient does not need to pull in as much additional room air, the delivered FiO₂ is more predictable. In this lesson, the Venturi mask and HFNC are major high-flow examples.

Nasal cannula FiO₂ is an estimate

The lesson uses the approximation:

FiO₂ ≈ 20% + (4% × oxygen flow in L/min)

Examples: 1 L/min ≈ 24%, 2 L/min ≈ 28%, 3 L/min ≈ 32%, 4 L/min ≈ 36%, 5 L/min ≈ 40%, and 6 L/min ≈ 44%. This is an approximation because breathing pattern changes how much room air mixes with the oxygen.

Device switching can accidentally reduce oxygen delivery

Never assume that a higher or lower liter flow means a stronger or weaker device. A patient on a simple mask at 8 L/min may be receiving roughly 60% FiO₂. Switching that patient to a nasal cannula at 3 L/min would provide only about 32% by the course approximation. The correct comparison is patient requirement versus new device capability, not just old flow versus new flow.

RTE trap: “Low-flow” does not mean “low L/min.” It means the device does not meet the patient's full inspiratory demand.

A correct device used incorrectly becomes the wrong therapy

Nasal cannula setup

  1. Connect tubing to the oxygen source.
  2. Set the prescribed flow.
  3. Verify gas is flowing from the prongs.
  4. Place the prongs in the nares and secure tubing over the ears.
  5. Check comfort, skin pressure points, and tubing patency.
  6. Reassess SpO₂, respiratory rate, work of breathing, and patient comfort.

Simple mask: the 5 L/min minimum is a safety rule

The lesson specifies 5–8 L/min. Below 5 L/min, exhaled carbon dioxide can accumulate inside the mask's dead space and be rebreathed. Increasing the flow is therefore not just about providing more oxygen; it also flushes exhaled gas from the mask.

PRB and NRB reservoir bags must remain inflated

Both reservoir-mask systems should be flushed with oxygen before placement. During inspiration, the reservoir bag should not collapse. A collapsing bag means patient inspiratory demand exceeds the gas being supplied.

The PRB lacks the full one-way valve arrangement of a nonrebreather. The NRB uses a reservoir and one-way valves to limit rebreathing and room-air entry. The lesson expects the reservoir on the NRB to stay substantially inflated—about two-thirds full during inspiration.

Venturi ports must stay open

A Venturi device depends on air entrainment through its ports. If bedding, clothing, a hand, or another object blocks those ports, the designed air-to-oxygen ratio changes and FiO₂ becomes unpredictable. A patient can therefore be on the “correct adapter” and still receive the wrong mixture because the device cannot entrain room air properly.

Evaluate the patient after every setup change

Correct equipment placement is not the end of the procedure. Reassess oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, mental status, work of breathing, and comfort. If the equipment appears correct but the patient is worsening, do not keep repeating the same setup checks while respiratory failure progresses.

Patient-first safety: A simple mask below 5 L/min, a collapsed reservoir bag, or blocked Venturi ports are equipment problems that can directly compromise therapy. Correct them immediately and reassess the patient.

Calculations explain what the equipment is actually doing

1. Nasal cannula FiO₂ estimate

FiO₂ ≈ 20 + (4 × L/min)

At 4 L/min: 20 + (4 × 4) = 36% FiO₂. Because the nasal cannula is a low-flow device, this is an estimate rather than a guaranteed FiO₂.

2. Venturi “magic box” method

The lesson teaches the air-to-oxygen ratio using the desired FiO₂:

Air parts = 100 − desired FiO₂%
O₂ parts = desired FiO₂% − 20
Reduce the ratio to air : oxygen.

Example: desired FiO₂ 40%

  • Air parts = 100 − 40 = 60
  • O₂ parts = 40 − 20 = 20
  • 60:20 reduces to 3:1 air:O₂

3. Total output flow

If the ratio is 3:1, there are 4 total parts. With an oxygen input of 8 L/min:

Total flow = total parts × O₂ input flow = 4 × 8 = 32 L/min

The clinical meaning is critical: a “precise FiO₂” device is only functioning as a high-flow system if total output is adequate for the patient's inspiratory demand.

Increasing Venturi input flow does not change the selected FiO₂

The jet and entrainment-port geometry set the air-to-oxygen ratio. Increasing oxygen input raises total output flow while the ratio—and therefore FiO₂—remains essentially fixed. To change FiO₂, change the adapter or selected FiO₂ setting.

4. Heliox correction factors

Oxygen-calibrated flowmeters under-read heliox because helium is much less dense. The lesson uses:

  • 80/20 heliox: actual flow = indicated flow × 1.8
  • 70/30 heliox: actual flow = indicated flow × 1.6

If an oxygen-calibrated flowmeter reads 12 L/min with 80/20 heliox: 12 × 1.8 = 21.6 L/min actual flow.

RTE calculation distinction: Getting the arithmetic right is only breadth. Depth means interpreting whether the calculated total flow is enough for the patient's demand and whether the measured flow can be trusted for the gas being used.

When oxygen is not working, identify why

Start with the patient. A falling SpO₂ may be caused by insufficient oxygen concentration, inadequate total flow, a device leak or blockage, worsening lung disease, shock/poor perfusion, or progression to ventilatory failure. The safest next step depends on the mechanism.

A practical troubleshooting sequence

  1. Assess the patient: mental status, respiratory rate, work of breathing, airway, SpO₂ waveform/quality, and overall stability.
  2. Verify the source and setting: oxygen connected, flow actually on, correct FiO₂/adapter selected.
  3. Inspect the device: fit, tubing patency, reservoir inflation, valve condition, Venturi-port patency, HFNC cannula/circuit.
  4. Compare capability with need: can this device provide the required FiO₂ and flow?
  5. Escalate when necessary: do not force a low-level device to manage a patient who has outgrown it.
  6. Reassess response: oxygenation, WOB, mental status, ventilation, and comfort.

Oxygenation failure versus ventilation failure

HFNC can improve oxygen delivery, wash out upper-airway dead space, reduce work of breathing, and provide a mild positive-pressure effect. It does not provide the same ventilatory support as bilevel NIV. The lesson specifically warns that significant hypercapnia with respiratory acidosis in a COPD exacerbation is a situation where NIV is more appropriate than relying on HFNC alone.

NRB can be correct even when SpO₂ is normal

The lesson highlights carbon monoxide poisoning and smoke inhalation because pulse oximetry can appear normal despite dangerous carboxyhemoglobin. High-concentration oxygen can therefore be indicated even when the displayed saturation is 99–100%. Similar high-FiO₂ uses in the lesson include preoxygenation before intubation and rapidly deteriorating patients.

Patient-first safety: Do not keep increasing oxygen device settings while a patient loses airway protection, becomes severely somnolent with rising CO₂, or develops refractory respiratory failure. Oxygen therapy must transition to ventilatory support when the clinical problem becomes ventilation or airway failure.

HFNC separates flow from FiO₂

High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) delivers heated, humidified gas through large-bore nasal prongs. The lesson teaches a flow range of approximately 10–60 L/min and an FiO₂ range of 21–100%. Unlike a standard cannula, HFNC allows the therapist to set flow and FiO₂ independently.

Why heat and humidity matter

High gas flows would be uncomfortable and drying without conditioning. Heated humidification improves tolerance and helps maintain airway moisture. HFNC can also reduce nasopharyngeal dead space, provide more stable FiO₂, reduce work of breathing, and create a mild PEEP-like effect.

HFNC setup logic

  1. Assemble the gas source/blender and heated humidification system.
  2. Fill/connect the approved sterile-water source according to the system.
  3. Connect the heated circuit and appropriate large-bore cannula.
  4. Set the prescribed flow and FiO₂.
  5. Allow the system to reach the intended temperature/conditioning state.
  6. Apply the cannula and verify fit without completely occluding the nares.
  7. Assess SpO₂, respiratory rate, work of breathing, comfort, mental status, and trend.

HFNC troubleshooting

  • Patient still hypoxemic: verify FiO₂, flow, cannula placement, source gas, circuit, and patient trajectory.
  • Work of breathing remains high: confirm flow is sufficient and consider whether the patient requires NIV or intubation rather than more oxygen alone.
  • Dryness/discomfort: verify heated humidification and temperature function.
  • Condensation/circuit issue: manage according to the system and humidification lesson without allowing water to interfere with flow.

Heliox treats airflow resistance, not hypoxemia

Heliox reduces gas density and can reduce turbulent airway resistance in selected severe obstruction such as croup or upper-airway narrowing. Its benefit depends on having enough helium in the mixture. When the patient requires very high FiO₂, the helium fraction becomes too low for the desired density effect. That is why the lesson emphasizes that heliox improves work of breathing, not oxygenation.

Other specialty oxygen-related systems

The lesson also introduces hyperbaric oxygen and inhaled nitric oxide to reinforce an equipment principle: specialty therapy requires dedicated delivery and monitoring equipment. The therapist must verify not just the prescribed gas, but also the system capable of delivering and monitoring it safely.

RTE distinction: HFNC is not “a nasal cannula turned up high.” It is a heated, humidified, high-flow system designed to meet inspiratory demand with independently controlled FiO₂ and flow.
Putting C5 together
For HFNC and specialty-gas therapy, verify the correct system, flow, FiO₂ or gas mixture, conditioning/monitoring, patient response, and device limitations. Oxygen delivery: match the device to the physiology

A liter-flow setting by itself does not tell you how much oxygen reaches the patient. You must understand device design, air entrainment, reservoir function, patient inspiratory demand, delivered FiO₂, and whether the patient's problem is oxygenation, ventilation, or both.

patient → oxygenation/WOB → device → flow + FiO₂ → setup quality → actual delivery → response → troubleshooting → escalation

RTE Depth of Clinical Judgment: take the next step

  • Information Gathering: identify current device, flow, FiO₂, SpO₂ trend, respiratory rate, work of breathing, mental status, and whether the device appears to be functioning correctly.
  • Information Gathering: determine whether a low-flow system is being diluted by high inspiratory demand or whether a high-flow device's total output is adequate.
  • Decision Making: choose a replacement device that preserves or increases the patient's needed FiO₂ rather than accidentally reducing support during a device switch.
  • Decision Making: correct a collapsed reservoir bag, low simple-mask flow, blocked Venturi ports, or other reversible equipment problem before assuming the patient needs a different diagnosis.
  • Decision Making: escalate from conventional oxygen to HFNC, NRB, NIV, or mechanical ventilation according to oxygenation, work of breathing, ventilation, airway protection, and response.
  • Decision Making: recognize that HFNC cannot substitute for appropriate ventilatory support in significant hypercapnic respiratory acidosis simply because the SpO₂ improves.
  • Decision Making: use high-concentration oxygen despite a normal pulse-oximeter reading when the clinical problem makes the SpO₂ unreliable, such as suspected carbon monoxide poisoning.
  • Reassessment: verify the selected device actually improves oxygenation and/or work of breathing and that no new equipment hazard has been introduced.
  • Communication: report the patient's oxygen requirement, device, settings, response, troubleshooting findings, and reason for escalation.
