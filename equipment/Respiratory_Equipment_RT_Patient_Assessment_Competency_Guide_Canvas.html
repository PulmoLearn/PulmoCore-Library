Respiratory Equipment • Competency Guide

RT Patient Assessment Equipment & Procedures

Learn how to select and use bedside assessment equipment, perform a systematic respiratory examination, recognize misleading data, distinguish equipment failure from patient deterioration, and escalate dangerous findings without delay.

Before you begin

Respiratory assessment is not a checklist performed before treatment. It is the process that tells the respiratory therapist whether the patient is stable, whether the equipment reading is believable, what mechanism may be causing deterioration, and what should happen next.

For the 2027 Respiratory Therapy Examination (RTE), Breadth of Knowledge includes correct use of the stethoscope, pulse oximeter, blood-pressure cuff, thermometer, pulse and respiratory-rate assessment, and inspection-palpation-percussion-auscultation (IPPA). Depth of Clinical Judgment requires students to integrate those findings, validate the data, identify dangerous patterns, and decide whether to correct equipment, gather another assessment, initiate support, or escalate immediately.

Big idea: Follow the chain patient → equipment/technique → data quality → pattern → mechanism → urgency → next assessment → intervention/support → reassessment → communication.

Core vocabulary: inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation, tactile fremitus, crepitus, resonance, hyperresonance, dullness, vesicular breath sounds, bronchial breath sounds, crackles, wheezes, rhonchi, stridor, pleural friction rub, pulse oximetry, plethysmographic waveform, systolic pressure, diastolic pressure, Korotkoff sounds, oscillometric blood pressure, tachypnea, bradypnea, cyanosis, accessory muscle use, paradoxical breathing, tripod position, orthopnea, escalation.

Equipment is useful only when the technique is correct

The lesson's equipment-first framework asks the same questions for every device: What is it? Why do we use it? How is it used correctly? What can make it wrong? What does an abnormal finding mean? When do we escalate?

Stethoscope

Use the diaphragm directly on skin and compare matching locations side to side. Listen through a complete inspiratory and expiratory cycle. Clothing creates friction and muffles sound. A finding becomes more meaningful when it is localized, reproducible, and fits the rest of the examination.

Pulse oximeter

Pulse oximetry estimates peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) and provides pulse rate and a plethysmographic waveform. Verify correct sensor placement, adequate perfusion, and a believable waveform before acting on an unexpected saturation. Pulse oximetry does not measure PaCO₂ or ventilation.

Blood-pressure cuff

Correct cuff size is essential. The lesson states that the bladder should encircle approximately 80% of the arm circumference. A cuff that is too small produces a falsely high reading; a cuff that is too large can produce a falsely low reading.

For manual measurement, position the supported arm at heart level, locate the brachial artery, place the diaphragm over it, inflate above expected systolic pressure, and deflate at approximately 2–3 mmHg/second. The first Korotkoff sound is systolic pressure; disappearance/muffling according to the taught method identifies diastolic pressure.

Thermometer

Route and technique affect the reading. The lesson lists oral temperature as approximately 36.5–37.5°C and reminds students that recent oral intake can affect oral measurements. Fever increases metabolic oxygen demand; hypothermia can depress respiratory drive.

Pulse and respiratory rate

Assess pulse rate, rhythm, and strength. Count for a full 60 seconds when rhythm is irregular. For respiratory rate, observe quietly when possible so the patient does not consciously change breathing. The lesson gives normal adult respiratory rate as 12–20 breaths/min and requires a 60-second count in unstable or irregular breathing.

Common mistake: Treating the equipment reading as the assessment. A saturation of 92%, a BP of 86/50, or a respiratory rate of 8 is meaningful only after you know the measurement was obtained correctly and fits the patient.

IPPA: Inspection → Palpation → Percussion → Auscultation

The lesson teaches a systematic IPPA sequence so each step adds information before the next. Assessment begins before touching the patient.

Inspection

Observe general appearance, mental status, posture, ability to speak, skin color, chest symmetry, respiratory rate/pattern, accessory-muscle use, retractions, nasal flaring, pursed-lip breathing, sputum, and airway secretions. Important danger signs taught include central cyanosis, severe accessory-muscle use, inability to speak, altered mental status, and paradoxical chest-wall movement.

Palpation

Assess chest expansion, tactile fremitus, tenderness, and crepitus. Increased fremitus supports consolidation; decreased/absent fremitus can occur with pleural effusion, pneumothorax, or emphysema. Subcutaneous crepitus suggests escaped air and requires escalation in the course context.

Percussion

NoteMeaning taught in lesson
ResonanceExpected normal aerated lung
HyperresonanceExcess air: emphysema or pneumothorax

Auscultation

Compare symmetrical lung fields. Normal vesicular sounds should be soft and low-pitched over peripheral lung. Adventitious sounds add clues:

  • Crackles: small airways/alveoli opening through fluid or collapse; seen in pulmonary edema, pneumonia, atelectasis.
  • Wheezes: narrowed airways; common in asthma/COPD.
  • Rhonchi: larger-airway secretions; may change after cough.
  • Stridor: upper-airway obstruction; an urgent finding.
  • Absent/diminished unilateral sounds: consider pneumothorax, effusion, obstruction, or mainstem intubation depending on context.

RTE distinction: Hyperresonance alone does not diagnose pneumothorax. Hyperresonance + acute unilateral breath-sound loss + asymmetric expansion + deterioration is the clinically meaningful pattern.

A number without signal quality is not enough

Pulse oximetry

Motion, poor peripheral perfusion, cold extremities, poor sensor placement, nail coverings, and dyshemoglobins can make SpO₂ misleading. Verify the pleth waveform and compare the monitor pulse with the patient's actual pulse. Reposition or choose a better site when the signal is poor.

The course also reinforces that standard pulse oximetry may appear normal in carbon monoxide poisoning. A normal SpO₂ does not prove normal oxygen delivery in smoke inhalation.

Blood pressure

A cuff that is too small makes the reading falsely high. A cuff that is too large can make it falsely low. Automatic oscillometric devices can be inaccurate during irregular rhythms, movement, and low-flow states. When the automatic reading does not fit the patient, repeat manually with the correct cuff size.

Pulse

Do not count an irregular pulse for only 15 seconds and multiply. The lesson requires a full 60-second count for irregular rhythm and provider/cardiac-monitor evaluation when clinically indicated.

Respiratory rate

Short counting intervals can badly misrepresent irregular breathing. The lesson specifically warns that a 15-second count in an unstable patient or a patient with Cheyne-Stokes respirations can be substantially inaccurate. Count for a full 60 seconds when the rate or rhythm is irregular or the patient is unstable.

Temperature

The route matters. Axillary temperature is less accurate and generally lower than core temperature. Tympanic values depend on probe technique and can be affected by cerumen. Recent hot/cold intake can alter oral readings. Recheck unexpected values with appropriate technique before using them to define the patient's trend.

Common beginner error: Repeating a bad measurement with the same bad setup. If the cuff is the wrong size or the pulse-ox signal is absent, three repeated readings do not make the data more valid.
Putting C3 together
Before interpreting a value, verify device fit, signal quality, counting interval, patient position, measurement route, and whether the number matches the patient. Repeat or change methods when the data are not trustworthy.

Patient first, then equipment—unless equipment is the obvious reversible cause

  1. Look at the patient: mental status, color, work of breathing, ability to speak, chest movement, perfusion.
  2. Check the measurement quality: waveform, cuff size, probe position, counting technique, device connection.
  3. Obtain an independent comparison: manual pulse/BP, alternate pulse-ox site, auscultation, repeat RR, or another appropriate assessment.
  4. Correct a reversible equipment problem.
  5. Reassess immediately.
  6. Escalate if the abnormality persists or the patient is unstable.

Case: SpO₂ 82%, patient looks comfortable

A patient is speaking normally without distress, but SpO₂ suddenly reads 82% with an erratic or absent pleth waveform and cold fingers. Warm/reposition the site or choose another site and compare pulse rate. The poor signal is a strong competing explanation.

Case: BP 186/102 with wrong cuff

A large-arm patient is measured with a standard adult cuff. Before concluding hypertensive crisis, obtain the correct large-adult cuff and repeat the measurement. The lesson explicitly teaches the too-small cuff as a cause of falsely high BP.

Case: the patient looks worse than the monitor

A patient becomes confused, uses accessory muscles, and cannot speak full sentences while the pulse oximeter reads 94%. Do not allow the saturation to overrule the clinical assessment. The patient may be exhausting, hypercapnic, or otherwise failing despite an acceptable SpO₂.

Patient-first safety: When the patient is visibly deteriorating, provide support and escalate while checking equipment. Troubleshooting should not become a reason to delay oxygenation, airway support, or emergency response.
Putting C4 together
Compare the monitor with the patient, validate the setup, obtain an independent measure, correct obvious equipment error, and reassess. If the patient is unstable, treatment and escalation occur in parallel with equipment verification.

Assessment should produce a communication

Communicate the pattern, not only a diagnosis label. For example: “The patient has sudden dyspnea, asymmetric chest rise, absent right-sided breath sounds, right-sided hyperresonance, SpO₂ 84%, and increasing distress. I am concerned about pneumothorax and need immediate evaluation.”

Do not document before responding

The course escalation principle is explicit: document as you notify, not before. A perfectly completed assessment note is not useful if the patient's respiratory failure progressed while the therapist was charting.

Escalation rule: When the pattern is dangerous, notify and support first, then continue collecting details. Waiting for complete certainty can cost the patient the time needed for treatment.
Putting C5 together
Recognize instability from the pattern of findings, support the immediate respiratory or hemodynamic problem, communicate concise evidence, and continue reassessment while definitive evaluation or treatment proceeds.

Patient assessment: equipment provides data; the therapist builds the meaning

A stethoscope, pulse oximeter, BP cuff, thermometer, and timer each contribute information. Clinical judgment comes from validating those measurements, recognizing patterns, deciding what mechanism is most likely, and acting when the patient is deteriorating.

patient → assessment → data quality → pattern → mechanism → urgency → next assessment/support → reassessment → communication

RTE Breadth of Knowledge: before moving on, you should be able to...

  • identify the purpose and correct use of the stethoscope, pulse oximeter, blood-pressure cuff, thermometer, pulse assessment, and respiratory-rate assessment;
  • perform assessment in the IPPA sequence: inspection → palpation → percussion → auscultation;
  • recognize inspection signs including cyanosis, accessory-muscle use, tripod positioning, asymmetric chest rise, and altered mental status;
  • interpret tactile fremitus, chest expansion, and crepitus;
  • differentiate resonance, hyperresonance, and dullness;
  • differentiate normal breath sounds from crackles, wheezes, rhonchi, stridor, and unilateral absent/diminished sounds;
  • apply the lesson's normal adult respiratory rate of 12–20/min and pulse of 60–100/min;
  • count irregular pulse and unstable/irregular respiratory rate for a full 60 seconds;
  • select a BP cuff with bladder coverage of approximately 80% of arm circumference and recognize small-cuff/large-cuff error;
  • deflate a manual BP cuff at approximately 2–3 mmHg/sec;
  • recognize pulse-ox limitations including motion, poor perfusion, poor probe placement, and dyshemoglobin problems;
  • recognize that normal SpO₂ does not exclude carbon monoxide exposure or ventilatory failure;
  • integrate IPPA patterns for pneumonia, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, COPD/emphysema, asthma, pulmonary edema, and atelectasis as taught in the lesson; and
  • recognize the course's immediate escalation findings.

RTE Depth of Clinical Judgment: take the next step

  • Information Gathering: assess mental status, color, respiratory rate/pattern, work of breathing, chest symmetry, pulse, BP, oxygenation, breath sounds, percussion, and palpation findings rather than making decisions from a single number.
  • Information Gathering: verify pulse-ox waveform/probe quality, BP cuff size, measurement position, counting interval, and other technical factors when bedside data conflict with the patient.
  • Decision Making: repeat or change the measurement method when the result is not technically trustworthy.
  • Decision Making: recognize unilateral hyperresonance plus absent breath sounds and acute distress as a pneumothorax pattern requiring urgent escalation rather than simply repeating auscultation.
  • Decision Making: recognize a silent chest in severe asthma as worsening airflow failure, not improvement because wheezing disappeared.
  • Decision Making: do not allow an acceptable SpO₂ to overrule altered mental status, severe work of breathing, or evidence of hypoventilation.
  • Decision Making: correct an obvious equipment problem when the patient is stable, but support and escalate simultaneously when the patient is deteriorating.
  • Reassessment: repeat the relevant assessment after oxygen, bronchodilator therapy, repositioning, equipment correction, or other intervention and compare the trend.
  • Communication: report the abnormal pattern, supporting findings, urgency, intervention already performed, and the next action needed.
National Board for Respiratory Care connection: The Respiratory Therapy Examination expects you to perform clinical assessment, use noninvasive monitoring equipment, evaluate monitoring results, recognize changes in patient condition, initiate or recommend respiratory care, respond to emergencies, and escalate concerns to the care team.
