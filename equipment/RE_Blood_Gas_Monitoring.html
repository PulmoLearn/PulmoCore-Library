Oxygenation vs. Ventilation — The Foundation

MOST IMPORTANT DISTINCTION IN THIS LESSON

Blood gas monitoring is built on one foundational concept that must be mastered before everything else: oxygenation and ventilation are not the same thing, they are monitored by different parameters, and treating one as the other is one of the most dangerous clinical errors in respiratory care.

🩸 Oxygenation

What it is: The delivery of oxygen to the blood and tissues.

Measured by: PaO₂ (arterial O₂ tension) and SaO₂/SpO₂ (hemoglobin saturation)

Normal PaO₂: 80–100 mmHg

Clinical concern: Hypoxemia (low PaO₂)

Monitored by: ABG (PaO₂) or pulse oximetry (SpO₂)

Treatment: Supplemental oxygen, HFNC, NIV, MV

💨 Ventilation

What it is: The movement of air in and out of the lungs — the process that eliminates CO₂.

Measured by: PaCO₂ and pH (CO₂ + water = carbonic acid = pH)

Normal PaCO₂: 35–45 mmHg

Clinical concern: Hypercapnia (↑PaCO₂) = hypoventilation; Hypocapnia (↓PaCO₂) = hyperventilation

Monitored by: ABG only — pulse oximetry cannot measure PaCO₂ or ventilation status

Treatment: Increase ventilation — NIV, MV, positioning

Critical Clinical Pearl: A patient can have a normal SpO₂ (96–98%) while simultaneously developing dangerous CO₂ retention (PaCO₂ 70+ mmHg) and acidosis (pH 7.20). Pulse oximetry completely misses this — and COPD patients on low-flow O₂ are particularly at risk. This is why the ABG is ordered: the SpO₂ cannot substitute for pH and PaCO₂.

Indications, Contraindications & Hazards

Indications for ABG
Evaluate ventilation status (PaCO₂/pH) · Evaluate oxygenation status (PaO₂/SaO₂) · Assess acid-base balance · Monitor response to oxygen or ventilatory therapy · Pre-operative surgical risk assessment · Unexplained dyspnea or altered mental status
Sample Rejection Criteria
Visible air bubbles not removed · Visible clot present · Sample not anticoagulated · Room-temperature storage >30 minutes · Missing patient identification · Incorrect or missing documentation · Malfunctioning or uncalibrated analyzer
Hazards of ABG Sampling
Bloodborne pathogen exposure · Hematoma or hemorrhage at puncture site · Arterial spasm · Pain and vasovagal response · Incorrect treatment from inaccurate results · Patient misidentification errors · Infection at puncture site

Types of Blood Gas Samples

Arterial Blood Gas (ABG)
Gold standard for oxygenation, ventilation, and acid-base status. Obtained from radial, brachial, or femoral artery. Provides PaO₂, PaCO₂, pH, HCO₃⁻, SaO₂, and base excess. Required when full cardiopulmonary picture is needed.
Venous Blood Gas (VBG)
Obtained from central venous line. pH and PaCO₂ differ from arterial values (VBG pH ~0.03–0.05 lower; PvCO₂ ~5 mmHg higher). PO₂ values are not clinically equivalent to PaO₂ — cannot assess oxygenation from VBG alone.
Capillary Blood Gas (CBG)
Used when arterial access is difficult — common in neonates and pediatric patients. Sample obtained from warmed heel, finger, or earlobe. Approximates arterial values if site is properly warmed and free-flowing.
Arterial Line (A-Line) Sampling
Indwelling arterial catheter for continuous blood pressure monitoring and frequent ABG sampling. Open system: requires discard volume before sample to clear dead space. Closed system: minimizes blood loss and open-air exposure. Preferred in ICU for critically ill patients.

ABG Supplies & Syringe Anatomy

EQUIPMENT: Preheparinized ABG Syringe

Required Supplies

20–22 gauge short-bevel needle
Preheparinized 1–3 mL ABG syringe with cap
Gauze pads, alcohol wipe (70% isopropyl), bandage
Specimen label, biohazard bag, sharps container

Syringe Component Profiles

Needle Hub

Protective cover removed before insertion. Never recap by hand — place directly into sharps container after use.

Short-Bevel Needle (20–22G)

Shorter bevel than standard needles reduces arterial wall trauma. Bevel must face UP during insertion for easier entry and reduced pain.

Preheparinized Syringe Barrel

Lined with heparin to prevent clot formation. 1 mL (point-of-care) or 3 mL (standard). Self-fills when artery is accessed — do not pull plunger in a true arterial puncture.

Plunger

In a properly performed radial puncture, arterial pressure pushes blood into the syringe without pulling. Pulling introduces room air — a major source of contamination.

Syringe Cap

Applied after needle is safely removed and discarded. Seals the sample air-tight to prevent CO₂ loss or O₂ gain from ambient air before analysis.

Safety Guard / Needle Safety

Activated using a hard surface (not the hand) after needle removal. Engineered device to prevent needlestick injury. Required by OSHA needlestick safety standards.

Why Heparin? Blood begins clotting immediately when it leaves the vessel. Heparin in the syringe prevents coagulation during transport and analysis. A clotted sample cannot be run through the analyzer — and should not be attempted — because clot particles can damage electrodes and produce falsely abnormal results.

Pre-Sampling Safety & Order Verification

The most common ABG errors occur before the sample reaches the analyzer — during ordering, preparation, and documentation. A technically perfect puncture with incomplete documentation produces a clinically useless result.

Required Checks Before Drawing

Clinical Checks
Valid physician order for ABG · Correct patient identity (two identifiers) · Oxygen delivery device and flow or FiO₂ at the time of draw · Recent changes to ventilator settings or oxygen support · Anticoagulant therapy (bleeding risk) · Presence of a palpable pulse at the intended site
Required Documentation
Date and time of collection · Sample source (radial, brachial, A-line) · FiO₂ or oxygen flow and delivery device · Ventilator settings if applicable · Patient temperature (if required by policy) · Collector name/initials · Whether the sample was on room air or supplemental oxygen
Critical Documentation Error: Drawing a "room air" ABG while the patient is still receiving supplemental oxygen — even 1–2 L/min nasal cannula — produces artificially elevated PaO₂ that misrepresents the patient's true baseline oxygenation. Always verify and document the exact oxygen source at the moment of the draw.

Modified Allen Test

REQUIRED BEFORE RADIAL ARTERY PUNCTURE

The Modified Allen test assesses collateral circulation to the hand from the ulnar artery before radial artery puncture. If the radial artery is damaged or goes into spasm, the ulnar artery must be able to perfuse the hand — this test confirms it can.

Positive Allen Test
Color returns to the blanched palm within 5–7 seconds after releasing ulnar pressure. Indicates adequate collateral circulation. ✅ Proceed with radial puncture.
Negative Allen Test
Color does NOT return (or takes >15 seconds). Indicates the hand depends entirely on the radial artery for perfusion — ulnar collaterals are insufficient. ❌ Do NOT use this radial site. Select an alternate site.

Activity: Modified Allen Test — Correct Order

Put the five steps of the Modified Allen test in the correct sequence.

Have the patient clench the fist tightly to expel blood from the palm
Apply firm simultaneous pressure over both the radial and ulnar arteries
Instruct the patient to open the hand — the palm should appear pale or blanched
Release pressure over the ulnar artery only — maintain pressure on the radial artery
Observe for return of pink color within 5–7 seconds — positive test confirms adequate collateral circulation
Order all five steps to continue.

Arterial Puncture — Procedure Overview

EQUIPMENT: ABG Syringe · Needle · Safety Guard

Procedure Essentials

  1. Confirm collateral circulation (Modified Allen test for radial site)
  2. Clean site with 70% isopropyl alcohol; allow to dry
  3. Palpate pulse with non-dominant hand; locate arterial landmark
  4. Insert 20–22 gauge needle bevel-up at 30–45° angle toward pulse
  5. Watch for spontaneous arterial flashback — do NOT pull back on plunger (pulling introduces room air)
  6. Allow 1–3 mL to fill; withdraw needle in one smooth motion
  7. Immediately apply firm direct pressure with gauze — maintain for 5 minutes (longer if anticoagulated)
  8. Activate safety guard using hard surface; place needle directly in sharps container (never recap by hand)
  9. Remove visible air bubbles immediately and cap the syringe
  10. Mix sample gently by rolling syringe between palms to coat with heparin (do not shake)
  11. Label sample at the bedside; place in biohazard bag; transport immediately
Common Sampling Sites
Radial artery — most common; accessible, compressible, collateral circulation present. Requires Allen test.
Brachial artery — larger vessel; used when radial is not accessible. Less collateral circulation — use with caution.
Femoral artery — emergency only; large vessel, difficult to compress, high infection risk.
Post-Puncture Care
Hold firm pressure minimum 5 minutes (10+ min for anticoagulated patients). Check for hematoma formation. Verify distal pulse after pressure release. Document puncture site, difficulty, patient tolerance, and any complications. Apply bandage when bleeding is confirmed stopped.

Sample Handling & Pre-Analytical Errors

VERY HIGH-YIELD — MOST ERRORS OCCUR HERE

The pre-analytical phase — everything that happens before the sample enters the analyzer — is where most ABG errors originate. Understanding the specific effect of each error on the results is a core NBRC testing area.

Air Bubble Contamination

PaCO₂ → Decreases ↓ (CO₂ diffuses into bubble) pH → Increases ↑ (less CO₂ = less acid) PaO₂ → Variable (depends on original PaO₂ vs. room air PO₂)

Action: Remove bubbles immediately after collection by holding syringe tip up and expelling; if significant bubble contamination persists, redraw.

Sample Delay / Room-Temperature Storage

PaCO₂ → Increases ↑ (cells continue metabolizing) pH → Decreases ↓ (more CO₂ = more acid) PaO₂ → Decreases ↓ (cells consume oxygen)

Action: Analyze within 5–10 minutes ideally; if delay >10 min, place in ice slurry (not direct ice). Analyze within 30 minutes max. Never store at room temperature for >30 minutes.

Blood Gas Analyzers

Blood gas analyzers use electrochemical sensors to measure pH, PaCO₂, and PaO₂ directly, then calculate derived values like HCO₃⁻ and base excess. Understanding which type of analyzer is appropriate for which situation is tested on the NBRC.

Stationary Blood Gas Analyzer

Examples: GEM system, Radiometer ABL

  • Located in central lab or respiratory department
  • High-volume testing capacity — appropriate for multiple simultaneous samples
  • Full blood gas panel including electrolytes and metabolites (depending on cartridge)
  • Requires dedicated maintenance, daily calibration, and QC
  • Higher accuracy with larger sample volumes
  • Longer turnaround time — sample must travel to lab

Point-of-Care Analyzer

Examples: i-STAT, Abbott cartridge-based systems

  • Bedside, ED, ICU, and transport settings
  • Small sample volume (typically 0.1–0.5 mL)
  • Results in approximately 60–120 seconds
  • Single-use precalibrated cartridges — minimal setup required
  • No daily electrode maintenance — cartridge handles calibration
  • More expensive per test than central lab; appropriate when speed is critical
How Analyzers Measure: Each electrode in the analyzer is specific to one parameter. The pH electrode uses a glass membrane that generates a voltage proportional to H⁺ ion concentration. The PaCO₂ electrode (Severinghaus) uses a CO₂-permeable membrane; CO₂ diffuses through and changes the pH of a buffer solution, which the electrode detects. The PaO₂ electrode (Clark) reduces O₂ at a platinum cathode, producing a measurable current proportional to PO₂.

Co-Oximetry

CRITICAL — PULSE OX CANNOT REPLACE THIS

A standard blood gas analyzer measures total hemoglobin saturation — it cannot distinguish between oxyhemoglobin, carboxyhemoglobin (COHb), or methemoglobin (MetHb). A co-oximeter uses multiple wavelengths of light to identify and quantify each hemoglobin species separately.

What Co-Oximetry Measures
Oxyhemoglobin (O₂Hb) — hemoglobin carrying O₂
Reduced hemoglobin (HHb) — deoxygenated
Carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) — bound to CO
Methemoglobin (MetHb) — oxidized, non-functional

Total hemoglobin and functional saturation are calculated from these fractions.
When Co-Oximetry Is Required
Carbon monoxide exposure or poisoning
Suspected methemoglobinemia
Pulse oximetry does not match clinical presentation
Patient appears cyanotic with normal SpO₂
SpO₂ <80% requiring confirmation
Any unexplained hypoxemia
Suspected abnormal hemoglobin variant
The CO Poisoning Trap (VERY HIGH-YIELD): Standard pulse oximetry cannot distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin — both absorb light at the same wavelengths detected by a 2-wavelength pulse oximeter. A CO-poisoned patient may show SpO₂ 98–100% while their functional oxygen saturation (oxyhemoglobin) is critically low. The cherry-red skin color, confusion, and history of smoke/CO exposure are the clinical cues — the SpO₂ cannot be trusted. ABG with co-oximetry is the only diagnostic tool.

Calibration, Quality Control & QA

A blood gas analyzer is only as trustworthy as its most recent successful quality control run. Calibration and QC are not bureaucratic formalities — they are the only way to know whether the analyzer's results reflect clinical reality.

1-Point Calibration
Electrodes calibrated to a single standard value. Typically performed automatically every 20–30 minutes on stationary analyzers. Detects small drift during operation. Not sufficient to verify full accuracy range.
2-Point Calibration
Electrodes calibrated at two standard concentrations (low and high). Typically performed every 8 hours. Verifies linearity across the clinical range. Most common full calibration interval in routine use.
3-Point Calibration
Most thorough calibration — three standard concentrations. Typically performed every 6 months or after electrode replacement. Required after major maintenance or when 2-point calibration repeatedly fails.

Quality Control (QC) — Three Error Categories

Pre-Analytical Errors
Sample collection, handling, and transport. Air bubbles, clots, delay, wrong patient, incorrect FiO₂ documentation. No analyzer can correct a bad sample.
Analytical Errors
Errors during measurement. Electrode failure, calibration drift, contaminated controls, expired reagents, temperature errors. Detected and resolved by calibration and QC runs.
Post-Analytical Errors
Incorrect reporting, transcription, or interpretation. Results reported to wrong patient, misinterpretation of values, failure to act on critical results. Training and verification processes prevent these.

Inaccurate PaO₂ readingsWater condensate on O₂ electrode probeInspect probe; dry or replace as indicated; verify calibration afterward

Pulse Oximetry

HIGH-YIELD EQUIPMENT SECTION

Pulse oximetry provides continuous, noninvasive SpO₂ and heart rate. It is the most widely used monitoring device in respiratory care — and also the most commonly misinterpreted. Understanding what it cannot measure is as important as understanding what it can.

What Pulse Ox Measures
SpO₂ — functional hemoglobin saturation. Heart rate. Plethysmographic waveform (perfusion quality indicator).

What It Does NOT Measure: PaO₂, PaCO₂, pH, HCO₃⁻, COHb, MetHb. A normal SpO₂ provides no information about ventilation or acid-base status.
Probe Placement Sites
Fingertip (most common) · Earlobe · Forehead · Bridge of nose · Infant hand or foot

Site selection affects signal quality. In low-perfusion states, forehead or earlobe probes are more reliable than peripheral finger sites.
Documentation Requirements
Probe type and placement site · Date and time · Patient activity level and position · FiO₂ and oxygen delivery method · Correlation of pulse ox heart rate with palpated or ECG heart rate · Any ABG or co-oximetry available for correlation

Transcutaneous Monitoring

EQUIPMENT: Transcutaneous PO₂/PCO₂ Monitor

Transcutaneous monitoring (TcPO₂/TcPCO₂) provides noninvasive continuous estimates of arterial oxygen and carbon dioxide tensions by measuring gas diffusion through the heated skin. It is most commonly used in neonatal and pediatric intensive care.

How It Works
Modified blood gas electrodes are attached to the skin surface. A heating element warms the skin to 42–45°C, causing local capillary vasodilation and arterialization of blood flow. This allows transcutaneous gas diffusion to approximate arterial levels. The PO₂ and PCO₂ measured at the skin surface reflect — but are not equal to — PaO₂ and PaCO₂.
Clinical Applications
Continuous monitoring in premature neonates where frequent ABG sampling is impractical. Trending PaO₂ and PaCO₂ changes during procedures. Monitoring during transport when access is limited. Not as accurate as direct ABG — used for trending, not for absolute values.
Safety Concern — Burns
The heating element required for arterialization creates a serious burn risk, especially in premature neonates with fragile skin. Sensors must be repositioned every 4–6 hours in adults and more frequently (every 2–4 hours) in neonates. Failure to reposition results in thermal skin injury — one of the most preventable complications of transcutaneous monitoring.
Accuracy Limitations: Transcutaneous values correlate well with ABG values when perfusion is adequate and technique is correct. Accuracy decreases with: poor peripheral perfusion · shock · peripheral vascular disease · edema · medication-induced vasodilation/constriction. Always correlate TcPO₂/TcPCO₂ with intermittent ABG for validation.

Escalation & RT Clinical Pearls

When to Escalate

  • PaO₂ critically low — apply/increase supplemental oxygen; notify provider; consider escalation to HFNC or NIV
  • PaCO₂ rising with somnolence or distress — ventilatory failure is imminent; escalate to NIV or intubation preparation
  • Severe acidosis (pH <7.20) — emergent intervention; notify provider/rapid response; prepare for ventilatory support
  • SpO₂ does not correlate with clinical appearance — consider CO poisoning; obtain ABG with co-oximetry immediately
  • Analyzer QC failure — remove from service; use alternate analyzer or central lab; do not run patient samples
  • Questionable sample quality (bubble, clot, delay) — do not report results as clinically valid; redraw before acting on data

RT Clinical Pearls

  • A normal SpO₂ does not rule out hypercapnia. The most dangerous clinical confusion in blood gas monitoring — PaCO₂ and pH require an ABG.
  • Air bubbles usually lower PaCO₂ and raise pH. CO₂ diffuses out of the sample into the air bubble faster than O₂ moves in — the net effect is always a decrease in PaCO₂.
  • A clotted sample must never be analyzed. Reject and redraw — running a clotted sample risks analyzer damage and produces falsely abnormal values.
  • The most common ABG errors occur before the sample reaches the analyzer — in collection, handling, and documentation, not in the machine itself.
  • Always document FiO₂ or oxygen device at the time of the draw. Without this, the ABG result cannot be clinically interpreted — oxygenation assessment is meaningless without knowing the oxygen delivery conditions.
  • Co-oximetry is the only test that detects CO poisoning directly. Standard ABG analyzers and pulse oximeters both fail in CO poisoning — only a co-oximeter with multi-wavelength analysis can measure COHb.

Common NBRC Traps — Blood Gas Monitoring

  • Interpreting SpO₂ as equivalent to PaO₂. SpO₂ is the percentage of hemoglobin saturated with oxygen. PaO₂ is the partial pressure of dissolved oxygen in plasma. They are related but not interchangeable. A patient with SpO₂ 94% may have PaO₂ anywhere from ~70 to 100 mmHg depending on their hemoglobin-oxygen dissociation curve position.
  • Trusting a normal SpO₂ in a CO-poisoned patient. Pulse oximetry reads carboxyhemoglobin as oxyhemoglobin. Cherry-red skin + smoke exposure + normal SpO₂ = CO poisoning until proven otherwise by co-oximetry.
  • Running a clotted ABG sample. Once a clot is visible, the sample must be rejected. Analyzing a clotted sample risks damaging the analyzer's electrodes and produces meaningless results.
  • Ignoring visible air bubbles in the syringe. Bubbles must be expelled immediately after collection. An uncorrected bubble causes a falsely low PaCO₂ and elevated pH — exactly the opposite of what a hypoventilating patient actually has.
  • Drawing an ABG and documenting it as "room air" when the patient is on supplemental oxygen. This produces a falsely elevated PaO₂ that makes the patient appear more oxygenated than they actually are. Always verify and document the actual oxygen device and flow at the time of the draw.
  • Not performing the Modified Allen test before radial puncture. If radial artery patency is compromised after the puncture and no collateral circulation exists, the hand may be at risk for ischemia. The Allen test is a required safety check — not optional.
  • Using pulse oximetry alone when pH or PaCO₂ information is needed. Pulse oximetry provides no information about acid-base status or CO₂ levels. A patient with a "good SpO₂" can simultaneously have severe respiratory acidosis — which pulse oximetry will completely miss.

FAQ

Common Questions

What is the difference between PaO₂ and SpO₂?

PaO₂ is the partial pressure of oxygen dissolved in arterial plasma, measured in mmHg by a blood gas analyzer. SpO₂ is the percentage of hemoglobin molecules in the blood that are bound to oxygen, measured noninvasively by pulse oximetry. They are related through the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve — but they are not interchangeable. A PaO₂ of 60 mmHg corresponds to approximately SpO₂ 90% on the standard dissociation curve. Pulse oximetry cannot measure PaO₂ directly, and the relationship between SpO₂ and PaO₂ changes with pH, temperature, and other factors.

Why does an air bubble decrease PaCO₂?

Room air contains essentially no CO₂ (~0.04%). Arterial blood contains approximately 5–6% CO₂. When an air bubble is in the syringe, CO₂ rapidly diffuses from the high-concentration blood into the low-concentration bubble. This reduces the CO₂ content of the sample. Less CO₂ means less carbonic acid — so pH rises alongside the falling PaCO₂. This is why it's critical to expel air bubbles immediately after collection, before CO₂ has time to equilibrate with the bubble. The effect on PaO₂ depends on whether the patient's PaO₂ is above or below approximately 160 mmHg (the room air PO₂).

Why is heparin used in ABG syringes?

Blood begins clotting within seconds of leaving the vessel — this is a normal physiological response. In a blood gas syringe, clotting would make the sample unusable (a clotted sample must be rejected), and if somehow analyzed, would produce inaccurate results and potentially damage analyzer electrodes. Heparin is an anticoagulant that inhibits the coagulation cascade, keeping the blood in a liquid state during transport and analysis. Most modern ABG syringes are preheparinized — the heparin is already present in the syringe body. The amount of heparin must be carefully calibrated: too little allows clotting; too much (excess liquid heparin) can dilute the sample and alter PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ values.

When should co-oximetry be ordered instead of just an ABG?

Co-oximetry is needed whenever the question involves abnormal hemoglobin species — not just total saturation. Order co-oximetry when: (1) Carbon monoxide poisoning is suspected (smoke inhalation, enclosed space, vehicle exposure) — pulse oximetry reads COHb as oxyhemoglobin and will show falsely normal SpO₂. (2) Methemoglobinemia is suspected (dapsone, nitrates, certain local anesthetics). (3) SpO₂ does not match the clinical picture. (4) SpO₂ is below 80% and co-oximetric confirmation is needed. (5) Unexplained severe hypoxemia when other causes have been ruled out. A standard ABG analyzer cannot measure COHb or MetHb — only a co-oximeter with multiple wavelengths can separate these fractions.

Why must FiO₂ be documented with every ABG result?

The PaO₂ value in isolation is meaningless without knowing how much oxygen the patient was receiving when the blood was drawn. A PaO₂ of 80 mmHg on room air (21% O₂) is perfectly normal. A PaO₂ of 80 mmHg on 100% FiO₂ via mechanical ventilation is catastrophically low. Clinicians use the P/F ratio (PaO₂ ÷ FiO₂) and the alveolar-arterial (A-a) gradient — both of which require knowing the FiO₂ — to assess the efficiency of oxygenation. Without documenting the oxygen device, flow, and FiO₂ at the time of the draw, the ABG cannot be properly interpreted and may actually be clinically misleading.

What is the difference between a GEM analyzer and an i-STAT?

Both are blood gas analyzers, but they serve different settings. A GEM system is a stationary, laboratory-grade analyzer typically found in a central lab or blood gas lab. It is maintained by biomedical staff or respiratory therapy, performs regular automated calibrations, requires larger sample volumes, and processes high-volume testing with full panels. The i-STAT is a handheld point-of-care device. It uses single-use, precalibrated cartridges that handle their own calibration internally — no daily maintenance is required. It accepts very small samples and produces results in about 2 minutes. The i-STAT is ideal for bedside, emergency, and transport settings where rapid turnaround is critical and central lab distance is a barrier.

Summary & Board-Prep Reminders

Oxygenation ≠ Ventilation
PaO₂/SpO₂ = oxygenation. PaCO₂/pH = ventilation. A normal SpO₂ never rules out CO₂ retention. Always get an ABG when ventilation status is the question.
Sample Errors — Know the Effects
Air bubble: ↓PaCO₂, ↑pH. Room-temp delay: ↑PaCO₂, ↓pH, ↓PaO₂. Clot: reject and redraw. Wrong FiO₂ documented: invalidates oxygenation interpretation.
Co-Oximetry Is Not Optional in CO Poisoning
Pulse ox reads COHb as oxyhemoglobin — SpO₂ 99% with CO poisoning is possible. Co-oximetry is the only way to measure COHb directly. Never rely on SpO₂ alone after smoke or CO exposure.
QC Failure = No Patient Samples
An analyzer with a failed QC run must be removed from service. No exceptions — not even in emergencies. Send samples to central lab or alternate analyzer until resolved.
