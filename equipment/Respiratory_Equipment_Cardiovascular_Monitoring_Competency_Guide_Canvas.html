Cardiovascular Monitoring Equipment & Procedures

Learn how to obtain reliable electrocardiographic data, set up and level invasive pressure systems, understand pulmonary-artery and cardiac-output monitoring, and distinguish true cardiovascular deterioration from technical artifact.

Before you begin

Cardiovascular monitors are only useful when the signal is technically valid. A misplaced ECG electrode can imitate an abnormal tracing. A pressure transducer that is not leveled correctly can create a false blood-pressure value. A dampened arterial waveform may represent tubing or air in the system rather than sudden hemodynamic collapse.

For the 2027 Respiratory Therapy Examination (RTE), Breadth of Knowledge includes ECG equipment and lead placement, basic heart-rate calculation, arterial-line components, transducer leveling, pulmonary-artery catheter concepts, and thermodilution cardiac output. Depth of Clinical Judgment means asking whether the data are trustworthy, deciding whether a change reflects the patient or the equipment, and determining what should be checked or escalated next.

Big idea: Follow the chain clinical question → monitor selection → setup → signal quality → waveform/value → patient correlation → troubleshooting → decision → reassessment.

Core vocabulary: electrocardiogram (ECG), electrode, lead, artifact, limb lead, precordial lead, V1–V6, right atrium, sinoatrial node, atrioventricular node, QRS complex, 1500 method, invasive arterial pressure, arterial catheter, pressure tubing, flush solution, pressure bag, transducer, zeroing, leveling, phlebostatic axis, overdamping, underdamping, pulmonary artery catheter, right atrial pressure, pulmonary artery pressure, pulmonary artery occlusion pressure, balloon inflation, thermodilution, cardiac output, cardiac index, waveform fidelity.

A 12-lead ECG uses 10 electrodes to create 12 views

A standard diagnostic 12-lead ECG uses four limb electrodes and six precordial chest electrodes. The machine combines electrical signals from these electrodes to create 12 different views of cardiac electrical activity. Accurate placement matters because moving an electrode changes the viewpoint and can change the tracing.

Prepare the skin before blaming the monitor

  1. Explain the procedure and position the patient appropriately.
  2. Expose the required electrode sites while preserving privacy.
  3. Clean and dry the skin; remove oil, lotion, sweat, or debris.
  4. Clip excessive hair when needed for electrode contact.
  5. Apply fresh electrodes firmly to prepared skin.
  6. Connect the correct lead wires and verify cable integrity.
  7. Ask the patient to relax and remain still during acquisition.
  8. Inspect the tracing for artifact before accepting it.

Precordial landmarks

  • V1: fourth intercostal space, right sternal border.
  • V2: fourth intercostal space, left sternal border.
  • V4: fifth intercostal space at the midclavicular line.
  • V3: midway between V2 and V4.
  • V5: level with V4 at the anterior axillary line.
  • V6: level with V4/V5 at the midaxillary line.

The tracing should match the pulse

Cardiac electrical activity normally begins in the sinoatrial node, travels through the atria, passes the atrioventricular node, and then activates the ventricles. The QRS complex represents ventricular depolarization and is commonly used to calculate ventricular rate.

1500 method

Heart rate = 1500 ÷ number of small boxes between consecutive R waves

Example: if there are 20 small boxes between R waves, 1500 ÷ 20 = 75 beats/min. This works best for a regular rhythm.

Do not diagnose from a monitor artifact

If the monitor suddenly displays a very rapid irregular-looking rhythm but the patient is awake, talking, and has a regular palpable pulse, check the tracing quality and electrodes immediately. If the patient is unresponsive or pulseless, treat the patient as an emergency instead of spending time perfecting electrode placement.

Patient-first safety: A collapsing patient is not an “artifact troubleshooting” problem. Check responsiveness and pulse and begin the appropriate emergency response while rhythm assessment continues.

An arterial line measures pressure through a fluid-filled system

The arterial catheter is connected to stiff pressure tubing, a flush solution and pressure system, and a transducer that converts mechanical pressure into an electrical signal. Any air bubble, clot, kink, loose connection, or incorrect transducer position can distort the waveform.

Level at the phlebostatic axis

The transducer should be leveled to the phlebostatic axis, the approximate level of the right atrium. The lesson describes this as the fourth intercostal space at the mid-anterior-posterior diameter of the chest. If the transducer is too low, the displayed pressure reads falsely high. If it is too high, pressure reads falsely low.

Zero before trusting the number

Zeroing exposes the transducer to atmospheric pressure so the monitor can treat atmospheric pressure as the reference point. Re-level and re-zero when the patient's position or transducer position changes enough to alter the reference relationship.

A pulmonary-artery catheter collects several kinds of hemodynamic information

The lesson follows the catheter from the venous system through the right atrium, right ventricle, and into the pulmonary artery. As the catheter advances, waveform shape changes. This helps verify location and connects anatomy with the pressure being measured.

Balloon inflation is temporary and purpose-specific

The distal balloon is inflated during advancement and briefly when obtaining a pulmonary artery occlusion/wedge-type measurement. It should not remain inflated continuously. Prolonged inflation can obstruct pulmonary blood flow and create serious injury.

Thermodilution cardiac output

The thermodilution method injects a known volume of fluid with a known temperature into the catheter and measures the downstream temperature change. The monitor uses the temperature-versus-time curve to estimate cardiac output. Technique matters: injection volume, timing, consistency, catheter position, and patient rhythm can influence the measurement.

Cardiac output versus cardiac index

Cardiac output is the amount of blood pumped by the heart per minute. Cardiac index adjusts cardiac output for body surface area so the value can be interpreted relative to patient size. The equipment does not make the value meaningful by itself; it must be interpreted with perfusion, blood pressure, oxygenation, and the overall clinical picture.

Common mistake: Treating a single hemodynamic number as a diagnosis. A value should fit the waveform, catheter position, patient trend, and other cardiovascular findings before it changes the care plan.

Ask two questions at the same time: “Is the patient changing?” and “Can I trust the monitor?”

Choose the monitor by the question

  • Need electrical rhythm/rate information? ECG monitoring or diagnostic 12-lead ECG.
  • Need continuous beat-to-beat arterial pressure? Invasive arterial-line monitoring.
  • Need more advanced right-heart/pulmonary hemodynamic or cardiac-output data? Pulmonary-artery/hemodynamic monitoring as clinically indicated.

Clinical case: artifact versus ventricular emergency

The monitor suddenly shows a chaotic rapid rhythm. Before assuming a lethal dysrhythmia, look at the patient. If the patient is awake with a normal palpable pulse while the tracing changes when the patient shivers or moves, artifact becomes likely. If the patient is unresponsive and pulseless, the patient—not electrode troubleshooting—takes priority.

Clinical case: arterial-line pressure falls

A patient's arterial pressure suddenly falls after the bed is raised. The patient remains alert, warm, and clinically unchanged. The transducer is now substantially above the phlebostatic axis. Re-level the transducer and compare with an independent cuff pressure before concluding that the patient became hypotensive.

Trend matters

A steadily falling blood pressure accompanied by tachycardia, cool skin, altered mental status, falling urine output, or worsening perfusion is much more convincing than one isolated low number with a poor waveform. Likewise, a new ECG rate or rhythm should be correlated with pulse, blood pressure, symptoms, and signal quality.

Patient-first safety: If the patient is unstable, support circulation/oxygenation and activate the appropriate response while the equipment is checked. Data validation should prevent inappropriate treatment, not delay necessary emergency care.

Procedure support begins before the catheter enters the patient

The respiratory therapist may assist the procedural team by ensuring monitoring and respiratory-support equipment is available and functional. Exact procedural responsibilities vary by setting and scope, but the course competency focuses on equipment readiness and safe physiologic monitoring.

Readiness checks

  • Appropriate ECG and pulse-ox monitoring are functioning.
  • Oxygen and emergency airway equipment are available when clinically appropriate.
  • The pressure-transducer system is assembled, flushed, leveled, and ready to zero when invasive pressure monitoring is planned.
  • Tubing is free of visible air and avoidable kinks.
  • Required monitor cables and connections are available.
  • The patient baseline is known so procedural changes can be recognized quickly.

Watch the patient during the procedure

A procedure can change respiratory or cardiovascular status. Monitor heart rate/rhythm, blood pressure, oxygenation, respiratory pattern, and patient responsiveness as appropriate to the procedure. If instability develops, assist with patient support rather than focusing only on obtaining the planned waveform.

Putting C6 together
Prepare a functioning monitoring system before the procedure, know the patient's baseline, identify equipment problems early, and prioritize physiologic stability if the procedure produces deterioration.

Cardiovascular monitoring: validate the signal before you trust the number

ECG electrodes, pressure tubing, transducers, catheters, and monitors are measurement systems. The RTE expects you to understand what they measure, how setup affects accuracy, and when a strange value is a technical problem versus a real patient emergency.

patient → monitor → setup → signal/waveform → value → clinical fit → troubleshoot or intervene → reassess

RTE Depth of Clinical Judgment: take the next step

  • Information Gathering: compare the ECG/pressure value with pulse, blood pressure, mental status, perfusion, symptoms, waveform quality, electrode contact, transducer level, and tubing integrity.
  • Information Gathering: identify the technical check most likely to distinguish true deterioration from artifact or pressure-system error.
  • Decision Making: correct misplaced/poor-contact ECG electrodes before interpreting a stable patient's abnormal tracing.
  • Decision Making: treat an unresponsive or pulseless patient as an emergency rather than spending time troubleshooting possible ECG artifact first.
  • Decision Making: re-level and re-zero an arterial transducer after a meaningful position change before diagnosing hypotension from a discordant value.
  • Decision Making: inspect and correct air, clot, kink, loose connections, or catheter-position issues when the arterial waveform becomes dampened.
  • Decision Making: compare invasive pressure with an independent measurement when the waveform/value does not fit the patient's condition.
  • Decision Making: interpret hemodynamic measurements only after verifying catheter/transducer configuration and measurement technique.
  • Reassessment: confirm that corrected equipment produces a believable waveform/value and that the patient remains clinically stable.
  • Communication: report the observed cardiovascular change, signal-quality concern, troubleshooting performed, and whether the change persisted after technical correction.
