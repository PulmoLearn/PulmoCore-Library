How the Nervous System Is Organized

The nervous system is divided into the central nervous system and the peripheral nervous system. The central nervous system contains the brain and spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system includes the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord.

Afferent pathways
Carry sensory information toward the central nervous system.
Efferent pathways
Carry motor commands away from the central nervous system toward muscles and glands.
Somatic motor system
Controls voluntary skeletal-muscle movement.
Autonomic nervous system
Regulates involuntary functions such as heart rate, vascular tone, digestion, glands, and airway smooth muscle.
Key distinction: Skeletal-muscle movement is primarily somatic. Cardiac muscle, glands, blood vessels, and airway smooth muscle are influenced by the autonomic nervous system.

Sympathetic and Parasympathetic Balance

The autonomic nervous system uses two major divisions that often produce opposing effects. Together they help maintain homeostasis.

Neurotransmission and the Neuroeffector Site

A neurotransmitter is released from a nerve ending and binds to a receptor on the target tissue. The functional junction where the nerve influences an organ, gland, or smooth muscle is the neuroeffector site.

Acetylcholine
The major neurotransmitter at parasympathetic neuroeffector sites and at all autonomic ganglia.
Norepinephrine
The principal neurotransmitter at most sympathetic neuroeffector sites.
Receptor determines response
The same transmitter can produce different effects depending on receptor type and tissue.
Clinical connection: Acetylcholine at airway muscarinic receptors causes bronchial smooth-muscle constriction and increased secretions. Blocking those receptors produces bronchodilation and drying of secretions.

Adrenergic Receptors: Alpha and Beta

Adrenergic receptors respond primarily to sympathetic neurotransmitters and sympathomimetic medications.

Alpha-1
Primarily vascular smooth-muscle contraction and vasoconstriction.
Beta-1
Primarily cardiac stimulation: increased heart rate, conduction, and contractility.
Beta-2
Primarily airway smooth-muscle relaxation and bronchodilation.

Beta-1 vocabulary

Chronotropic
Affects heart rate.
Dromotropic
Affects electrical conduction through the heart.
Inotropic
Affects force of myocardial contraction.
Positive effect
An increase in the named function.

Cholinergic Receptors: Muscarinic and Nicotinic

Cholinergic receptors respond to acetylcholine. The two major receptor families are muscarinic and nicotinic.

Muscarinic receptors
Located on parasympathetic target organs, including airway smooth muscle and glands.
Nicotinic receptors
Located at autonomic ganglia and the neuromuscular junction.

Airway effect: Acetylcholine binding to airway muscarinic receptors promotes bronchoconstriction and secretion. Antimuscarinic drugs such as ipratropium and tiotropium block this response.
