Acetylcholine Starts Skeletal-Muscle Contraction

At the neuromuscular junction, a motor nerve releases acetylcholine. Acetylcholine binds nicotinic receptors on skeletal muscle, depolarizes the membrane, and triggers calcium release inside the muscle fiber. Calcium then permits actin and myosin interaction.

Acetylcholinesterase rapidly breaks down acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft. This ends receptor stimulation and allows the contraction to stop.

Neuromuscular Blockers Cause Paralysis—Not Sedation or Analgesia

NMBAs can reduce spontaneous movement, decrease oxygen consumption from muscle activity, prevent dislodgement of critical devices, and improve patient–ventilator synchrony in selected situations. They do not increase spontaneous breathing.

Never equate immobility with comfort. A paralyzed patient requires adequate analgesia, sedation, ventilatory support, eye care, pressure-injury prevention, and frequent reassessment.

Monitor the Degree of Blockade and Match the Reversal Strategy

Peripheral nerve stimulation and train-of-four assessment help estimate neuromuscular blockade. Cholinesterase inhibitors such as neostigmine increase acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction and may reverse selected nondepolarizing agents. Reversal should be guided by the specific NMBA, depth of blockade, and protocol.

Any Deep Sedation Can Threaten Ventilation and Airway Protection

Propofol, benzodiazepines, opioids, general anesthetics, and sufficiently deep ketamine sedation can impair airway reflexes or ventilation. Topical lidocaine intentionally suppresses local airway sensation during procedures such as bronchoscopy.

Monitor depth continuously. A patient can progress from moderate sedation to deep sedation, apnea, hypotension, or loss of protective reflexes.

Anesthesia Means Loss of Sensation

Local anesthesia blocks sensation in a specific area. General anesthesia produces a reversible state of unconsciousness with loss of sensation and protective reflexes. Inhaled anesthetics can reduce tidal volume and blunt ventilatory responses to hypoxemia and hypercapnia.

Lidocaine is commonly used for topical airway anesthesia during bronchoscopy. Nitrous oxide is associated with postoperative nausea and vomiting in susceptible patients.

Analgesia Reduces Pain Perception

Opioids such as morphine and fentanyl provide analgesia but can cause sedation, nausea, constipation, hypotension, and dose-dependent ventilatory depression. Epidural administration is possible for selected agents and procedures.

Naloxone reverses opioid effects, including ventilatory depression. Its duration may be shorter than the opioid’s, so recurrent depression is possible.

Flumazenil reverses benzodiazepine effects in selected situations, but it may precipitate seizures in susceptible or dependent patients.

Key Takeaways

Paralysis is not sedation
Always provide appropriate analgesia and sedation before and during NMBA therapy.
Reversal must match the class
Neostigmine for selected nondepolarizing blockade, flumazenil for benzodiazepines, and naloxone for opioids.
Airway risk rises with depth
Sedatives, anesthetics, and opioids can suppress ventilation and airway reflexes.
Monitor continuously
Use clinical sedation scales, respiratory monitoring, and neuromuscular assessment rather than immobility alone.
