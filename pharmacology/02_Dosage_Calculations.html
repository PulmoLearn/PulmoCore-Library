Safe Number Writing Prevents Medication Errors

Medication calculations are only safe when the number is written clearly. A misplaced decimal can create a tenfold or hundredfold error even when the calculation method is correct.

Use a leading zero
Write 0.5 mg, not .5 mg.
Never use a trailing zero
Write 5 mg, not 5.0 mg.
Reason: “.5 mg” may be misread as 5 mg. “5.0 mg” may be misread as 50 mg.

The Metric System: Powers of Ten

The metric system is based on powers of 10. That makes conversion predictable: moving to a smaller unit increases the numerical value, while moving to a larger unit decreases it.

Dimensional Analysis: Let the Units Guide You

Dimensional analysis uses conversion factors written as fractions. Arrange each factor so unwanted units cancel and the desired unit remains.

Example:
125 lb × (1 kg / 2.2 lb) = 56.8 kg ≈ 57 kg
Safety check: Ask whether the answer is reasonable. Converting pounds to kilograms should produce a smaller number.

A four-step routine

1. Identify the target unit
mg, mL, kg, or another required unit.
2. Write the known value
Include both number and unit.
3. Multiply by conversion factors
Orient factors so units cancel.
4. Verify magnitude and notation
Check decimal placement and safe rounding.

Weight-Based Dosing

Many respiratory medications are ordered by body weight. The basic relationship is:

Ordered dose = dose per kilogram × patient weight in kilograms

When the patient’s weight is given in pounds, convert to kilograms first:

kg = lb ÷ 2.2

Percent strength

A percent weight/volume solution expresses grams of solute per 100 mL of solution.

1% = 1 g / 100 mL = 10 mg/mL
0.02% = 0.02 g / 100 mL = 0.2 mg/mL

Dilution and Reconstitution

Dilution lowers concentration by adding solvent. The amount of drug remains the same unless drug is removed or added. Reconstitution adds a specified volume of diluent to a powder or concentrated product to create a usable solution.

C₁V₁ = C₂V₂
Use this relationship when the amount of drug stays constant while volume and concentration change.

Calculating Volume from the Dose on Hand

When a vial or solution lists a concentration, calculate the volume needed using:

Volume to give = ordered dose ÷ concentration on hand
Example: 4 mg ordered ÷ 10 mg/mL = 0.4 mL.

Continuous Therapy Requires One More Dimension: Time

For continuous nebulization or infusions, include the rate and duration:

Total dose = dose rate × weight × time

Confirm that the units cancel to the requested final unit.

Key Takeaways

Carry the units
Units are part of the calculation and reveal setup errors.
Convert weight first
Most medication dosing uses kilograms, not pounds or grams.
Dose and concentration are different
Dilution changes concentration and volume, not the amount of active drug already present.
Check reasonableness
Verify decimal placement, magnitude, concentration, and whether the final unit matches the order.
