Acute Hypoxemia Produces Immediate Compensatory Responses

Tachypnea, tachycardia, anxiety, increased work of breathing, and sympathetic activation can occur rapidly. Polycythemia is a chronic adaptation and does not develop during an acute episode.

High Flow Means the System Meets or Exceeds Inspiratory Demand

A true high-flow system supplies enough total flow to meet or exceed the patient’s peak inspiratory flow. Humidification and precise FiO₂ may be present, but they do not define high flow by themselves.

Air-entrainment principle: As the selected oxygen percentage decreases, more room air is entrained and total flow increases.

More Oxygen Is Not Always Better

ComplicationMechanismPrevention
Absorption atelectasisNitrogen washout allows alveolar gas to be absorbed rapidlyUse the lowest effective FiO₂ and apply recruitment/PEEP when indicated
Oxygen toxicityReactive oxygen species injure pulmonary tissue during prolonged high exposureReduce FiO₂ as soon as clinically possible
ROPExcess oxygen exposure disrupts retinal vascular development in premature infantsUse neonatal saturation targets and avoid hyperoxemia
Hyperbaric oxygen riskPressure and oxygen exposure may cause barotrauma or oxygen toxicityUse only for approved indications and monitor carefully

Hyperbaric oxygen may be used for selected cases of carbon monoxide poisoning, decompression sickness, and arterial gas embolism. COPD alone is not an indication.

Titrate Oxygen in COPD to the Patient—not to an Outdated “Hypoxic Drive” Rule

Oxygen-induced hypercapnia can occur in susceptible COPD patients through worsening V/Q mismatch, the Haldane effect, and a smaller contribution from reduced ventilatory drive. The safest approach is controlled oxygen with repeat assessment rather than withholding oxygen from a hypoxemic patient.

Target the prescribed saturation range. Many patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure are initially targeted to an SpO₂ around 88%–92% until ABG results clarify the response.

Heliox Reduces Gas Density and Can Reduce Turbulent Resistance

Heliox is a helium–oxygen mixture. Because helium is less dense than nitrogen, heliox can reduce turbulent flow resistance through narrowed large airways and may improve aerosol delivery in selected patients.

Important limitation: Heliox is an adjunct, not a bronchodilator. Its benefit decreases as the required oxygen concentration rises because less helium is available.

Inhaled Nitric Oxide Is a Selective Pulmonary Vasodilator

Inhaled nitric oxide reaches ventilated lung units, relaxes pulmonary vascular smooth muscle, lowers pulmonary vascular resistance, and can improve V/Q matching. It has minimal systemic vasodilator effect because it is rapidly inactivated by hemoglobin.

Common indication
Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn in appropriately selected infants.
Major safety issue
Abrupt discontinuation can cause rebound pulmonary hypertension and worsening hypoxemia.

Monitoring includes oxygenation, hemodynamics, methemoglobin, nitrogen dioxide exposure, and device function. Inhaled nitric oxide is not a routine treatment for acute asthma.

Key Takeaways

Treat the mechanism
Hypoventilation, low V/Q, dead space, and shunt are not interchangeable.
Match the device to demand
FiO₂, total flow, humidification, and interface all matter.
Titrate oxygen carefully
Correct hypoxemia while limiting hyperoxia and monitoring ventilation.
Specialty gases are targeted therapies
Heliox reduces density; inhaled nitric oxide lowers pulmonary vascular resistance.
