Fever Suggests Infection, but It Does Not Identify the Pathogen

Fever commonly occurs with both bacterial and viral infection. A decreased respiratory rate is not a typical cause of fever. Temperature must be interpreted with symptoms, imaging, white blood cell findings, sputum characteristics, and microbiology data.

Bacterial clues
Focal infiltrate, purulent sputum, neutrophilia, positive culture, and compatible clinical course.
Viral clues
Exposure history, systemic symptoms, viral testing, diffuse airway involvement, and epidemiologic pattern.
Do not treat fever alone. Anti-infective selection should be based on the likely or confirmed organism, severity, site of infection, resistance patterns, allergies, and patient-specific factors.

Gram Stain Reflects Cell-Wall Structure

Gram-positive bacteria retain crystal violet and appear purple because of their thick peptidoglycan layer. Gram-negative bacteria have a thinner peptidoglycan layer plus an outer membrane and stain pink or red after counterstaining.

FindingExamplesWhy it matters
Gram-positiveStaphylococci, streptococci, clostridia, listeriaSuggests a thick peptidoglycan cell wall
Gram-negativePseudomonas, Klebsiella, Legionella, HaemophilusSuggests an outer membrane and different antimicrobial susceptibility

Susceptibility testing determines which antimicrobial agents inhibit or kill the organism. It does not identify the organism’s gram category or distinguish bacteria from viruses.

Antibacterial Therapy Must Balance Coverage and Stewardship

Broad-spectrum antibiotics cover a wide range of organisms. Narrow-spectrum therapy targets a smaller group. Empiric broad coverage may be needed initially in severe illness, but therapy should be narrowed when culture data allow.

Aerosolized Anti-Infectives Require Airway Preparation

Inhaled antimicrobials can irritate the airway and provoke bronchospasm. When prescribed, a bronchodilator is commonly administered first to improve airway caliber and tolerance.

Before treatment
Assess breath sounds, airway reactivity, vital signs, oxygenation, and ability to use the device.
During and after treatment
Monitor for cough, bronchospasm, dyspnea, voice change, systemic toxicity, and treatment effectiveness.

Use the device approved or specified for the medication. Different aerosolized antimicrobials may require different nebulizers, filters, scavenging systems, or environmental precautions.

Drug Interactions and Nonadherence Can Turn Therapy into Harm

Macrolides such as erythromycin can inhibit theophylline metabolism and raise serum concentrations. New headache, anxiety, nausea, tremor, insomnia, palpitations, or arrhythmias should prompt medication review and possible serum-level assessment.

Tuberculosis treatment requires multiple drugs for a prolonged period. Missed doses and incomplete treatment allow resistant organisms to survive and multiply.

Viruses Are Classified by Their Genetic Material

Viruses contain either RNA or DNA as their genetic material. Antivirals target virus-specific steps rather than bacterial cell walls.

Fungal Infection Risk Increases When Normal Defenses Are Disrupted

Immunosuppression, prolonged antibiotic exposure, and inhaled corticosteroids can increase fungal risk. Viral immunization does not cause fungal infection.

Oral candidiasis: White or patchy oral lesions and pain after inhaled corticosteroid use suggest Candida, especially when the patient does not rinse and spit after treatment.
