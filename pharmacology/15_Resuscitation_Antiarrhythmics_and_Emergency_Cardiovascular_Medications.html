VF and Pulseless VT Require CPR and Early Defibrillation

Survival depends most strongly on immediate high-quality CPR and rapid defibrillation. Synchronized cardioversion is not used for a pulseless shockable rhythm because there is no organized R wave to synchronize with.

Medication does not replace electricity. Epinephrine and an antiarrhythmic may be added during the algorithm, but neither should delay defibrillation or interrupt compressions.

Asystole and PEA Are Treated Without Defibrillation

For asystole and PEA, provide high-quality CPR, administer epinephrine promptly, support oxygenation and ventilation, and search for reversible causes. Confirm apparent asystole in more than one lead and verify connections.

Current correction: Atropine and lidocaine are not routine treatments for asystole. Vasopressin is not used as a simple substitute for epinephrine in the standard adult cardiac-arrest algorithm.

Use IV or IO Access for Resuscitation Medications

Peripheral IV or intraosseous access is preferred. IO access should be obtained promptly when IV access cannot be established. Endotracheal drug administration is unreliable and is not the preferred modern route.

Historical context: Older materials taught 2–2.5 times the IV dose for certain endotracheal medications. Current practice prioritizes IV or IO access rather than memorizing endotracheal dosing.

Epinephrine Improves Perfusion Pressure During Cardiac Arrest

Epinephrine stimulates alpha and beta receptors. During arrest, alpha-mediated vasoconstriction increases aortic diastolic pressure and can improve coronary and cerebral perfusion. Its beta effects increase heart rate, contractility, arrhythmia risk, and myocardial oxygen demand.

Epinephrine is also first-line therapy for anaphylaxis. Extra caution is appropriate in patients vulnerable to tachyarrhythmias, including those receiving digoxin, but life-threatening indications still require treatment.

Vasopressin distinction: Vasopressin may be used for vasodilatory shock in selected settings, but it is not the routine replacement for epinephrine in the current adult cardiac-arrest algorithm.

Treat Symptomatic Bradycardia When It Causes Poor Perfusion

Bradycardia may result from hypoxia, ischemia, medications, conduction-system disease, or excessive parasympathetic tone. Decreased parasympathetic tone generally increases rather than decreases heart rate.

For adult symptomatic bradycardia with cardiopulmonary compromise, atropine is given as a 1 mg IV bolus, repeated every 3–5 minutes to a maximum total dose of 3 mg. If ineffective, consider transcutaneous pacing and/or dopamine or epinephrine infusion.

Adenosine Temporarily Blocks AV-Nodal Conduction

Adenosine is used for selected regular narrow-complex tachycardias. Give 6 mg by rapid IV push followed immediately by a saline flush; a second dose of 12 mg may be used if needed.

Do not use adenosine blindly. Avoid it for irregular or polymorphic wide-complex tachycardia. In asthma, adenosine can provoke bronchospasm, so risk and alternatives must be considered.

For atrial-fibrillation rate control in a patient with asthma, nonselective beta blockers such as propranolol pose greater bronchospasm risk than beta-1 selective agents, though all choices require individualized assessment.

Key Takeaways

Electricity treats shockable rhythms
VF and pulseless VT require early defibrillation and uninterrupted CPR.
PEA and asystole require causes
Use CPR, epinephrine, and the Hs and Ts—not defibrillation.
Use current doses
Atropine 1 mg for adult bradycardia, amiodarone 300 then 150 mg in arrest, adenosine 6 then 12 mg.
Access and timing matter
Use IV or IO delivery and never delay CPR or defibrillation for medication preparation.
