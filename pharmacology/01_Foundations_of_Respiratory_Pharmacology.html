The Language of Pharmacology

Pharmacology is the study of drugs and their effects on living systems. It asks two connected questions: what the body does to a drug, and what the drug does to the body.

Therapeutic use
The intended clinical purpose of a medication, such as reversing bronchospasm or reducing airway inflammation.
Pharmacotherapy
The use of medications as part of a treatment plan after weighing expected benefit against risk.
Disease management
A broader plan that can include medication, education, lifestyle change, equipment, and follow-up.

Respiratory care connection: A bronchodilator may be one part of asthma management, but education, inhaler technique, trigger reduction, and reassessment are also essential.

Drug Approval, Naming, and Labeling

Before a medication reaches routine clinical use, it moves through preclinical testing and phased human trials designed to evaluate safety, dose, effectiveness, and adverse effects. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulates drug approval. Within the FDA, the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) evaluates most prescription and over-the-counter drugs. Approval does not mean risk-free; it means evidence supports use under defined conditions.

Generic name
The standard name of the active medication, such as albuterol.
Trade name
A manufacturer’s branded product name. More than one brand may contain the same generic drug.
Indication
A condition or clinical purpose for which the medication is used.
Off-label use
Use outside the formally approved labeling when supported by evidence and clinical judgment.
Package information commonly includes: indications, dosing, preparation, route, contraindications, warnings, adverse reactions, interactions, storage, age limits, and special-population considerations. Age recommendations are product-specific, so clinicians should verify the current label rather than assume all formulations have the same age indication.

Medication reconciliation

Medication reconciliation means comparing the patient’s current medications, allergies, supplements, and recent changes with the active orders. This reduces omissions, duplications, interactions, and accidental continuation of inappropriate therapy.

Contraindications, Warnings, Interactions, and Reactions

Contraindication
A condition in which a medication should not be used because the risk is unacceptable.
Warning or precaution
A risk that requires heightened monitoring, dose adjustment, or careful selection.
Side effect
A predictable secondary effect that may be mild, tolerable, or clinically important.
Allergic reaction
An immune response ranging from rash to anaphylaxis. It is not the same as a predictable side effect.

Drug interactions

Synergism occurs when combined drugs produce a greater effect than either alone. Potentiation occurs when one substance increases the effect of another. Interactions may improve treatment or increase toxicity.

Selectivity and dose

A selective drug prefers one receptor type, but selectivity is rarely absolute. As dose rises, effects at other receptors may become more likely. A beta-2 agonist can therefore cause bronchodilation while still producing tremor or tachycardia.

Pregnancy risk and teratogenicity

Some medications can affect fetal development. A drug with teratogenic potential can cause structural or functional harm to the developing fetus. Medication decisions during pregnancy require a patient-specific risk–benefit assessment; “avoid all drugs” is not safe when untreated disease also threatens the patient or fetus.

Clinical principle: The prescribed dose is not automatically the deposited lung dose. Device choice, technique, inspiratory flow, airway obstruction, and circuit setup all influence delivery.

Why inhalation is often preferred: it places medication directly at the airway target and can reduce systemic exposure. These advantages are lost when device selection or technique is poor.

Pharmacokinetics: What the Body Does to the Drug

Absorption
Movement from the administration site into the bloodstream.
Distribution
Movement through blood and tissues toward sites of action.
Metabolism
Chemical alteration, primarily in the liver, into active or inactive metabolites.
Elimination
Removal through kidneys, feces, exhaled gas, sweat, or other routes.

ADME matters clinically: Liver or kidney impairment can increase drug exposure, prolong action, and raise toxicity risk.

Half-life and steady drug levels

The half-life is the time required for the amount or concentration of a drug to decrease by 50%. It helps determine dosing intervals, accumulation, and how long effects or adverse effects may persist.

Tolerance and receptor downregulation

Tolerance is a reduced response after repeated exposure. One mechanism is receptor downregulation, in which repeated stimulation causes fewer receptors to remain available or responsive. A patient using a rescue bronchodilator many times each day may report that it “does not work like it used to.” The correct response is not automatically to increase the dose; reassess disease control, technique, adherence, and the need for maintenance therapy.

Clinical distinction: Tolerance describes the reduced clinical response over time. Desensitization describes reduced receptor or tissue responsiveness after ongoing exposure. Downregulation is one mechanism in which fewer receptors remain available.

Pharmacodynamics: What the Drug Does to the Body

Pharmacodynamics describes the physiologic and biochemical effects caused by a drug. Drugs may bind receptors, change ion channels, alter enzymes, affect intracellular messengers, or disrupt essential cellular processes.

Agonist
Binds to a receptor and activates it. A beta-2 agonist promotes airway smooth-muscle relaxation.
Antagonist
Binds to a receptor and blocks activation by another substance.
Affinity
The strength with which a drug binds to a receptor.
Potency
The amount of drug required to produce a given effect. Greater potency does not automatically mean greater clinical benefit.
Efficacy
The maximum effect a drug can produce once its target is sufficiently activated.

Lock-and-key is useful but incomplete: receptors can change sensitivity, drugs may bind more than one target, and the same receptor can produce different effects in different tissues.

Beta-2 signaling example: beta-2 receptor stimulation activates adenyl cyclase, which converts ATP to cyclic AMP (cAMP). Increased cAMP activates protein kinase A, lowers intracellular calcium activity, and promotes airway smooth-muscle relaxation.

Overdose and antidotes

Overdose may result from excessive dosing, duplication, impaired metabolism or elimination, accidental exposure, or intentional ingestion. An antidote counteracts a specific toxic effect. Examples include naloxone for opioid toxicity and acetylcysteine for acetaminophen toxicity.

Medication Orders, Administration Rights, and Safe Practice

Interpreting orders

Medication orders should identify the patient, drug, dose, route, frequency or timing, and any relevant indication or parameters. Ambiguous, incomplete, or unsafe orders must be clarified before administration.

AbbreviationMeaningSafety point
BIDTwice dailyTiming should still be clarified by policy or schedule
QIDFour times dailyNot identical to every 6 hours in all contexts
Q4HEvery 4 hoursIncludes overnight unless otherwise specified
PRNAs neededRequires an indication and safe interval or limit
STATImmediatelyUsed when treatment must be given without delay
NPONothing by mouthDoes not automatically mean all medications are withheld; clarify the order and route
Avoid error-prone notation: use a leading zero for doses less than one (0.5 mg), never use a trailing zero (5 mg, not 5.0 mg), and avoid unsafe abbreviations such as QD or QOD.

Key Takeaways

Read beyond the drug name
Indication, dose, route, timing, warnings, and patient-specific risks all matter.
Separate kinetics from dynamics
Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to the drug; pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body.
Reconcile before administering
Compare the patient’s report, medication list, allergies, supplements, and active orders.
Reassess after therapy
Safe administration includes monitoring both the intended response and adverse effects.
