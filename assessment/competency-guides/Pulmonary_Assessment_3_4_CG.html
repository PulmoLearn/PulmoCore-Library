Pulmonary Assessment • 3.4 Competency Guide

Clinical Documentation, HIPAA & Professional Compliance

Learn how respiratory therapists create clinically useful records, document treatment response and adverse reactions, correct records transparently, protect protected health information, use approved communication and photography workflows, and follow required reporting processes without widening disclosure.

Before you begin

Documentation is not clerical cleanup after patient care. It is part of patient care. The respiratory record should allow the next clinician to understand the patient's current respiratory condition, what therapy was delivered, whether it helped, whether it caused harm, what communication occurred, what still needs attention, and what should happen next.

Privacy and compliance work the same way. The goal is not to prevent appropriate information sharing. The goal is to make sure the right information reaches the right person, for the right purpose, through the right channel, with no unnecessary disclosure.

Big idea for Week 3.4: Use the sequence document what actually happened → compare before and after → record benefit and harm → communicate the next step → protect the information pathway → use approved reporting and correction processes.

C1: Select the respiratory care assessment form or treatment form according to the purpose, timing, and scope of the encounter.

Choose the record that matches the encounter

A respiratory care assessment form creates a comprehensive respiratory picture. It is used for the initial respiratory evaluation and again when a significant change in patient status requires a new whole-patient assessment.

A respiratory treatment form documents an individual therapy session. It should show why the therapy was given, the patient's condition before the treatment, what was delivered, how the patient responded, whether adverse effects occurred, and what follow-up is required.

When to use the comprehensive assessment form

  • initial respiratory evaluation;
  • significant increase in oxygen or ventilatory support;
  • new altered mental status;
  • new unilateral breath-sound loss;
  • rapid respiratory deterioration;
  • major treatment intolerance or another significant change requiring a full reassessment.

When to use the treatment form

Complete a treatment record after each therapy session and, when feasible, before leaving the patient-care area. Each session should be traceable to a patient, date, time, therapist, modality, medication or setting, baseline, response, adverse effects, and follow-up.

Why timing matters

End-of-shift reconstruction increases the chance that values, times, device settings, responses, and communication details will be omitted or remembered incorrectly. Timely documentation also allows the next clinician to act on a significant change immediately.

Clinical reasoning: The assessment form answers, “What is the patient's overall respiratory condition?” The treatment form answers, “What happened during this specific therapy session?”
Putting C1 together
Use the comprehensive form for the whole respiratory picture and the treatment form for each individual therapy and response.
C2: Complete a comprehensive respiratory assessment record using current, relevant, objective data from the history, physical examination, diagnostics, oxygen support, orders, assessment, and plan.

Build a record another clinician can safely use

Patient and encounter context

Verify the correct patient, encounter, date/time, location, and responsible provider before entering findings. A beautifully written note in the wrong chart is a patient-safety event.

Chief complaint and history

Record the main respiratory concern, onset, progression, aggravating and relieving factors, associated symptoms, relevant cardiopulmonary history, smoking or vaping exposure, allergies, home oxygen or respiratory equipment, home medications, and baseline function.

Current vital signs and oxygen support

Document heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, temperature when relevant, SpO2, oxygen device, flow or FiO2, and the patient's condition at the time of measurement. SpO2 without the oxygen setting is incomplete.

Physical assessment

Include breath sounds by location and quality, work of breathing, chest movement, cough effectiveness, secretion amount and character when relevant, mental status, ability to speak, distress, and other findings that affect interpretation.

Diagnostic information

Summarize current imaging, arterial blood gases, laboratory results, microbiology, pulmonary function findings, or other tests that affect respiratory decisions. Include timing and test conditions when they change meaning—for example, the FiO2 during an arterial blood gas.

Orders and current therapy

Reconcile the active respiratory orders with what the patient is actually receiving. Identify duplicate therapy, missing orders, contraindications, discrepancies, or unclear instructions before proceeding.

Assessment and plan

The assessment should interpret what the current evidence supports without overdiagnosing. The plan should identify what should be initiated, continued, modified, discontinued, verified, communicated, or reassessed and should include a time or trigger for reassessment.

Use current data

Six-hour-old oxygen saturation or yesterday's breath sounds may be useful historical comparison, but they are not a substitute for current findings when the patient is changing.

Clinical example: A patient with pneumonia has SpO2 92% on a simple mask at 6 L/min, right-lower-lobe crackles, productive yellow sputum, and a same-day chest radiograph showing right-lower-lobe consolidation. A complete assessment connects the support, bedside findings, imaging, current orders, interpretation, and reassessment plan rather than listing them as unrelated facts.
Putting C2 together
Document current data, their clinical conditions, what they mean, and what the next clinician needs to know or do.
C3: Complete a treatment record after each therapy session using required pre-treatment, mid-treatment when indicated, and post-treatment findings.

A treatment record must prove both delivery and evaluation

Pre-treatment: establish the baseline

Before therapy, record the indication, therapy or medication, relevant vital signs, SpO2 with oxygen support, breath sounds, work of breathing, cough and secretions, position, symptoms, mental status, and any parameter required by the modality or medication.

Mid-treatment: monitor prolonged therapy when policy requires it

For prolonged treatments, mid-treatment data may include heart rate, respiratory rate, SpO2, symptoms, tolerance, or early adverse effects. Mid-treatment documentation does not replace the final post-treatment assessment.

Post-treatment: determine the outcome

Repeat the findings that matter to the original indication. If the goal was bronchospasm relief, compare wheezing, airflow, respiratory rate, dyspnea, and heart rate. If the goal was secretion clearance, compare cough, sputum mobilization, breath sounds, and work of breathing.

The oxygen setting must be part of the comparison

A rise in SpO2 from 90% to 94% means something different if the oxygen setting remained unchanged than if oxygen was simultaneously increased. State the device and flow or FiO2 before and after.

Record interruption or termination

If therapy is stopped early, record why, how much was delivered when known, the patient's condition, immediate action, notification, and reassessment plan.

Patient refusal still requires documentation

Record the therapy offered, objective patient condition, the patient's stated reason for refusal, education about expected benefits and risks, the final patient decision, required notification, and the follow-up or reassessment plan. Avoid judgmental wording.

Weak note: “Albuterol given; tolerated well.”
Stronger note: records the baseline, dose, oxygen setting, pre/post breath sounds and vital signs, patient-reported response, adverse effects, notification, and follow-up.
Putting C3 together
Pre-treatment establishes the baseline, mid-treatment monitors prolonged therapy when required, and post-treatment determines whether the intervention helped, failed, or caused harm.
C4: Compare pre- and post-treatment findings to determine therapeutic benefit, no meaningful change, deterioration, or an adverse response.

Effectiveness and tolerance are separate judgments

Therapeutic benefit

Benefit is shown by a meaningful improvement in the problem the therapy was intended to treat. Examples include reduced wheezing, lower respiratory rate, improved secretion clearance, improved inspiratory capacity, less dyspnea, or improved oxygenation on the same or less support.

No meaningful change

No response is clinically important. If the same symptoms, breath sounds, oxygen need, work of breathing, and objective values remain unchanged, the record should say so. Repeated ineffective treatment may need modification or further evaluation.

Deterioration

A patient may worsen despite treatment. Increased dyspnea, falling SpO2 on the same support, rising respiratory rate, decreased airflow, new altered mental status, or another worsening finding should be documented and communicated.

Adverse response

An adverse response is an unwanted effect associated with treatment. Bronchodilator-related examples include tachycardia, palpitations, tremor, anxiety, chest discomfort, and paradoxical bronchospasm.

A treatment can help and harm at the same time

If wheezing decreases but the patient develops significant tachycardia and palpitations, both findings belong in the record. Improved respiratory findings do not erase cardiovascular harm.

Example: Before Albuterol: HR 88/min, RR 24/min, SpO2 91% on 2 L/min nasal cannula, diffuse expiratory wheezing, moderate dyspnea. After: HR 118/min, RR 20/min, SpO2 94% on the same support, wheezing reduced, easier breathing, and palpitations. The treatment produced respiratory benefit and an adverse heart-rate response under the course rule.
Documentation principle: Do not use “better,” “stable,” or “tolerated well” when measurable comparison can show exactly what changed.
Putting C4 together
Compare the same clinically relevant variables before and after therapy and document benefit, no change, deterioration, and adverse effects independently.
C5: Calculate the course bronchodilator heart-rate threshold and document the baseline, threshold, post-treatment value, symptoms, action, notification, and reassessment.

Course bronchodilator tachycardia rule

For this course, post-treatment tachycardia is classified as an adverse response when the heart rate exceeds 130% of the pretreatment baseline or exceeds 125 beats/min. These are independent triggers. This is a course-specific learning rule and should always be integrated with current facility medication policy, rhythm, symptoms, and whole-patient stability.

Patient-specific threshold = baseline HR × 1.30

Example 1: percentage trigger

Pretreatment HR = 88/min. Calculate 88 × 1.30 = 114.4/min. Post-treatment HR = 118/min. The patient exceeded 130% of baseline, so the course rule classifies the response as adverse even though 118 is below the independent 125/min threshold.

Example 2: Unit 3 exam case

Pretreatment HR = 92/min. Calculate 92 × 1.30 = 119.6/min. Post-treatment HR = 124/min and the patient reports palpitations. The post-treatment rate exceeds the patient-specific threshold, so the adverse cardiovascular response must be documented even though wheezing improved.

What the complete adverse-response entry should show

  • pretreatment HR;
  • calculated 130% threshold;
  • post-treatment HR;
  • rhythm or relevant cardiac assessment when indicated;
  • patient symptoms such as palpitations or chest discomfort;
  • other relevant vital signs;
  • respiratory response, including benefit;
  • amount of treatment delivered if interrupted;
  • action taken;
  • person notified and time;
  • recommendation or orders received;
  • reassessment plan and follow-up result.

Patient care comes before finishing the note

If the patient develops clinically important symptoms or instability, stop or modify therapy according to the patient's condition and policy, assess the patient, provide supportive care, notify appropriately, and then complete the documentation.

Do not chart only the respiratory benefit: “Wheezing improved” is incomplete when the same treatment also produced a significant adverse heart-rate response.
Putting C5 together
Calculate baseline × 1.30, compare with the independent 125/min trigger, and document the complete respiratory and cardiovascular response.
C6: Use objective, specific, timely, complete, and legally defensible documentation, including approved correction, addendum, and late-entry practices.

Describe what happened without hiding the record

Objective and specific wording

Replace vague or judgmental labels with observable facts. Instead of “patient difficult,” document the actual behavior: “Patient removed mask twice and stated, ‘I cannot tolerate this pressure.’ SpO2 fell from 93% to 87% during mask removal.”

Document only what actually occurred

Do not pre-chart care, copy forward findings that were not independently verified, chart another clinician's treatment as your own, or document education that was not provided.

Correction

Use the approved EHR or paper correction process. Do not erase, delete, overwrite, backdate, or conceal the original entry. Identify what is being corrected and provide the accurate information while preserving authorship and chronology.

Addendum

An addendum adds clinically important information after the original note. It should be attributable to the author and date/time and should not make it appear that the added information was present in the original entry.

Late entry

A late entry documents information after the event occurred. The current entry time and the actual event time or date, when known, must remain distinct. Do not backdate the entry to create a false appearance of contemporaneous documentation.

Audit trail

Electronic health records preserve information about authorship, entry time, edits, and access. Changing visible text does not necessarily erase the system history.

Patient refusal

A complete refusal entry records the therapy offered, current objective assessment, the patient's stated reason, education provided, final decision, required notification, and follow-up plan without blaming or labeling the patient.

Clinical record versus incident report

The clinical record documents patient care and clinically relevant events. A privacy, security, or safety incident-reporting system serves a separate institutional function. Do not automatically copy incident-report details into the patient record unless policy directs it.

Transparency principle: Correcting an honest error strengthens the record. Concealing the original entry or altering chronology creates greater patient-safety and professional risk.
Putting C6 together
Write objective, current facts; preserve the original record; distinguish event time from entry time; and use the approved correction, addendum, late-entry, and incident-reporting processes.
C7: Identify PHI and distinguish the HIPAA Privacy Rule, Security Rule, transaction/code-set requirements, and administrative, physical, and technical safeguards.

HIPAA is more than “do not say the patient's name”

Protected health information

Protected health information, or PHI, is identifiable health information related to health status, treatment, or payment. A patient's name is only one identifier. Medical record numbers, dates, photographs, contact information, device identifiers, account information, locations, unusual case details, and combinations of facts may identify a person.

A medical record number associated with a diagnosis is PHI. Removing the patient's name or face does not automatically make a clinical story or image de-identified.

Privacy Rule

The HIPAA Privacy Rule addresses appropriate use and disclosure of PHI and patient rights, including rights related to access and requesting amendment of the record.

Security Rule

The HIPAA Security Rule focuses on electronic protected health information and requires safeguards for confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

Transaction and code-set standards

HIPAA also includes standards for certain electronic health-care transactions and standardized code sets. These should not be confused with patient privacy rights or electronic-security controls.

Three safeguard categories

SafeguardPrimary functionExamples
AdministrativeOrganizational controlsPolicies, workforce training, access authorization, risk analysis, incident response, contingency planning
PhysicalProtect locations, equipment, and paperLocked record rooms, controlled access, secure printers, monitor positioning, locked storage, approved shredding
TechnicalTechnology-based access and transmission protectionEncryption, unique credentials, authentication, audit logs, automatic screen lock, secure messaging
Quiz connection: Encryption, unique passwords, audit logs, and automatic screen lock are technical safeguards. Workforce training is primarily administrative. Locked paper storage is primarily physical.
Putting C7 together
Know what counts as PHI, what each HIPAA rule primarily governs, and how administrative, physical, and technical safeguards differ.
C8: Protect electronic, verbal, and paper information through appropriate access control, screen security, private communication, secure disposal, and audit-trail awareness.

Privacy becomes real through everyday habits

Access only what you need for your work

Being an employee does not create permission to open any chart. Access must be connected to a legitimate work-related purpose. Curiosity access is unauthorized even if the information is never shared.

Never share credentials

Every action under a user account can be attributed to that account in the audit trail. Shared credentials destroy accountability and may expose records to unauthorized use.

Secure unattended workstations

Lock or log off an unattended workstation. A full chart visible on an unlocked hallway computer is a privacy and security concern even if the therapist intends to return in one minute.

Protect paper and screens from incidental viewing

Retrieve printouts promptly, avoid leaving reports on open carts or printers, position screens away from public sight lines, use locked storage, and place discarded PHI in approved confidential waste.

Protect verbal communication

Use an appropriate location and volume, confirm who is present, and discuss only information required for the legitimate clinical purpose. Elevators, cafeterias, parking areas, and public hallways are poor places for case discussion.

Audit trails are active safety tools

Electronic systems can record which patient record was accessed, by whom, when, and sometimes from where and what activity occurred. Do not assume inappropriate access is invisible.

If a privacy or security risk occurs

  1. Contain it: lock the screen, retrieve the paper, stop a transmission when possible, secure the device, or remove information from public view.
  2. Do not conceal it or investigate beyond your role.
  3. Notify through the approved privacy, security, supervisory, compliance, or risk-management process.
  4. Document objective facts through the correct reporting system and cooperate with follow-up.
Near miss principle: An unsecured chart discovered before known viewing is still a process failure that should be corrected and handled according to policy.
Putting C8 together
Protect PHI from the moment it is viewed or created through storage, communication, disposal, and incident response.
C9: Differentiate permitted treatment communication and minimum-necessary disclosure from curiosity access, public discussion, unauthorized family or employer disclosure, and social-media violations.

HIPAA does not block necessary patient care

Treatment communication is permitted

Clinicians directly involved in the patient's care can share relevant information through appropriate channels for treatment purposes. A respiratory therapist can communicate clinically necessary respiratory findings to the nurse, provider, pharmacist, or another involved clinician without seeking separate patient authorization for every conversation.

Minimum necessary does not mean “say as little as possible”

When the minimum-necessary principle applies, share only the information needed for the legitimate task. But do not withhold critical details required for safe care. A handoff about an adverse bronchodilator response should include enough information to understand the baseline, treatment, response, symptoms, action, and pending reassessment.

Family does not automatically equal authorization

A family member's concern does not automatically create a right to receive detailed health information. Follow patient preference, authorization, applicable law, and facility policy.

Employers do not automatically receive diagnoses

An employer request does not independently authorize disclosure. Workers' compensation or other legal processes follow specific facility workflows and authorization requirements.

Curiosity is not a clinical purpose

Do not access, photograph, discuss, or share a patient's chart because the case is interesting, the patient is famous, the clinician used to care for the patient, or the learner wants material for a presentation.

Social media and informal messaging

Do not post clinical stories, images, unusual details, or “de-identified” cases through personal social media or informal groups unless they have gone through the institution's approved educational or communication process. Removing a name does not guarantee that the patient cannot be recognized.

Decision questions: Who needs the information? Why? Is this person involved in the legitimate task? Is this the approved channel? Am I sharing enough for safe care but no unrelated information?
Putting C9 together
Protect confidentiality without obstructing appropriate treatment communication.
C10: Follow approved mandatory-reporting and required-disclosure processes while continuing to protect information from unnecessary disclosure.

A required disclosure is still a limited disclosure

Some circumstances permit or require information to be reported despite a patient's request for secrecy. The exact legal requirements vary by jurisdiction, role, and event, so the respiratory therapist should use the approved facility process rather than independently interpreting complex reporting law.

Course example: active tuberculosis

Active tuberculosis may require public-health reporting. If the patient asks the therapist not to report it, the therapist should respectfully explain that certain public-health information must be handled through required processes and then follow the designated institutional pathway.

Required reporting does not authorize broad disclosure

The patient's family, employer, unrelated coworkers, visitors, or the public do not automatically receive the diagnosis because a public-health report is required. Share only with the authorized recipient and only the information required by the process.

Other situations may trigger reporting pathways

Depending on jurisdiction and role, suspected abuse, certain injuries, threats, communicable diseases, adverse device or medication events, vital-statistics requirements, court orders, subpoenas, or other legal demands may require a defined response. Route these through the appropriate institutional resources such as supervision, privacy, infection prevention, risk management, social work, health-information management, or legal counsel.

Document facts, not legal conclusions beyond your role

Record the relevant assessment findings or patient statements, time, notification, instructions received, and actions taken according to policy. Do not independently place unnecessary confidential details into unrelated documentation.

Boundary principle: “Must report” does not mean “may tell everyone.” Follow the required pathway and continue protecting the information from unrelated disclosure.
Putting C10 together
Identify the obligation, use the approved recipient and channel, limit the information, and seek institutional guidance when the boundary is unclear.
C11: Use only approved clinical photography, storage, transmission, consent, and documentation workflows; never use personal devices or informal messaging for patient images.

A clinical image is part of an information pathway

No face does not mean no identity

A photograph of skin breakdown, a device, a tattoo, a rare injury, a room label, a date, or surrounding clinical context may identify the patient even when the face is not visible.

Approved clinical photography requires a complete workflow

The facility process should address legitimate purpose, authorization or consent when required, approved equipment, secure capture, chart integration, storage, transmission, access, retention, and use for treatment versus teaching.

Do not use a personal phone

A personal device can create uncontrolled copies, automatic cloud backups, metadata, thumbnails, message caches, or other storage outside the clinical system. Deleting the visible image after upload does not prove that all copies or traces are gone.

Treatment use does not automatically authorize teaching use

An image captured appropriately for patient care does not automatically become available for class, conference, marketing, publication, or social media. Educational use requires the separate approved process.

If an image is captured inadvertently on a personal device

Secure the device and report the event immediately through the facility privacy or security process. Do not forward it, edit it, conceal the event, or independently decide that deletion solves the risk.

Unit 3 case: An RT wants to photograph skin breakdown beneath a noninvasive-ventilation mask. The face would not be shown, but the RT plans to use a personal phone and delete the image after uploading it. The compliant action is to not use the personal phone, use the approved clinical-imaging process, and report any inadvertent personal-device capture through the privacy or security pathway.
Clinical reasoning: Evaluate the entire information pathway—not only the content visible in the image.
Putting C11 together
Use approved equipment and workflow from capture through storage, transmission, charting, retention, and any later teaching use.
C12: Communicate adverse responses, privacy concerns, safety findings, and required reports using concise closed-loop communication and appropriate chain-of-command escalation.

Communication closes the safety loop

Adverse treatment response

A complete handoff should state baseline, therapy delivered, therapeutic response, adverse effect, immediate action, current status, person notified, recommendation or order received, and the reassessment plan.

Provider notification must be visible in the record

“Provider aware” is too vague. Document the time, relevant findings communicated, person notified, recommendation, response or orders received, and follow-up plan.

Privacy or security concern

Contain the immediate risk first, then notify the appropriate supervisor, privacy officer, security team, health-information management, risk management, or other designated resource. Preserve facts and avoid unnecessary disclosure while the event is reviewed.

Chain of command

Use the appropriate escalation pathway when the first contact cannot resolve a patient-safety, privacy, compliance, or reporting concern. Different issues may require different resources: supervising respiratory therapist, provider, clinical instructor, charge nurse, privacy officer, infection prevention, risk management, security, social work, or legal counsel.

Closed-loop communication

The sender states the message or requested action, the receiver confirms it, and the sender verifies that the correct information was understood. This is especially important for time-sensitive adverse reactions, deterioration, and mandatory reporting.

Integrated case: benefit plus adverse response

“Before Albuterol, HR 92/min with diffuse wheezing. Course threshold is 119.6/min. After treatment HR is 124/min with palpitations; wheezing improved to mild end-expiratory wheezing. Treatment stopped/completed as documented, patient reassessed, and provider notified at 10:20. Recommend review of continued bronchodilator plan; repeat HR, symptoms, and respiratory assessment in 15 minutes.”

Integrated case: required public-health report with continued confidentiality

A patient with confirmed active tuberculosis asks that the diagnosis not be reported. A family member and employer call requesting details. The therapist should maintain confidentiality from those unauthorized callers while following the approved mandatory public-health reporting pathway and sharing only the information required.

Communication rule: State what happened, what the evidence shows, what you already did, who needs to act next, and when follow-up must occur.
Putting C12 together
Use closed-loop communication and the correct chain of command to protect both patient safety and information privacy.

Week 3.4: The record should tell the truth, support the next decision, and protect the patient

Complete respiratory documentation shows the patient's baseline, intervention, response, adverse effects, action, notification, and reassessment. Professional compliance protects that information while still allowing the communication and reporting required for safe care.

correct record → current baseline → intervention → measurable response → adverse effects → action/notification → transparent correction if needed → protected communication → required reporting through approved channels
