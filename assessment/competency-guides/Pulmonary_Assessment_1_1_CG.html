Pulmonary Assessment • 1.1 Competency Guide

Introduction to Respiratory Patient Assessment & Interviewing

Your first job in respiratory care is not to memorize treatments. It is to learn how to look at a patient, gather reliable information, recognize what has changed, and decide what needs attention first.

Before you begin

This may be your first course in health care, so this chapter introduces both respiratory assessment and the language used by the health-care team. Medical words and abbreviations are spelled out and explained as they appear. You are not expected to know them before reading.

Big idea for Week 1.1: Do not treat a number, a diagnosis label, or a computer screen. First build an accurate picture of the patient. Ask: What is happening now? What is normal for this patient? Can I trust the data? How urgent is the problem? What should I assess, verify, communicate, or escalate next?

Vocabulary you will begin using this week: assessment, baseline, chief complaint, history of present illness, subjective data, objective data, dyspnea, sputum, oxygen saturation, pulse oximetry, fraction of inspired oxygen, electronic health record, medication administration record, advance directive, do-not-resuscitate order, reassessment, escalation.

C1: Locate and verify clinically relevant respiratory information in the history and physical, orders, medication administration record, laboratory and imaging results, flowsheets, respiratory therapy notes, and advance-directive documentation.

The patient's story is spread across several places

In a hospital, information about a patient is stored in an electronic health record, often shortened later to EHR. Think of the electronic health record as a large digital chart. Different parts of the chart answer different questions. One section may tell you why the patient came to the hospital. Another tells you which treatments were ordered. Another shows what medication was actually given. Another shows the patient's oxygen level over time.

A respiratory therapist must know where to look, but finding a number in the chart is only the beginning. You must also ask whether that information is current, whether it was collected under the same conditions as other values, and whether it matches what you see at the bedside.

History and physical examination report

The history and physical examination is often shortened to H&P. It is usually completed by a provider near the time of admission. This report commonly includes the reason for admission, the patient's chief complaint, the history of the current problem, important past illnesses and surgeries, medications used at home, allergies, smoking or exposure history, and the provider's admission assessment.

Beginner connection: If you need to know, “Why is this patient here, and what is the important medical background?” the history and physical examination report is a strong place to start.

Orders versus what was actually done

Orders tell the health-care team what has been prescribed. An order might say to give a breathing treatment every four hours, provide oxygen within a certain range, obtain a blood test, or monitor a patient in a particular way. An order does not prove that the treatment or medication was actually given.

The medication administration record, later shortened to MAR, documents medications that were actually administered. It commonly includes the medication, dose, route, date, time, and administration status. This difference is extremely important. An ordered medication can be delayed, refused, held, or discontinued.

Advance directives and do-not-resuscitate orders

An advance directive is documentation of a patient's wishes about medical care if the patient becomes unable to communicate those wishes. A do-not-resuscitate order, commonly abbreviated later as DNR, addresses resuscitation according to the active order and local policy.

A do-not-resuscitate order does not automatically mean “do not treat.” A patient may still receive respiratory assessment, oxygen, medication, airway-clearance therapy, symptom relief, and other routine or comfort-focused care unless the active plan says otherwise. A wristband or verbal report should alert you to verify the current order; it should not replace verification.

Clinical reasoning: The chart is important, but the chart is not the patient. Documentation may be delayed, incomplete, or outdated. If the record says one thing and the bedside says another, stop and verify.

Clinical example

The history and physical examination says a patient was admitted for worsening shortness of breath. The orders include albuterol every four hours. You need to know whether the last dose was actually given. The order tells you what was prescribed, but the medication administration record tells you whether the medication was administered and when. A respiratory therapy note may then explain the patient's breath sounds and response after the treatment.

Putting C1 together
Be able to choose the correct chart source, distinguish an order from completed care, verify current advance-directive information, and recognize that missing information means unknown—verify, not “probably normal.”
C2: Differentiate chief complaint, history of present illness, baseline respiratory status, subjective patient reports, and objective measured or observed findings.

Organizing the patient's information

When a patient says, “My breathing is worse,” that statement matters—but it is not yet a complete respiratory assessment. You need to know what “worse” means, when the change began, what the patient's breathing is usually like, and what you can measure or observe now.

Chief complaint

The chief complaint is the main reason the patient is seeking care. It is often best expressed in the patient's own words, such as “I can't catch my breath” or “I have been coughing for three days.” The chief complaint is not the same thing as a final diagnosis.

History of present illness

The history of present illness, commonly shortened later to HPI, tells the story of the current problem. It includes when the symptom started, how long it has been present, whether it is improving or worsening, how severe it is, what makes it better or worse, other symptoms that occur with it, and how it affects normal activity.

For shortness of breath, useful questions include: When did this begin? Was the onset sudden or gradual? What were you doing when it started? Is it worse when walking or lying flat? Does anything make it better? Is this different from your usual breathing?

Baseline respiratory status

Baseline means the patient's usual condition before the current change. A patient's baseline may include usual activity tolerance, home oxygen use, usual cough or sputum, normal sleep needs, and the amount of shortness of breath normally experienced.

This is why a value that looks “abnormal” in a textbook may not represent a new emergency for every patient. The question is not only, “Is this number outside the usual reference range?” It is also, “Is this different from this patient's normal condition?”

Subjective and objective data

Subjective data are symptoms, experiences, or history reported by the patient or caregiver. Examples include “My chest feels tight,” “I get short of breath climbing stairs,” or “I use oxygen at night.” Subjective does not mean unimportant. Symptoms may be the first warning that a patient is getting worse.

Objective data are measured or directly observed findings. Examples include a respiratory rate counted by the clinician, oxygen saturation displayed by a pulse oximeter, accessory-muscle use observed by the therapist, a blood-gas result, or a verified imaging report.

Oxygen saturation needs context

A pulse oximeter is the sensor commonly placed on a finger to estimate the percentage of hemoglobin carrying oxygen. This value is called peripheral oxygen saturation and is written as SpO2. You will use the abbreviation often later, but for now remember what it means.

An oxygen saturation value by itself is incomplete. You also need to know whether the patient is breathing room air or receiving supplemental oxygen. If oxygen is being used, document the device and its setting. A nasal cannula may be documented by flow in liters per minute. Other devices may be described by the fraction of inspired oxygen, written as FiO2, which means the fraction or concentration of oxygen in the gas being inhaled.

Board-style thinking: “SpO2 89%” is not a complete patient picture. Ask: On room air or oxygen? Which device? What setting? At rest or after activity? What is the patient's baseline? How does the patient look?

Clinical example

A patient says, “I normally walk to the mailbox without stopping, but today I get short of breath just crossing the room.” That is subjective information, but it reveals a meaningful decline from baseline function. The respiratory therapist should now collect objective information such as respiratory rate, work of breathing, oxygen saturation, oxygen device and setting, mental status, and breath sounds.

Putting C2 together
Be able to separate the patient's main concern, the story of the current illness, the usual baseline, reported symptoms, and measured findings. Then combine them rather than letting one data point stand alone.
C3: Use focused, respectful, patient-centered interview questions to clarify dyspnea, cough, sputum, associated symptoms, home therapies, exposure history, functional status, and learning needs.

A good interview is part of the respiratory assessment

Respiratory therapists use equipment, but they also use conversation as an assessment tool. A patient can tell you when symptoms began, what makes them worse, what normal life looks like, which treatments are used at home, and what exposures may be affecting the lungs.

Begin with an open question such as, “Tell me what has changed with your breathing.” Then use focused questions to fill in the missing details. Avoid questions that assume the diagnosis or tell the patient what answer you expect.

Dyspnea: the medical word for shortness of breath

Dyspnea means the uncomfortable feeling of difficult or labored breathing. It is a symptom, so the patient describes it. Ask when it began, whether it was sudden or gradual, what makes it worse, what relieves it, whether it occurs at rest or with activity, and how it compares with normal breathing.

A high-value follow-up question does more than collect extra details. It helps determine whether the problem is new, how much function has changed, and what needs to be assessed next. For example: “When did the shortness of breath start, what were you doing when it began, and is this different from your usual breathing?”

Cough and sputum

Ask whether the cough is new or chronic, dry or productive, and whether it changes with time of day, activity, position, food, or other triggers. Sputum is mucus or other material coughed up from the lower respiratory tract. If sputum is present, ask whether the amount, color, thickness, or odor has changed from the patient's usual pattern.

Do not assume that a particular sputum color proves a specific infection. At this stage, your job is to collect an accurate description and recognize a change that may need further evaluation.

Associated symptoms

Breathing symptoms may occur with chest discomfort, wheezing, fever, chills, fatigue, swelling, dizziness, faintness, or difficulty lying flat. These associated symptoms help the team understand the pattern and urgency of the problem.

Home therapies and medications

Ask about home oxygen, inhalers, nebulizers, positive-airway-pressure devices used during sleep, and any other respiratory equipment. Ask not only what was prescribed but what the patient actually uses. A patient may have an inhaler listed in the chart but rarely take it, or may use oxygen at a different setting than an old record shows.

Smoking, vaping, occupational, and environmental exposures

Smoking and vaping questions should be neutral and specific. The purpose is to understand exposure, not to judge the patient. Occupational and environmental exposures also matter. Dust, fumes, chemicals, smoke, mold, animals, combustion products, and other exposures can help explain respiratory symptoms or identify safety concerns.

Neutral wording is more likely to produce accurate information. Instead of “You don't smoke, do you?” ask, “Do you currently smoke, vape, or use inhaled products? Have you used them in the past?”

Functional status

Functional status describes what the patient can actually do. Ask about walking, stairs, bathing, dressing, sleeping, work, or other normal activities. A change in function may reveal worsening respiratory status even when the patient uses vague words such as “I feel off.”

Language access and health literacy

Health literacy refers to a person's ability to find, understand, and use health information. Do not assume that a patient understands medical terms just because the patient is educated in another area. Use plain language and check understanding.

When language assistance is needed, use a qualified interpreter according to facility policy. Speak to the patient—not to the interpreter—and allow time for interpretation. Family members can provide support, but complex medical communication should use qualified language assistance when available and required by policy.

Teach-back is a method for checking understanding. Instead of asking, “Do you understand?” ask the patient to explain the information in their own words. For example: “Just so I know I explained it clearly, tell me how you will use this device when you get home.”

Clinical reasoning: A focused interview is not a long checklist. Ask the question that is most likely to clarify the patient's current change, baseline, oxygen use, and level of function.

Clinical example

A patient says, “My breathing has been worse.” Asking, “Would you like more oxygen?” jumps ahead to treatment. A better approach is: “When did the breathing change? What makes it worse? What can you normally do without becoming short of breath, and what can you do today?” Those questions help define the problem before treatment is selected.

Putting C3 together
Be ready to ask respectful questions about dyspnea, cough, sputum, home care, smoking or vaping, work and environmental exposures, daily function, and learning needs. Choose questions that clarify the problem without assuming the answer.
C4: Perform a rapid first-look assessment and recognize findings that indicate stability, a meaningful change, or immediate respiratory instability.

Look at the patient before the monitor

A first-look assessment is the rapid impression you form when you first see the patient. Before you become focused on the computer or monitor, ask whether the patient appears able to move air, breathe effectively, maintain adequate circulation and color, and interact appropriately.

You are not trying to complete the entire physical examination in fifteen seconds. You are trying to answer one immediate question: Does this patient look stable enough for a routine assessment, or is something happening that requires urgent help?

Appearance and posture

Notice whether the patient looks comfortable, anxious, sweaty, pale, blue or gray, exhausted, or acutely ill. A patient sitting upright and leaning forward with the arms supported may be using posture to help breathe. This is sometimes called the tripod position.

Speech tells you about respiratory reserve

Speaking requires controlled exhalation. A patient who can speak comfortably in full sentences usually has more respiratory reserve than a patient who can only say a few words before stopping to breathe. One- or two-word speech, a weak voice, or inability to speak can signal severe distress.

Work of breathing

Work of breathing describes how much effort the patient is using to breathe. Look for rapid breathing, visible contraction of neck or shoulder muscles, inward pulling of the chest wall between the ribs, nasal flaring, prolonged exhalation, shallow breathing, or visible fatigue.

Accessory muscles are muscles that are not usually needed for quiet breathing but may be recruited when breathing becomes difficult. Visible use of these muscles is an important objective sign of increased respiratory effort.

Color and mental status

Cyanosis means a bluish or gray discoloration that may occur when oxygenation is severely impaired, although it can be difficult to see and may appear late. Do not wait for cyanosis before deciding that a patient is in serious distress.

Mental status refers to alertness, orientation, behavior, and ability to interact. Agitation or confusion may be an early warning sign. Drowsiness, decreasing responsiveness, or inability to cooperate can signal serious deterioration, fatigue, medication effects, or another acute problem.

Safety priority: Do not complete a long interview while a patient is exhausted, blue or gray, unable to speak normally, becoming less responsive, or rapidly worsening. Recognize instability and get help.

Clinical example

A nurse tells you that a patient in a nearby room is suddenly struggling to breathe and becoming less responsive. The patient is not assigned to you. Patient safety comes first. Go to the bedside, perform a rapid assessment, summon appropriate help according to policy, remain with the patient, and then clarify who will assume ongoing responsibility.

Putting C4 together
Be able to look at the patient and classify the situation as stable, concerning, or urgent. Explain which exact observation supports your judgment.
C5: Identify missing, contradictory, outdated, or unreliable respiratory data and verify the patient, device, setting, timing, and current condition before interpreting results or trends.

Good clinical decisions require trustworthy data

Health-care data are not automatically correct just because they appear on a screen. A sensor can be loose. The chart may still show yesterday's oxygen setting. A patient may have been walking when one oxygen saturation was measured and resting during the next. A value can be real but still be misleading if the conditions are different.

Missing data

A blank field is not the same as a normal result. If the oxygen-device field is empty, you do not know that the patient was on room air. The correct response is unknown—verify.

Contradictory data

Suppose the flowsheet says the patient is receiving oxygen at 2 liters per minute through a nasal cannula, but you enter the room and find a different device running at a higher setting. Do not choose the value you like better or average the two. Assess the patient, inspect the equipment, determine the current support, check time stamps and recent notes, and identify when the change occurred.

Outdated data

A result may have been accurate when it was recorded but no longer represent the patient's current condition. This is why time matters. Ask: Was this value obtained before or after a treatment? Before or after activity? Before or after oxygen was increased? Before or after the patient became more confused?

Unreliable measurements

Pulse oximetry can be affected by poor sensor contact, movement, poor circulation, or other technical and physiologic factors. If the displayed value does not fit the patient's appearance or changes suddenly without explanation, verify the signal and reassess rather than immediately accepting the number.

A trend only exists when the conditions can be compared

A trend is a meaningful pattern of change over time. But values can only be compared when you understand the conditions under which they were obtained.

Imagine that the patient's oxygen saturation was 91% on room air two hours ago and is now 93% while receiving much more supplemental oxygen. Looking only at the percentages might make the second value look “better.” In reality, the patient now requires more support to maintain that saturation. The rising oxygen requirement is clinically important information.

Before you call two values a trend, compare:
  • the correct patient and measurement;
  • the time each value was obtained;
  • the oxygen device and setting;
  • rest versus activity;
  • before versus after treatment or repositioning;
  • the patient's symptoms, work of breathing, and mental status.

A practical verification sequence

  1. Look at the patient. Does the value fit the clinical appearance?
  2. Verify the patient. Make sure the data belong to the correct person.
  3. Inspect the device. Identify the oxygen or respiratory-support device actually in use.
  4. Verify the setting. Record flow or oxygen concentration as appropriate.
  5. Repeat questionable measurements. Correct obvious sensor or technique problems.
  6. Check timing. Review time stamps and recent changes.
  7. Reconcile the record. Determine why the chart and bedside differ and communicate the discrepancy when needed.
Competency-exam thinking: The safest answer is often not “pick the newest number.” It is “assess the patient, verify the device and setting, check timing, and reconcile conflicting information before interpreting the trend.”
Putting C5 together
Do not guess, average conflicting values, or assume missing information. Verify the patient, device, setting, timing, measurement quality, and current condition before deciding what the numbers mean.
C6: Communicate respiratory findings and concerns clearly, prioritize patient safety over workflow ambiguity, and escalate promptly when the patient appears unstable.

Assessment is only useful if the right people know what you found

Respiratory therapists work as part of a health-care team. A concerning assessment may need to be communicated to a nurse, provider, rapid-response team, or emergency/code team depending on the severity of the situation and institutional policy.

Escalation means increasing the level or urgency of response because the patient's condition requires more help. Escalation is not overreacting when objective findings show instability. It is a patient-safety responsibility.

What makes a respiratory report useful?

A useful report is short enough to be understood quickly but specific enough for the receiver to know why you are concerned. Include the patient's current condition, important respiratory findings, oxygen device and setting, oxygen saturation, work of breathing, mental status, relevant change from baseline, and the urgency of the situation.

A concise respiratory safety report might sound like this:

“The patient has become increasingly drowsy and is now speaking only one or two words at a time. Respiratory effort is severe. Oxygen saturation is 84% on the current oxygen device at the documented setting. This is a major change from earlier. I need immediate assistance at the bedside.”

Do not wait for perfect information

If the patient is obviously unstable, you do not need every laboratory result before asking for help. Communicate the urgent findings you have while additional information is gathered. Waiting to make the report “complete” can delay care.

Closed-loop communication

Closed-loop communication means that the message is sent, received, confirmed, and linked to a clear next action. For example, the therapist reports the concern, the receiver repeats the critical information or acknowledges it, and the team confirms who is doing what next.

Reassessment continues after help is called

Reassessment means checking the patient again after time has passed or after an intervention or change in condition. Calling for help does not end your responsibility to observe. The patient's respiratory rate, effort, oxygenation, mental status, or responsiveness may continue to change while the response team is arriving.

Workflow never outranks immediate safety

Hospitals use assignments, roles, and chains of communication to organize care. Those systems matter, but they should not delay the immediate assessment of a patient who appears to be in serious respiratory distress. Address the emergency first, then clarify ongoing responsibility.

Urgent pattern to recognize: exhaustion + blue or gray color + inability to speak normally + altered mental status + severe work of breathing = immediate respiratory instability until proven otherwise.

Clinical example

You find a patient exhausted, blue around the lips, able to say only one or two words at a time, and less responsive than earlier. Report the current oxygen device and setting, oxygen saturation, respiratory pattern, work of breathing, and mental-status change. Call for immediate assistance according to policy, stay with the patient, and continue reassessment while help arrives.

Putting C6 together
Communicate what you see, what you measure, what support the patient is receiving, how the condition is changing, and how urgently help is needed. Patient safety comes before assignment confusion or perfect documentation.

Week 1.1: Bring the whole picture together

A respiratory therapist rarely makes a safe decision from one piece of information. This week teaches you to combine the chart, patient interview, bedside appearance, basic measurements, oxygen support, baseline status, and the reliability of the data.

As you move into the PulmoLearn lesson, keep returning to this sequence: look at the patient → gather the right information → compare with baseline → verify questionable data → decide how urgent the situation is → communicate → reassess.

Before the weekly quiz, you should be able to...

  • identify the history and physical examination as the likely source of the provider's admission history and assessment;
  • distinguish prescribed orders from the medication administration record showing what was actually given;
  • distinguish subjective patient reports from objective measured or observed findings;
  • explain what belongs in the history of present illness;
  • choose a focused, nonjudgmental question that clarifies worsening dyspnea and functional change;
  • use plain language, qualified language assistance, and teach-back when needed;
  • explain why smoking, vaping, occupational, and environmental exposures matter;
  • verify the active advance-directive or do-not-resuscitate order rather than assuming a wristband tells the whole story;
  • recognize exhaustion, blue or gray color, markedly limited speech, severe work of breathing, and altered mental status as urgent findings;
  • document the oxygen device and its flow rate or fraction of inspired oxygen with oxygen-saturation data;
  • verify current bedside support when the chart and actual equipment do not match; and
  • treat missing device information as unknown rather than assuming room air.

For the Unit 1 Competency Exam, take the next step

The unit exam will not only ask whether you remember a definition. You will need to use the information in a patient case.

  • Choose the highest-yield question to clarify onset, triggers, baseline breathing, usual function, and home oxygen use.
  • Recognize when immediate bedside assessment and escalation are more important than assignment boundaries.
  • Decide whether oxygen-saturation values can truly be compared by checking oxygen support and timing.
  • Recognize that a rising oxygen requirement can represent deterioration even if the saturation number looks similar or slightly better.
  • Resolve conflicting or outdated data by assessing the patient and verifying the device, setting, time, and record.
  • Distinguish a stable chronic baseline from a meaningful new change and from immediate respiratory instability.
  • Communicate objective findings, current support, direction of change, and urgency clearly.
