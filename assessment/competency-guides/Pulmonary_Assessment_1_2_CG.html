Pulmonary Assessment • 1.2 Competency Guide

Physical Assessment & Disease Differentiation

Learn how respiratory therapists use inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation to build a reliable bedside picture—then combine findings to recognize common respiratory patterns and urgent abnormalities.

Before you begin

This lesson introduces many physical-assessment words that may be completely new to you. That is expected. The goal is not to memorize a long list of sounds and disease names all at once. Instead, learn what each assessment technique tells you, what normal feels or sounds like, and how several findings fit together.

Big idea for Week 1.2: One abnormal sound does not diagnose a disease. Respiratory therapists look for a pattern. Ask: What do I see? What do I feel? What does percussion suggest about the amount of air or density underneath? What do I hear? Are the right and left sides different? Do all of these findings tell the same story?

Vocabulary you will begin using this week: inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation, symmetry, hemithorax, trachea, tactile fremitus, crepitus, resonance, dullness, hyperresonance, wheeze, rhonchi, crackles, pleural friction rub, stridor, consolidation, atelectasis, pleural effusion, pneumothorax, emphysema, pulmonary edema.

Four ways to examine the chest

A respiratory physical examination uses four main methods: inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation. These words sound technical at first, but each describes a simple way of gathering information.

Method Plain-language meaning What it helps you assess
InspectionLooking carefullyGeneral appearance, posture, respiratory pattern, chest movement, work of breathing, color, speech, mental status
PalpationUsing the hands to feelChest expansion, vibration, tenderness, crepitus, tracheal position
PercussionTapping the chest and listening to the toneWhether the tissue beneath the chest wall is normally air filled, unusually dense, fluid filled, or contains excess air
AuscultationListening with a stethoscopeAir movement, breath-sound quality, added sounds, and abnormal voice transmission

The routine sequence

The usual respiratory examination sequence is inspection → palpation → percussion → auscultation. Inspection comes first because you may recognize respiratory distress before you ever touch the patient. If the patient is severely unstable, the routine examination can be shortened while help is obtained.

Compare the right and left sides

The chest has two sides. Each half is called a hemithorax. When possible, compare the same level on the right and left before moving downward. For example, listen to the upper right chest and then the same location on the upper left chest. Side-to-side comparison makes subtle differences easier to detect.

Do not examine only the front of the chest if the patient's condition allows a more complete examination. The lungs extend through the front, sides, and back of the chest, and disease may be localized to one area.

Describe before you diagnose

A beginning clinician may be tempted to hear an abnormal sound and immediately name a disease. Resist that urge. First describe exactly what you found, where you found it, and whether it was on one side or both sides.

Better documentation: “Breath sounds markedly diminished at the right base.”
Less useful: “Bad lungs.”

Physical examination supports a pattern. It does not usually prove a medical diagnosis by itself. A professional description might say that the findings are consistent with a particular process or raise concern for a condition that needs further assessment.

Clinical reasoning: A systematic sequence reduces missed findings. Side-to-side comparison helps you recognize asymmetry. Description comes before interpretation.

Clinical example

A patient becomes suddenly short of breath after a bedside procedure. You first inspect the patient and notice distress and less movement of the left chest. You then compare the right and left sides using palpation, percussion, and auscultation. The findings form a focal pattern that can be communicated quickly and may require urgent action.

Putting C1 together
Know the sequence inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation and what information each step contributes. Compare matched locations rather than examining one entire side before the other.

What you can see and feel

Inspection begins before you touch the patient

Look at general appearance, posture, ability to speak, respiratory rate, depth, rhythm, chest movement, skin color, and mental status. In Week 1.1 you learned that a patient who becomes quiet and drowsy may be worsening rather than improving. That same principle continues here.

Accessory-muscle use is seen primarily during inspection. Accessory muscles are muscles in the neck, shoulders, or abdomen that are recruited when normal breathing muscles need extra help. Visible accessory-muscle use suggests increased work of breathing.

Respiratory pattern

Describe what you actually observe: fast or slow breathing, deep or shallow breaths, regular or irregular pattern, prolonged exhalation, pauses, visible fatigue, or inward movement of part of the chest during inspiration. Avoid using a disease label when a precise description is possible.

Chest symmetry and expansion

Symmetry means that both sides move in a similar way. During quiet breathing, the right and left sides of the chest should generally expand together. Reduced motion on one side does not identify one specific disease. It tells you that a focal problem may be present and that the affected area deserves closer palpation, percussion, and auscultation.

During palpation, place your hands symmetrically and compare how far each side moves with inspiration. Reduced expansion may occur with pain, pleural fluid, pleural air, lung collapse, obstruction, or previous surgery.

Tracheal position

The trachea, or windpipe, normally lies near the midline of the neck. A new shift can be an important clue. A pressure process in one side of the chest may push the trachea and central structures away from the affected side. Major loss of lung volume may pull structures toward the affected side.

Tracheal deviation is never interpreted by itself. It becomes especially concerning when it occurs with sudden respiratory distress and a major difference in breath sounds between the two sides.

Tactile fremitus

Tactile fremitus is vibration from the patient's voice that can be felt through the chest wall. “Tactile” means related to touch. “Fremitus” means vibration.

To assess it, place the ulnar edge or broad surface of your hands on matching areas of the chest while the patient repeats the same phrase in the same voice. Compare the vibration from side to side.

Increased fremitus can occur when lung tissue becomes denser but remains connected to an open airway, as in consolidation. Dense lung can transmit voice vibration to the chest wall more strongly than normal air-filled lung.

Decreased fremitus can occur when air or fluid in the pleural space separates the lung from the chest wall. That layer interferes with transmission of vibration.

Think of vibration transmission:
  • Dense consolidated lung: vibration may transmit more strongly.
  • Pleural air or pleural fluid: vibration is often reduced.

Crepitus

Crepitus in this respiratory-assessment context is a crackling feeling under the skin caused by air in the subcutaneous tissue. It may feel like tiny bubbles or crisp cereal under the fingertips. New or expanding crepitus should be assessed and communicated because it can indicate air leaking into soft tissue.

Do not memorize fremitus in isolation: Increased or decreased vibration becomes useful when paired with percussion, breath sounds, chest expansion, symptoms, and laterality.

Clinical example

A patient has reduced movement of the right lower chest. Palpation shows decreased expansion and decreased tactile fremitus at the right base. These findings do not yet prove the cause, but they tell you the problem is focal and that percussion and auscultation of the same region can help distinguish pleural fluid, pleural air, collapse, or another process.

Percussion tells you about density and air

Percussion means tapping the chest wall in a controlled way and interpreting the tone produced. The tone changes depending on how much air, fluid, or dense tissue lies beneath the area being tapped.

Percussion tone General meaning Examples
ResonantExpected over normally air-filled lungNormal lung tissue
DullMore density, fluid, or less air beneath the chest wallConsolidation, pleural effusion, atelectatic volume loss
HyperresonantMore air than expectedHyperinflation, air trapping, pneumothorax

Dullness does not tell you whether the increased density is fluid, consolidated lung, or collapsed lung. Hyperresonance does not by itself tell you whether the patient has emphysema or a pneumothorax. You need the rest of the examination.

Auscultation: listening to air movement

Auscultation means listening to body sounds with a stethoscope. In respiratory assessment, first decide whether air movement is normal, decreased, or absent. Then identify the quality of the normal breath sound and any added sound.

Normal breath sounds

Normal peripheral lung fields usually have softer breath sounds because sound from the central airways is filtered through air-filled lung tissue. Bronchial breath sounds are normally louder and more tubular over the large central airway. Hearing bronchial-quality sounds in a peripheral lung region can be abnormal and may indicate that dense lung tissue is transmitting central airway sound outward more effectively.

Wheezes

A wheeze is a musical sound caused by turbulent airflow through narrowed airways. A high-pitched sibilant wheeze is often most noticeable during exhalation. The sound tells you that airflow is moving through narrowed passages. It does not, by itself, name the disease causing the narrowing.

Rhonchi

Rhonchi are lower-pitched, coarse, continuous sounds often associated with secretions or obstruction in larger airways. They may change after coughing or airway clearance. If a sound changes after the patient coughs, that information is useful.

Crackles

Crackles are brief, discontinuous popping sounds. Fine crackles are usually higher pitched and brief. They can occur when small airways or alveoli reopen or when fluid is present in or around small air spaces. Crackles must be interpreted with location, timing, oxygenation, symptoms, and other findings.

Pleural friction rub

The pleura are thin membranes around the lungs and inside the chest wall. Normally they slide smoothly. A pleural friction rub is a rough, grating sound that can occur when inflamed pleural surfaces rub against one another. It may be associated with sharp pain that worsens with breathing.

Stridor is an upper-airway red flag

Stridor is a harsh, high-pitched sound that usually comes from significant narrowing of the upper airway. It is often loudest over the neck. Because upper-airway obstruction can rapidly become life threatening, stridor requires prompt assessment of airway stability and patient condition.

Safety point: A harsh upper-airway sound with increasing distress is not just another breath sound to document. Assess airway stability and escalate promptly.

Abnormal voice transmission

Voice sounds can also be evaluated through the stethoscope. Normally, spoken words become muffled as sound passes through air-filled lung. When lung tissue becomes dense, voice sounds may transmit more clearly. Terms you may encounter later include bronchophony, egophony, and whispered pectoriloquy. For now, remember the concept: unusually clear or altered voice transmission over a peripheral area can support a dense-lung pattern such as consolidation.

Quiz connection: Know that dull percussion suggests increased density, fluid, or collapse; hyperresonance suggests excess air; a sibilant wheeze comes from narrowed airways; fine crackles are brief high-pitched discontinuous sounds; and stridor is the strongest upper-airway red flag in this lesson.

Patterns are more useful than isolated findings

Now you can begin combining the examination findings. The goal is not to say, “Dullness equals one disease.” Instead, ask whether the pattern shows too much air, pleural air, pleural fluid, dense lung tissue, loss of lung volume, narrowed airways, or fluid in the lungs.

Pneumothorax: air in the pleural space

A pneumothorax occurs when air enters the pleural space between the lung and chest wall. That air separates the lung from the chest wall and may allow part of the lung to collapse.

  • Reduced movement on the affected side
  • Hyperresonant percussion because excess air is present
  • Decreased tactile fremitus because pleural air reduces vibration transmission
  • Markedly diminished or absent breath sounds over the affected area

When these findings occur suddenly with severe distress, falling oxygen saturation, low blood pressure, and tracheal deviation away from the affected side, the pattern raises concern for a tension pneumothorax, a life-threatening pressure emergency.

Pleural effusion: fluid in the pleural space

A pleural effusion is fluid collecting between the lung and chest wall. Fluid is denser than air, so percussion becomes dull. The fluid also separates the lung from the chest wall, which reduces vibration and breath-sound transmission.

  • Reduced expansion over the affected region
  • Dull percussion
  • Decreased tactile fremitus
  • Decreased or absent breath sounds over the fluid

Consolidation: air spaces become dense

Consolidation means normally air-filled lung becomes filled with material such as inflammatory fluid. The lung becomes denser, but the airway leading to that region may remain open.

  • Dull percussion because the lung is denser
  • Increased tactile fremitus because dense lung transmits vibration more strongly
  • Bronchial-quality breath sounds heard in an abnormal peripheral location
  • Abnormally increased or altered voice transmission may be present
One of the most important comparisons in Week 1.2:

Pleural effusion: dull + decreased fremitus + decreased breath sounds.
Consolidation: dull + increased fremitus + stronger bronchial/voice transmission.

Atelectasis: loss of lung volume

Atelectasis means part of the lung is not fully expanded. Findings vary with the size and cause, but a focal area may have reduced chest movement, decreased breath sounds, and dullness from loss of air. Significant volume loss can pull the trachea or mediastinal structures toward the affected side.

Airway obstruction

Airway obstruction means airflow is limited by narrowing, secretions, swelling, or another blockage. Inspection may show prolonged exhalation and increased work of breathing. Auscultation may reveal wheezes or rhonchi. Severe obstruction can produce very little air movement, so a patient with a “quiet chest” can actually be more dangerous than a patient with loud wheezing.

Emphysema and hyperinflation

Emphysema can produce chronic hyperinflation, meaning the lungs contain more trapped air than normal. Findings may be more diffuse and bilateral rather than suddenly one-sided. Percussion can be hyperresonant, breath sounds may be diminished, and expiration may be prolonged.

Pulmonary edema

Pulmonary edema means fluid has accumulated within the lung tissue and air spaces. Patients may have shortness of breath, increased work of breathing, low oxygen saturation, and diffuse crackles. The pattern is often bilateral rather than sharply limited to one small area.

PneumothoraxHyperresonantDecreasedMarkedly diminished or absent, usually unilateral

Pattern-thinking rule: Ask whether the findings represent excess air, pleural air, pleural fluid, dense lung, volume loss, airway narrowing, or diffuse alveolar/interstitial fluid. Then verify whether all four assessment methods support the same pattern.

What if the findings do not make sense?

Real physical examinations are not always perfect. Clothing, obesity, muscle, breast tissue, pain, shallow breathing, poor positioning, background noise, incorrect stethoscope placement, or inconsistent percussion technique can change what you find. A good clinician does not force conflicting findings into a disease label.

Internal consistency

A set of findings is internally consistent when the pieces fit the same physiologic pattern. For example, unilateral hyperresonance, decreased fremitus, and markedly diminished breath sounds all point toward excess pleural air.

Now imagine a chart says the same area is hyperresonant and has increased tactile fremitus and bronchial breath sounds. Hyperresonance suggests excess air, while increased fremitus and bronchial sounds suggest dense consolidated lung. Those findings conflict. If the patient is unchanged and stable, the correct response is to repeat and verify the examination rather than inventing two diseases to explain poor-quality data.

Verify your technique

  1. Make sure the chest is adequately exposed while preserving privacy.
  2. Reposition the patient when appropriate.
  3. Confirm the correct anatomical landmark.
  4. Use the same technique and pressure on both sides.
  5. Percuss over intercostal spaces rather than directly over bone.
  6. Check that the stethoscope is functioning and the earpieces are oriented correctly.
  7. Ask the patient to breathe consistently.
  8. Repeat the finding and compare the opposite side.
  9. Compare with prior documentation, symptoms, oxygenation, and current condition.

Choose the next assessment that tests your suspected pattern

If a stable patient has focal dullness and diminished breath sounds at one base, the most useful next bedside assessment is not an unrelated neurologic test. Compare tactile fremitus and chest expansion in that region because those findings help distinguish pleural fluid from dense lung tissue and other causes.

Serial findings

Serial means repeated over time. A physical examination is only a snapshot. Compare findings after treatment, repositioning, a procedure, or a change in symptoms. A new unilateral decrease in breath sounds is much more significant when both sides were previously equal.

Competency-exam thinking: If the findings conflict, do not choose only the clue that supports your favorite diagnosis. Repeat and compare the examination systematically.
Putting C5 together
Recognize poor-quality or conflicting data, repeat the specific part of the examination that needs verification, and choose follow-up assessments that directly test the suspected pattern.
C6: Recognize high-risk physical-assessment patterns and prioritize immediate reassessment, oxygenation support, urgent communication, or emergency response rather than routine documentation.

Some physical findings change the priority immediately

Most physical-assessment findings allow time to gather more information. Some patterns do not. If a patient has severe respiratory distress, falling oxygen saturation, altered mental status, low blood pressure, stridor, or sudden unilateral loss of breath sounds, the examination becomes focused on immediate safety.

Tension pneumothorax: a pressure emergency

A tension pneumothorax is more dangerous than a simple pneumothorax because pleural pressure continues to increase. The pressure can compress the affected lung, shift central structures, reduce blood return to the heart, and cause shock.

The high-risk pattern emphasized in this course is:

  • sudden severe respiratory distress;
  • markedly diminished or absent breath sounds on one side;
  • hyperresonance on the affected side;
  • decreased tactile fremitus;
  • tracheal deviation away from the pressure side when advanced;
  • falling peripheral oxygen saturation;
  • hypotension, meaning abnormally low blood pressure.
Emergency priority: This pattern requires immediate escalation, oxygenation support, monitoring, and emergency response according to facility policy. Routine documentation and nonurgent treatment do not come first.

Stridor and upper-airway instability

Stridor may indicate significant upper-airway narrowing. If stridor is accompanied by increasing work of breathing, reduced air movement, inability to speak normally, altered mental status, or worsening oxygenation, treat the situation as an airway emergency until proven otherwise.

A quieter patient may be getting worse

Severe airflow obstruction can produce less wheezing when very little air is moving. Likewise, a patient who was breathing rapidly and using accessory muscles may become quieter because of fatigue. Decreasing respiratory effort with worsening mental status or poor air movement is not reassuring.

Communicate a pattern, not a vague concern

A useful urgent report includes side and location, breath-sound intensity, percussion tone, fremitus when relevant, oxygen support, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, work of breathing, mental status, and direction of change.

Example: “The patient developed sudden severe dyspnea after the procedure. Breath sounds are absent on the left, percussion is hyperresonant, left fremitus is decreased, oxygen saturation is falling, and blood pressure is now low. This is an acute high-risk unilateral pattern requiring immediate evaluation.”

Clinical example

After a procedure, a patient suddenly becomes very short of breath and hypotensive. The left chest moves less than the right. Breath sounds are absent on the left, percussion is hyperresonant, tactile fremitus is decreased, and the trachea appears shifted to the right. This is not a routine documentation problem or a simple “wheezing” case. The cluster indicates a life-threatening pressure pattern and demands emergency escalation.

Putting C6 together
Recognize the difference between a stable pattern that needs more assessment and an unstable pattern that requires immediate support and escalation.

Week 1.2: Build the physical-assessment story

Your hands, eyes, ears, and stethoscope give you several different views of the same patient. Strong respiratory assessment comes from combining them rather than letting one finding control the interpretation.

Use this sequence as you enter the PulmoLearn lesson: inspect → palpate → percuss → auscultate → compare right with left → decide whether the findings agree → determine urgency → communicate or reassess.

Before the weekly quiz, you should be able to...

  • state the routine sequence: inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation;
  • identify accessory-muscle use as a finding obtained mainly through inspection;
  • explain why consolidation can increase tactile fremitus;
  • explain that dull percussion suggests fluid, dense tissue, or collapse;
  • explain that hyperresonance suggests too much or trapped air;
  • connect a high-pitched sibilant wheeze with airflow through narrowed airways;
  • describe fine crackles as brief, high-pitched, discontinuous sounds;
  • recognize stridor as an important upper-airway warning sign;
  • interpret tracheal shift away from unilateral absent breath sounds and hyperresonance as a pressure process;
  • recognize the pneumothorax cluster: unilateral hyperresonance + decreased fremitus + markedly diminished or absent breath sounds;
  • recognize the pleural-effusion cluster: focal dullness + decreased fremitus + decreased breath sounds; and
  • recognize the consolidation cluster: focal dullness + increased fremitus + bronchial breath sounds or increased voice transmission.

For the Unit 1 Competency Exam, take the next step

The unit exam will ask you to apply the physical findings to a patient rather than simply name a breath sound.

  • Recognize the life-threatening tension-pneumothorax pattern from unilateral absent breath sounds, hyperresonance, decreased fremitus, tracheal deviation away, falling oxygen saturation, and hypotension.
  • Prioritize immediate escalation and oxygenation support over routine documentation when the patient is unstable.
  • Choose the next bedside assessment that directly tests the suspected pattern.
  • Use tactile fremitus and chest-expansion comparison to strengthen a suspected pleural-effusion pattern.
  • Recognize internally inconsistent data, such as hyperresonance paired with increased fremitus and bronchial breath sounds.
  • Repeat and compare percussion, fremitus, and auscultation when the findings conflict and the patient is stable.
  • Avoid confirmation bias—do not keep only the findings that support the diagnosis you expected.
  • Communicate laterality, location, air movement, percussion, fremitus, oxygen support, vital signs, trajectory, and urgency.
