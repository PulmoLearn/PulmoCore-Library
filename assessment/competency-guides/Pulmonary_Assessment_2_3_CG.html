Pulmonary Assessment • 2.3 Competency Guide

ECG Assessment: Acquisition, Interpretation, Patient Correlation & Artifact

Learn how to choose the ECG system that answers the clinical question, obtain an accurate tracing, locate the electrodes correctly, read the ECG grid and basic waveform events, recognize common rhythms and ischemic patterns, and verify every alarming tracing against the patient before acting.

Before you begin

An electrocardiogram records electrical activity, not the mechanical pulse. A perfectly interpreted strip from the wrong lead placement is unreliable. A normal telemetry rhythm does not rule out myocardial ischemia. A frightening monitor pattern in an awake patient with a strong regular pulse may be artifact. For respiratory therapists, ECG competency therefore begins with choosing the right monitoring system and validating the data before assigning meaning.

Big idea for Week 2.3: Follow the sequence clinical question → correct ECG system → accurate acquisition → structured interpretation → patient correlation → technical validation → communication or escalation.

Core vocabulary: bedside monitor, telemetry, diagnostic 12-lead ECG, electrode, lead, intercostal space, P wave, PR interval, QRS complex, ST segment, T wave, sinus rhythm, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, supraventricular tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, asystole, ischemia, injury, infarction, contiguous leads, artifact, wandering baseline, lead reversal.

C1: Select bedside monitoring, cardiac telemetry, or a diagnostic 12-lead ECG according to the patient's symptoms, mobility, setting, and diagnostic need.

Different ECG systems answer different questions

Bedside monitoring uses a small number of electrodes to provide continuous rhythm, rate, and alarm surveillance for patients in a fixed care location. It is common in intensive care, emergency, procedural, and transport settings.

Telemetry provides continuous rhythm surveillance through a wireless transmitter so a stable patient can move about the unit while rhythm data are sent to a central monitoring station.

A diagnostic 12-lead ECG uses ten electrodes to create twelve electrical views of the heart. It provides a diagnostic snapshot used to evaluate rhythm, conduction, chamber-stress patterns, ischemia, injury, and infarction.

Telemetry and a 12-lead are complementary

A patient can have sinus rhythm on telemetry and still have acute myocardial ischemia. Telemetry may display only selected leads; it cannot substitute for a diagnostic 12-lead when the question is acute coronary syndrome.

Symptoms that should prompt diagnostic consideration

Chest pressure, pain radiating to the arm or jaw, new diaphoresis, nausea, unexplained dyspnea, palpitations, syncope or presyncope, new unexplained tachycardia or bradycardia, or hemodynamic deterioration may warrant prompt 12-lead acquisition. Older adults, women, patients with diabetes, and critically ill patients may present atypically with dyspnea, weakness, nausea, or unexplained deterioration rather than classic crushing chest pain.

Respiratory-therapy relevance

Continuous ECG monitoring may be required during bronchoscopy, moderate sedation, mechanical ventilation, unstable transport, and other high-risk respiratory procedures. The monitoring system should match the information needed and the patient's mobility and acuity.

Clinical example: Crushing substernal pressure, diaphoresis, and nausea with sinus rhythm on telemetry still require prompt diagnostic 12-lead acquisition while vital signs and perfusion are assessed.
Putting C1 together
Choose continuous bedside monitoring for fixed high-acuity surveillance, telemetry for ambulatory rhythm surveillance, and a diagnostic 12-lead when the clinical question requires multi-lead diagnostic information.
C2: Prepare the patient and skin, position electrodes accurately, and verify technical settings to obtain an interpretable ECG.

Good interpretation begins with low-noise acquisition

Verify patient identity, explain that the ECG records electrical activity and does not deliver electricity, preserve privacy, and position the patient comfortably with the torso supported and muscles relaxed.

Reduce skin impedance

Clip excessive hair when needed, remove oil or lotion, dry diaphoresis, and gently prepare the skin according to equipment and facility guidance. Replace dry or expired electrodes and secure loose wires.

Use stable electrode sites

Avoid wounds, wet skin, large muscle groups, bony prominences, and sites with excessive motion when the system allows. Diagnostic limb placement and modified torso monitoring placement are not interchangeable; torso substitution can change morphology and should be documented.

Verify technical settings

Standard diagnostic ECG settings are commonly 25 mm/sec paper speed and 10 mm/mV gain. If a nonstandard setting is used, the tracing must be interpreted using that actual setting and labeled appropriately.

Assess the patient while acquiring the tracing

If the patient develops worsening chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, or instability during acquisition, patient assessment and emergency communication occur in parallel with technical troubleshooting.

Common mistake: Repeating a noisy tracing without correcting poor contact, sweat, tremor, movement, or cable problems simply reproduces bad data.
Putting C2 together
Prepare the patient and skin, secure electrodes and wires, verify speed and gain, and reduce noise before interpreting.
C3: Locate limb and precordial electrodes, including V1 through V6, using reproducible anatomic landmarks rather than estimation.

Use anatomy, not the nipple line

The six precordial, or chest, electrodes must be placed reproducibly because incorrect position can alter R-wave progression, ST-T morphology, axis interpretation, and automated analysis.

  1. V1: fourth intercostal space at the right sternal border.
  2. V2: fourth intercostal space at the left sternal border.
  3. V4: fifth intercostal space at the midclavicular line.
  4. V3: midway between V2 and V4; establish V2 and V4 first.
  5. V5: same horizontal level as V4 at the anterior axillary line.
  6. V6: same horizontal level as V4 at the midaxillary line.

Find the sternal angle, identify the second rib, and count intercostal spaces. Nipple position varies and is not a reliable landmark.

Limb electrodes

Standard diagnostic limb electrodes are placed on accepted limb sites. Monitoring systems may move limb electrodes to the torso to reduce motion, but a modified placement can change the tracing and should not be silently presented as a standard diagnostic ECG.

V4–V6 should stay horizontal

V5 and V6 are placed at the same horizontal level as V4 rather than following a downward curve around the chest.

Why exact placement matters: Incorrect V1/V2 height or lateral-lead placement can imitate conduction or ischemic abnormalities and make serial ECG comparison unreliable.
Putting C3 together
Locate V1–V6 from reproducible skeletal landmarks, not estimation, and document any necessary modified placement.
C4: Interpret ECG paper timing and voltage and identify the electrical events represented by the P wave, PR interval, QRS complex, ST segment, and T wave.

Read the grid before measuring the heart

At the standard speed of 25 mm/sec, one small horizontal box equals 0.04 second and one large box equals 0.20 second. Five large boxes equal one second. At the standard gain of 10 mm/mV, 10 vertical millimeters equal 1 millivolt.

The ECG records electrical events. Mechanical contraction and pulse must still be assessed at the bedside.

Data-quality check: If paper speed or gain is changed, measurements and apparent waveform size change. Confirm settings before calling an interval or voltage abnormal.
Putting C4 together
Know the standard grid and the major electrical events before interpreting intervals, rate, rhythm, or ST-T changes.
C5: Apply a systematic rhythm-analysis sequence to determine regularity, rate, atrial activity, atrioventricular relationship, PR interval, and QRS width.

Use the same rhythm sequence every time

  1. Regularity: Compare R-to-R intervals and, when needed, P-to-P intervals.
  2. Rate: Determine ventricular and atrial rate using an appropriate method for regular or irregular rhythms.
  3. Atrial activity: Are P waves present? Do they look alike? Is another atrial waveform present?
  4. Atrioventricular relationship: Does each P wave conduct to a QRS? Does each QRS have a corresponding atrial event?
  5. PR interval: Is it measurable and consistent?
  6. QRS width: Is ventricular activation narrow or wide?

Rate alone cannot identify the rhythm

A rate of 150/min could be sinus tachycardia, atrial flutter with 2:1 conduction, supraventricular tachycardia, or ventricular tachycardia depending on atrial activity, QRS width, regularity, and clinical context.

Compare the electrical rhythm with the pulse

When a rhythm is alarming, palpate the pulse and assess perfusion. Electrical activity does not guarantee effective mechanical output.

Beginner rule: Do not skip directly to a rhythm name. Describe what you see first.
Putting C5 together
Regularity → rate → atrial activity → P/QRS relationship → PR → QRS width → patient correlation.
C6: Recognize normal sinus rhythm and common patterns including sinus bradycardia or tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, supraventricular tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, and asystole.

Recognize high-yield rhythm patterns

Normal sinus rhythm is regular, has a normal-rate sinus pattern, a consistent P wave before each QRS, and a consistent PR relationship.

Sinus bradycardia and sinus tachycardia preserve the sinus P-QRS relationship but occur at slow or fast rates. The patient determines whether the rate is tolerated.

Atrial fibrillation has no consistent normal P waves and an irregularly irregular ventricular response.

Atrial flutter produces rapid organized atrial activity, often with repetitive flutter waves and a fixed or variable ventricular conduction ratio.

Supraventricular tachycardia is commonly a very regular narrow-complex tachycardia in which atrial activity may be difficult to identify because of the rapid rate.

Ventricular tachycardia is an organized wide-complex ventricular rhythm. It may occur with a pulse or without one. Pulse status changes the emergency pathway.

Ventricular fibrillation is chaotic ventricular electrical activity with no effective organized ventricular contraction and no pulse.

Asystole is absence of meaningful ventricular electrical activity. Verify the patient, connections, and more than one appropriate view before accepting an apparent flat line as true asystole.

Patient-first rule: Whenever VT, VF, or another alarming rhythm appears, state whether the patient has a pulse and whether perfusion is adequate.
Putting C6 together
Recognize the electrical pattern, then immediately determine pulse and stability.
C7: Recognize chest-pain and ischemic or injury patterns that require prompt diagnostic 12-lead acquisition, comparison, communication, and escalation.

Ischemia can occur in a patient with a normal monitor rhythm

Start with symptoms and stability. Ask about onset, quality, location, radiation, severity, duration, provoking or relieving factors, dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, weakness, and syncope while vital signs, oxygenation, rhythm, and perfusion are assessed.

Obtain a diagnostic 12-lead promptly

Suspected acute coronary syndrome requires prompt diagnostic ECG evaluation even when telemetry shows sinus rhythm. Troponin and other biomarkers complement the ECG but do not replace timely acquisition.

Ischemic patterns

ST-segment depression and T-wave inversion may support myocardial ischemia, but they are not disease-specific. Interpret them with symptoms, contiguous leads, prior ECGs, and serial change.

Injury patterns

New ST-segment elevation in anatomically related contiguous leads with compatible symptoms is a high-risk time-sensitive finding requiring immediate communication and activation of the institutional acute-coronary pathway.

Q waves and infarction

Pathologic Q waves may suggest established myocardial necrosis or prior infarction. They do not alone determine that the event is currently acute.

NSTEMI can occur without ST elevation

A non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction is diagnosed from symptoms, serial ECG changes, and cardiac biomarkers. The absence of ST elevation does not make severe chest symptoms nonurgent.

Lead groups help communicate location

  • Inferior: II, III, aVF
  • Lateral: I, aVL, V5, V6
  • Anterior/septal: V1–V4
Clinical reasoning: A normal first ECG does not always end evaluation. Evolving symptoms may require serial tracings and comparison with prior ECGs.
Putting C7 together
Connect symptoms and stability with prompt 12-lead acquisition, contiguous-lead changes, prior/serial comparison, and urgent communication.
C8: Correlate ECG findings with pulse, blood pressure, mental status, symptoms, and perfusion before determining whether a rhythm is stable, unstable, pulseless, or artifactual.

Treat the patient, not the monitor

The same wide-complex tachycardia has a different pathway in an alert patient with a strong pulse and normal blood pressure than in an unresponsive pulseless patient.

Signs of poor rhythm tolerance

Hypotension, weak or absent pulse, altered mental status, syncope, ischemic chest discomfort, worsening dyspnea, cool or mottled skin, delayed capillary refill, or signs of shock suggest that the rhythm is not producing adequate circulation.

Pulseless electrical activity requires the patient assessment

Organized electrical activity on a monitor with no palpable pulse is pulseless electrical activity. The rhythm strip alone cannot make that diagnosis because the defining finding is absence of mechanical circulation.

Respiratory causes can contribute to rhythm instability

Hypoxemia, hypercapnia, severe acid-base disturbance, beta-agonist medications, and electrolyte abnormalities may change rate or rhythm. Correlate new ECG findings with the respiratory event.

Key question: Is the rhythm causing instability, reflecting instability, or both?
Putting C8 together
Use pulse, pressure, consciousness, symptoms, and perfusion to determine what the electrical tracing means clinically.
C9: Identify and correct wandering baseline, motion or tremor artifact, poor electrode contact, electrical interference, and other acquisition problems before reporting the tracing.

Artifact can imitate lethal rhythms

Motion or tremor artifact may create rapid irregular deflections that resemble atrial or ventricular arrhythmia. Wandering baseline causes slow up-and-down drift from breathing, movement, or poor contact. Loose electrodes may create intermittent breaks or abrupt shifts. Electrical interference can create repetitive high-frequency noise.

Use the mechanical pulse as a reality check

During a nebulizer treatment, a monitor suddenly shows a wide-complex rhythm at 190/min. The patient is alert and talking, while the palpable pulse is regular at 88/min and the hands are trembling. The monitor-pulse mismatch strongly supports tremor artifact.

Correct the source

  1. Assess the patient and pulse.
  2. Check electrodes and lead wires.
  3. Reduce movement or tremor when possible.
  4. Clean/dry skin and replace poor electrodes.
  5. Move or troubleshoot electrical interference sources.
  6. Reacquire a clean tracing before reporting a rhythm diagnosis.
Safety distinction: If the patient is unstable, support and escalate while technical validation occurs. Do not spend several minutes perfecting electrodes on a collapsing patient.
Putting C9 together
Artifact is a data-quality problem. Compare tracing with patient and pulse, fix the technical cause, and reacquire before reporting.
C10: Recognize lead reversal or misplacement, correct the technical error, reacquire the tracing, and communicate only validated ECG data.

A technically clean tracing can still be wrong

Lead reversal or electrode misplacement may create a smooth, noise-free ECG that is physiologically misleading. This is more dangerous than obvious artifact because the tracing can look believable.

Right-arm/left-arm reversal

Reversing right-arm and left-arm electrodes changes limb-lead orientation and can produce unusual axis and polarity patterns that imitate disease. If a new ECG suddenly conflicts with the patient's prior tracing without a clinical explanation, inspect electrode identity and placement.

Chest-lead misplacement

V1/V2 placed too high or V4–V6 placed at incorrect horizontal levels can alter R-wave progression and ST-T morphology. Re-landmark anatomically rather than copying the old position.

Do not interpret first and verify later

When technical error is suspected, correct the placement, reacquire the ECG, compare the new tracing with the prior study, and communicate the validated result. Do not report a suspected ischemic or axis change from a tracing you believe is technically wrong.

Symptomatic patients still require parallel clinical assessment

If the patient has active chest pain or instability, technical correction and emergency assessment occur simultaneously. Do not let troubleshooting delay urgent care.

Competency-exam thinking: When a newly abnormal ECG is inconsistent with the patient and prior tracing, verify lead placement before treating the technical pattern as a new diagnosis.
Putting C10 together
Suspect placement error when a clean tracing does not make physiologic sense; correct, reacquire, compare, and report only validated data.

Week 2.3: Reliable tracing + structured analysis + patient correlation

ECG assessment is not rhythm-name memorization. Choose the correct system, obtain trustworthy data, analyze systematically, determine whether the patient has effective circulation, and recognize when symptoms require diagnostic 12-lead evaluation even if continuous monitoring looks reassuring.

question → system → landmarks → grid/settings → rhythm analysis → ischemia check → pulse/perfusion → artifact/placement validation → communicate/escalate

Before the weekly quiz, you should be able to...

  • distinguish bedside monitoring, telemetry, and diagnostic 12-lead ECG;
  • prepare the skin and verify standard ECG settings;
  • place V1 at the fourth intercostal space right sternal border and V4 at the fifth intercostal space midclavicular line;
  • place V2, V3, V5, and V6 using correct relationships rather than estimation;
  • recall that one small box at 25 mm/sec equals 0.04 second and one large box equals 0.20 second;
  • identify the electrical events represented by the P wave and QRS complex;
  • apply a regularity-rate-P-wave-PR-QRS analysis sequence;
  • recognize normal sinus rhythm, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, SVT, VT, VF, and asystole;
  • distinguish organized VT from chaotic VF and state pulse status;
  • recognize acute coronary symptoms as an indication for prompt diagnostic 12-lead ECG even with reassuring telemetry;
  • recognize motion/tremor artifact when the monitor and palpable pulse disagree; and
  • recognize lead reversal or misplacement as a reason to correct and reacquire before reporting.

For the Unit 2 Competency Exam, take the next step

  • Recognize tremor artifact from a chaotic monitor tracing when the patient is alert and the palpable pulse is regular at a much lower rate.
  • Assess the patient before treating an alarming monitor display.
  • Obtain a diagnostic 12-lead promptly for acute coronary symptoms even when telemetry shows sinus rhythm.
  • Recognize that a single monitoring lead cannot exclude ischemia or injury in other territories.
  • Compare serial and prior ECGs when symptoms evolve.
  • Recognize suspected lead reversal or chest-lead misplacement when a new tracing conflicts with the patient and prior ECG.
  • Correct the technical error and reacquire the tracing before reporting a new abnormality.
  • Communicate the ECG finding with pulse, blood pressure, symptoms, oxygenation, mental status, technical validity, and urgency.
National Board for Respiratory Care connection: The Respiratory Therapy Examination expects you to select and operate ECG/monitoring systems, obtain technically valid data, evaluate ECG results, correlate electrical findings with the patient's condition, recognize high-risk cardiopulmonary patterns, and communicate concerns leading to escalation of care.
