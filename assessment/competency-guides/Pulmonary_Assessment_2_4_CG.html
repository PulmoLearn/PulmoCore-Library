Pulmonary Assessment • 2.4 Competency Guide

Radiologic Examination: Imaging Selection, CXR Interpretation, Device Position & Safety

Learn how respiratory therapists choose the imaging study that answers the clinical question, decide whether a chest radiograph is trustworthy, recognize major cardiopulmonary patterns, verify airway-device position, and protect the patient during imaging and transport.

Before you begin

Imaging is another form of patient data. A chest radiograph, computed tomography scan, magnetic resonance imaging study, echocardiogram, positron emission tomography scan, or ventilation-perfusion scan can provide important information—but only if the study answers the right question and the patient can undergo it safely.

Big idea for Week 2.4: Start with the clinical question, not the fanciest test. Then ask: Is this the safest useful study? Is the image technically adequate? What major pattern or device problem is present? Does it match the bedside assessment? Is the finding stable enough for more imaging, or does it require immediate action?

C1: Select chest radiography, CT or CTPA, MRI, echocardiography or TEE, PET, or V/Q imaging according to the clinical question and patient-specific safety constraints.

Start with the question, not the modality

The “best” imaging study is not always the most detailed study. The best study is the one that answers the immediate question with acceptable risk.

Clinical questionUseful study
Where is the endotracheal tube after intubation?Portable chest radiograph
Is there a pulmonary arterial filling defect from pulmonary embolism?Computed tomography pulmonary angiography
What does detailed thoracic anatomy show?Computed tomography
What is the heart's structure and function?Echocardiography
Is metabolically active tissue present?Positron emission tomography
Is ventilation present where pulmonary perfusion is missing?Ventilation-perfusion imaging

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography, commonly shortened to CTPA, uses timed iodinated contrast to outline the pulmonary arteries. It is a primary test for suspected pulmonary embolism when the patient is stable enough for transport and contrast can be given safely.

If the patient has severe iodinated-contrast allergy or major renal impairment, routine CTPA may create avoidable risk. A ventilation-perfusion scan, commonly shortened to V/Q scan, may be a useful alternative when the clinical situation and chest radiograph make it appropriate.

Clinical reasoning: A normal chest radiograph does not rule out pulmonary embolism. Chest radiography and pulmonary arterial imaging answer different questions.

Know what the study cannot answer

Portable chest radiography is fast and useful for tube depth and major thoracic change, but it is two-dimensional and may miss smaller lesions or pulmonary emboli. Standard chest CT gives detailed cross-sectional anatomy but does not automatically equal a pulmonary angiographic study. CTPA is timed specifically to opacify the pulmonary arteries. MRI avoids ionizing radiation but introduces magnetic-field compatibility, monitoring, access, and time limitations. PET answers a metabolic-activity question rather than an acute airway or pleural question.

A normal chest radiograph does not end every workup

Pulmonary embolism, severe bronchospasm, early disease, and other important conditions may occur with a normal or near-normal radiograph. Use the image for the question it can answer and choose another modality when the unresolved question requires it.

Putting C1 together
Choose the study by matching the clinical question with the safest useful modality for that specific patient.
C2: Explain the physical principle, primary clinical use, major limitation, and respiratory-therapy relevance of each major cardiopulmonary imaging modality.

Know what each study can—and cannot—tell you

Chest radiography uses ionizing radiation to create a two-dimensional image. It is fast and widely available, especially at the bedside, but it provides less anatomical detail than computed tomography.

Computed tomography, commonly called CT, uses multiple x-ray measurements to produce cross-sectional images. It can show lung parenchyma, pleura, mediastinum, masses, trauma, bronchiectasis, and other detailed structures.

Magnetic resonance imaging, commonly called MRI, uses a strong magnetic field and radiofrequency energy rather than ionizing radiation. It provides excellent soft-tissue detail but takes longer, limits access to the patient, and creates major compatibility hazards for implants and equipment.

Transthoracic echocardiography uses ultrasound from outside the chest to evaluate heart structure and function. Transesophageal echocardiography, commonly called TEE, places an ultrasound probe in the esophagus for closer cardiac views. TEE is invasive and can create sedation and airway risk.

Positron emission tomography, commonly called PET, uses a radiotracer to evaluate metabolic activity. It is useful for malignancy, metastatic disease, inflammation, or treatment response—not for acute airway-device position or sudden pneumothorax.

Ventilation-perfusion imaging compares where inhaled tracer reaches with where injected tracer travels through pulmonary blood flow.

Quiz habit: For every modality, know its purpose, one important limitation, and what respiratory support or safety issue the therapist may need to manage.

Physical principle helps explain the limitation

X-ray based studies depend on differential absorption of ionizing radiation. CT obtains many x-ray measurements to reconstruct cross-sectional anatomy. MRI uses magnetic fields and radiofrequency signals. Echocardiography uses reflected ultrasound. PET and V/Q imaging use radiotracers. Knowing the physical principle helps predict what the study visualizes well, what safety hazard accompanies it, and why another study may be needed.

TEE deserves separate respiratory planning

Transesophageal echocardiography places a probe in the esophagus and may use topical anesthesia and sedation. Unlike transthoracic echocardiography, it can create airway, ventilation, aspiration, and sedation-recovery concerns that require appropriate respiratory monitoring and emergency readiness.

Putting C2 together
Do not memorize image names alone. Know the question each modality is designed to answer.
C3: Prepare, monitor, and support patients safely during imaging, transport, contrast administration, MRI exposure, nuclear-medicine procedures, and sedation-dependent studies.

Leaving the bedside changes the risk

Transporting a patient to imaging often moves the patient away from familiar staff, wall oxygen, suction, emergency supplies, and immediate help. The respiratory-care plan must continue during transport and the procedure.

Before leaving the unit, verify airway security, oxygen supply, ventilator settings, battery life, monitoring, suction, manual ventilation capability, emergency airway supplies, medication needs, and the handoff plan.

MRI is a special environment

Never assume a device is safe because it looks harmless. Verify whether ventilators, monitors, oxygen systems, infusion pumps, implants, and other equipment are labeled safe or conditional for the MRI environment. Ferromagnetic objects can become dangerous projectiles.

TEE can create airway and sedation risk

Transesophageal echocardiography may involve topical anesthesia and sedation. Monitor oxygen saturation, electrocardiogram, blood pressure, ventilation, and level of consciousness. Have suction, oxygen, and airway-support equipment available according to policy.

Contrast and nuclear-medicine procedures

When iodinated contrast is planned, review relevant allergy history, renal function, vascular access, and institutional preparation. For nuclear-medicine procedures, follow facility radiation-safety, contamination-control, and infection-prevention procedures.

Safety priority: Never reduce respiratory monitoring simply because the patient has entered the radiology department. Continuity of airway, oxygenation, ventilation, and monitoring remains essential.

Plan for loss of normal bedside resources

Before transporting a critically ill patient, calculate whether oxygen supply and battery life will last for transport, waiting, the study, and return with a safety margin. Verify portable suction, manual ventilation capability, emergency airway supplies, infusion continuity, monitoring, and who will manage the airway during the procedure.

Do not casually disconnect a ventilator circuit for imaging

Circuit disconnection can interrupt PEEP, derecruit unstable lungs, expose staff to secretions, and increase contamination. Coordinate positioning and imaging around the airway and circuit whenever possible.

Putting C3 together
Plan for the respiratory risks of transport, equipment compatibility, contrast, sedation, and limited access before the procedure starts.
C4: Evaluate chest-radiograph technical quality, including projection, patient positioning and rotation, inspiration, penetration, and motion, before interpreting anatomy or pathology.

Quality first, pathology second

A technically poor chest radiograph can imitate disease. Before interpreting anatomy, check patient identity, date and time, projection, position, rotation, inspiration, penetration, motion, and image coverage.

Projection

A standard upright posteroanterior chest radiograph, often written as PA, passes the beam from back to front. A portable anteroposterior image, often written as AP, passes the beam from front to back. AP projection can magnify the heart and mediastinum, so apparent cardiomegaly must be interpreted carefully.

Rotation

If the patient is rotated, the mediastinum, heart, and lung density can appear asymmetric. Do not call a subtle mediastinal shift until rotation has been considered.

Inspiration

Poor inspiration makes the lungs look smaller and denser and can exaggerate heart size. Compare the diaphragm and visible rib expansion with the quality expected for the image.

Penetration and motion

An underpenetrated image may look too white and hide structures. An overpenetrated image may look too dark and hide subtle opacities. Motion blurs borders and can make lines and lung markings difficult to interpret.

Clinical reasoning: If technical quality prevents a confident conclusion, say so. Do not convert an image limitation into a diagnosis.

Technical error can imitate pathology

Rotation can imitate mediastinal shift or asymmetric lung density. Poor inspiration can make the bases look crowded and the heart appear larger. Underpenetration can create false opacity; overpenetration can hide subtle lung markings. AP portable projection magnifies the heart compared with a standard PA image. These are not small technical details—they change what diagnoses the image can support.

Putting C4 together
Assess projection, position, rotation, inspiration, penetration, motion, and coverage before deciding what the image means.
C5: Apply a systematic chest-radiograph review sequence that includes airway, tubes and lines, bones and soft tissues, cardiac and mediastinal structures, diaphragms and pleural spaces, and lung fields.

Use the same sequence every time

A repeatable sequence helps prevent the common error of seeing one dramatic abnormality and forgetting to inspect the rest of the image.

  1. Airway: Check tracheal position and the carina.
  2. Tubes and lines: Evaluate endotracheal tube, enteric tube, central lines, chest tubes, and other visible devices.
  3. Bones and soft tissues: Look for fractures, deformity, subcutaneous air, or asymmetry.
  4. Cardiac and mediastinal structures: Assess heart size, mediastinal width, and shift.
  5. Diaphragms and pleural spaces: Check costophrenic angles, pleural fluid, pleural air, and diaphragm position.
  6. Lung fields: Compare both lungs for opacity, lucency, vascular markings, volume loss, edema, consolidation, or hyperinflation.

Then compare the image with any prior study. New change is often more important than a chronic stable abnormality.

RT priority: Review the airway and devices early. A correctly recognized lung opacity does not protect the patient from an endotracheal tube that is too deep.

Describe before naming

When an abnormality is found, first describe its side, location, density or lucency, distribution, associated volume change, pleural involvement, and device relationship. This reduces confirmation bias and makes communication more useful than saying only “looks like pneumonia.”

Review visible devices beyond the endotracheal tube

Enteric tubes should follow an expected esophageal course below the diaphragm, central venous lines should terminate in an acceptable central location according to policy, and chest tubes should follow an appropriate pleural course without obvious side-port displacement. The respiratory-therapy goal is recognition and escalation, not memorization of every specialty-device criterion.

Putting C5 together
Use one complete sequence for every image so airway and device safety are never skipped.
C6: Recognize common cardiopulmonary imaging patterns including consolidation, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, pulmonary edema, cardiomegaly, atelectatic volume loss, and obstructive hyperinflation when present.

Recognize high-yield patterns, not every radiology diagnosis

Consolidation

Consolidation appears as increased lung opacity when air spaces fill with inflammatory material, fluid, blood, or cells. The pattern should be correlated with fever, sputum, focal breath sounds, oxygenation, and other data rather than being labeled by image alone.

Pneumothorax

A pneumothorax may show a visible pleural line with absent peripheral vascular markings beyond that line. The involved side may appear more lucent because pleural air contains no pulmonary vessels.

Pleural effusion

An upright chest radiograph may show blunting of the costophrenic angle and a curved or meniscus-shaped opacity when pleural fluid is present.

Cardiogenic pulmonary edema

Common findings include bilateral perihilar opacities, vascular congestion, possible Kerley B lines, pleural effusions, and cardiomegaly. The image should be integrated with blood pressure, cardiac history, fluid status, crackles, oxygenation, and work of breathing.

Cardiomegaly

Cardiomegaly means an enlarged cardiac silhouette. Remember that AP portable projection can exaggerate apparent heart size.

Atelectatic volume loss

Atelectasis may create opacity with signs of volume loss, such as displaced fissures, elevated diaphragm, rib crowding, or shift toward the affected side when the volume loss is substantial.

Obstructive hyperinflation

Hyperinflation may produce increased lung volume, lower or flatter diaphragms, increased lucency, and a more elongated cardiac silhouette. It must be interpreted with patient history and technique.

Pattern-thinking rule: Describe location, distribution, volume change, pleural findings, and associated bedside data before naming the likely process.

Consolidation versus atelectasis

Both can produce opacity. Atelectasis is distinguished by evidence of volume loss, such as fissure displacement, elevated hemidiaphragm, rib crowding, or mediastinal pull toward the affected side. Consolidation may show air bronchograms without the same volume-loss pattern.

Position changes pleural appearances

Pleural air and fluid do not always appear as the classic upright textbook pattern. In supine patients, pneumothorax may collect anteriorly or basally and effusion may layer diffusely. Use the projection and patient position before deciding that a classic pleural sign must be absent.

Kerley B lines and cardiomegaly are clues, not complete diagnoses

Kerley B lines represent interstitial thickening and should be interpreted with vascular congestion, edema distribution, effusions, heart size, and bedside findings. Apparent cardiomegaly on an AP portable image should be qualified because projection magnification can enlarge the silhouette.

Putting C6 together
Recognize the major patterns respiratory therapists are expected to identify and connect them with the patient rather than attempting a radiologist-level differential diagnosis.
C7: Interpret basic V/Q scan findings and distinguish a ventilation-perfusion mismatch from a matched defect while recognizing the influence of chest-radiograph abnormalities on test usefulness.

Ventilation and perfusion should generally match

The ventilation phase shows where inhaled tracer reaches the lungs. The perfusion phase shows where tracer delivered through the bloodstream reaches the pulmonary circulation.

A region that is ventilated but underperfused creates a ventilation-perfusion mismatch. Multiple segmental mismatched defects can increase concern for pulmonary embolism when interpreted with the patient's clinical probability and other imaging.

A matched defect means ventilation and perfusion are both reduced in the same area. Matched defects are not necessarily normal; they may reflect pneumonia, chronic lung disease, edema, or another regional abnormality.

A near-normal chest radiograph can make V/Q interpretation more useful. Extensive pneumonia, pulmonary edema, or chronic obstructive disease may create more complicated matched and mismatched patterns and reduce specificity.

Exam connection: A stable patient with suspected pulmonary embolism, severe iodinated-contrast allergy, major renal impairment, and a near-normal chest radiograph is a classic situation in which V/Q imaging may be selected instead of routine CTPA.

A V/Q scan is easiest to interpret when the background lung is relatively clear

Extensive pneumonia, pulmonary edema, or chronic structural lung disease can create complicated ventilation and perfusion defects. A near-normal chest radiograph can make a mismatched perfusion defect more useful for pulmonary-embolism assessment.

Putting C7 together
Mismatch means ventilation is present where perfusion is reduced. Matched defects mean both are abnormal in the same region.
C8: Evaluate adult endotracheal tube position on chest radiography and integrate device depth with bilateral breath sounds, oxygenation, ventilation, recent movement, and the need for repositioning or repeat confirmation.

Endotracheal tube depth can change ventilation immediately

An endotracheal tube passes through the vocal cords into the trachea. The carina is the point where the trachea divides into the right and left main bronchi.

On a neutral-head adult chest radiograph, the tube tip is generally expected to rest several centimeters above the carina, often about 3–5 cm depending on local criteria and patient anatomy. A tip at the carina is too low because normal head and neck movement can move the tube farther into a main bronchus.

Right-mainstem intubation

If the tube advances into the right main bronchus, the right lung may be ventilated while left-lung ventilation decreases. Clues include worsening oxygenation, reduced left chest rise, markedly diminished left breath sounds, and a radiograph showing the tube tip within the right mainstem bronchus.

The response is to withdraw the tube to an appropriate depth, reassess bilateral chest movement and breath sounds, confirm exhaled carbon dioxide and oxygenation, secure the tube, document the new depth, and obtain repeat confirmation as indicated.

A cuff does not prevent mainstem intubation. The entire tube and cuff can migrate distally.

Patient-first rule: Do not wait for a final radiology report when the bedside examination and image clearly show an immediately correctable mainstem tube position.

Head and neck movement changes tube depth

Neck flexion tends to move an endotracheal tube tip toward the carina, while extension tends to withdraw it. Recent transport, repositioning, bed movement, or fixation changes can explain why a previously acceptable tube becomes too deep or too shallow.

Confirm more than the radiographic depth

After repositioning, reassess bilateral chest rise and breath sounds, oxygenation, exhaled carbon dioxide, airway pressures when ventilated, securement, and the new documented depth. Imaging confirms depth and complications but does not replace immediate bedside airway verification.

Putting C8 together
Evaluate tube depth with the radiograph and bedside findings together, correct malposition, and then confirm that ventilation has actually improved.
C9: Integrate imaging findings with patient symptoms, respiratory examination, oxygenation, ventilation, and hemodynamics to determine which abnormality is most urgent.

The most dramatic image is not always the most urgent problem

Interpret imaging with the patient's symptoms, breath sounds, work of breathing, oxygen support, oxygen saturation, ventilation, blood pressure, heart rate, mental status, and recent procedures.

A chronic large opacity in a comfortable patient may be less urgent than a new small pneumothorax in a mechanically ventilated patient whose blood pressure is falling.

Look for agreement between the bedside and the image

Unilateral breath-sound loss plus a right-mainstem endotracheal tube position strongly supports tube malposition. Bilateral crackles, hypoxemia, elevated blood pressure, cardiomegaly, vascular congestion, and bilateral perihilar opacities support cardiogenic pulmonary edema.

If the image and bedside do not agree, verify image quality, patient identity, timing, current device position, and whether the patient's condition changed after the image was obtained.

Clinical reasoning: Imaging is a snapshot. The patient may have changed since the exposure was made.

Physiologic effect determines urgency

A small stable pneumothorax and a tension pneumothorax are not equivalent simply because both contain pleural air. Hypotension, severe distress, rapidly worsening oxygenation, unilateral absent breath sounds, and pressure-related shift make the tension physiology urgent.

Putting C9 together
Prioritize the finding that best explains the patient's current instability, not simply the largest radiographic abnormality.
C10: Apply radiation, contrast, renal, pregnancy, MRI, transport, equipment-compatibility, infection-prevention, and sedation-monitoring precautions.

Safety changes the imaging pathway

Radiation protection

Ionizing-radiation exposure should be kept as low as reasonably achievable, commonly remembered as ALARA. Practical principles include minimizing unnecessary time near the active beam, maximizing distance, using appropriate barriers or shielding according to policy, and avoiding the primary beam.

Contrast and renal risk

Iodinated contrast may be needed for studies such as CTPA. Review relevant allergy history, kidney function, vascular access, and institutional protocols. A major safety concern may change the preferred study rather than canceling the diagnostic question.

Pregnancy

Pregnancy requires a risk-benefit discussion about radiation, contrast, and alternative modalities. Do not assume that all imaging is prohibited. The correct decision depends on urgency, diagnostic value, gestational considerations, and local policy.

MRI compatibility

Screen implants, foreign bodies, ventilators, monitors, oxygen cylinders, infusion pumps, tools, and personal items before entering controlled MRI zones. Compatibility is a hard safety requirement.

Infection prevention

Maintain clean respiratory equipment, appropriate isolation precautions, airway-device security, and safe handling of circuits during transport and imaging. Avoid unnecessary ventilator-circuit disconnection.

Clinical reasoning: Safety precautions should protect the patient without losing sight of the diagnostic question that still needs an answer.

ALARA is a practical workflow

Keep exposure as low as reasonably achievable by minimizing unnecessary time near the active beam, maximizing distance, using structural or personal protection according to policy, coordinating positioning before exposure to reduce repeats, and never standing in the primary beam without a necessary clinical reason.

Pregnancy changes the risk-benefit analysis—not automatically the need for diagnosis

Verify pregnancy status when relevant, communicate the urgency and body region, use current institutional radiation/contrast policy, and select an alternative modality when it provides equivalent information with less risk. Do not cancel clinically necessary imaging reflexively.

Putting C10 together
Modify the imaging plan when contrast, renal, pregnancy, MRI, transport, infection, or sedation risks change what can be done safely.
C11: Recognize tension pneumothorax, severe pulmonary edema, airway-device malposition, and other high-risk imaging patterns that require immediate communication and escalation.

When imaging becomes an emergency clue

Tension pneumothorax

A tension pneumothorax is a pressure emergency. Pleural air accumulates and compresses the lung and central circulation. A chest image may show a pleural line, absent peripheral markings, lung collapse, and mediastinal shift away from the affected side.

But the most important clues are often at the bedside: severe distress, hypotension, sudden unilateral breath-sound loss, worsening oxygenation, and contralateral tracheal deviation in advanced cases.

Emergency priority: If tension pneumothorax is clinically evident in an unstable patient, activate emergency management. Do not delay treatment to obtain another confirmatory image.

Severe pulmonary edema

Bilateral edema findings become urgent when paired with severe hypoxemia, marked work of breathing, inability to lie flat, altered mental status, or hemodynamic instability.

Airway-device malposition

An endotracheal tube in a mainstem bronchus or another clearly unsafe position can create immediate reversible hypoxemia. Correct the position and reassess rather than treating only the oxygen saturation.

Oxygen improvement does not remove tension physiology

Supplemental oxygen may temporarily raise SpO2 while intrathoracic pressure continues to compress venous return and cardiac output. Hemodynamic compromise and the mechanical pressure problem still require emergency treatment.

Putting C11 together
Recognize which image findings are urgent because they are producing airway, breathing, oxygenation, ventilation, or circulatory instability.
C12: Withhold unsupported conclusions from a technically inadequate study and recommend repeat imaging, an alternate modality, additional bedside assessment, or urgent intervention as appropriate.

Sometimes the correct interpretation is “this study is not good enough”

Good clinical judgment includes knowing when not to make a confident conclusion. A rotated, poorly inspired, underpenetrated, motion-blurred, incompletely covered, or incorrectly identified image may not support the answer you need.

Choose the next step based on urgency

  • Stable patient + technically poor image: repeat or correct the image when the missing information matters.
  • Wrong modality for the question: recommend a more appropriate study.
  • Imaging does not fit the bedside: repeat bedside assessment and verify timing, identity, and current device position.
  • Unstable patient with an obvious emergency pattern: intervene and escalate rather than waiting for perfect imaging.

Clinical example

A portable AP chest radiograph is severely rotated and poorly inspired. The cardiac silhouette looks large and the mediastinum appears shifted. The patient is stable. The correct response is not to diagnose cardiomegaly and mediastinal shift confidently. State the technical limitations and obtain a more reliable study if those conclusions would affect care.

Competency-exam thinking: Withholding an unsupported conclusion is a clinical skill—not a failure to interpret.

Match the next step to the reason the study failed

If rotation or poor inspiration prevents interpretation, repeat the image when the answer matters and the patient is stable. If the wrong modality was chosen, select the modality that answers the unresolved question. If the patient became unstable, bedside reassessment and treatment may take priority over obtaining a technically perfect study.

Putting C12 together
Know when the study is inadequate, explain why, and choose the safest next information-gathering or intervention step.

Week 2.4: Image + bedside + urgency

Imaging should strengthen your understanding of the patient, not replace the bedside assessment. First decide whether the study is appropriate and technically trustworthy. Then identify the major pattern, check devices, correlate the image with current physiology, and act according to urgency.

clinical question → safest modality → technical quality → airway/devices → systematic image review → bedside correlation → urgency → communicate, correct, repeat, or escalate

Before the weekly quiz, you should be able to...

  • choose portable chest radiography for rapid post-intubation tube-position confirmation;
  • choose CTPA when pulmonary embolism is suspected and iodinated contrast and transport are acceptable;
  • explain that MRI provides strong soft-tissue detail without ionizing radiation but requires strict compatibility screening;
  • distinguish transthoracic echocardiography from invasive, sedation-related TEE;
  • identify PET as metabolic imaging rather than an acute airway or pleural study;
  • explain the ventilation and perfusion phases of a V/Q scan and identify a mismatch;
  • evaluate projection, rotation, inspiration, penetration, motion, and image coverage before pathology;
  • use a consistent airway/devices/bones/cardiac/diaphragm/lung-field review sequence;
  • recognize pneumothorax from a pleural line with absent peripheral vascular markings;
  • recognize pleural effusion from costophrenic-angle blunting and a meniscus-type opacity;
  • recognize cardiogenic pulmonary-edema patterns such as bilateral perihilar opacities, vascular congestion, cardiomegaly, or effusions;
  • identify expected adult endotracheal tube position above the carina and recognize a right-mainstem position;
  • apply radiation, contrast, renal, pregnancy, MRI, transport, infection, and sedation precautions; and
  • withhold a confident conclusion when the image is technically inadequate.

For the Unit 2 Competency Exam, take the next step

  • Select V/Q imaging for a stable patient with suspected pulmonary embolism when severe iodinated-contrast allergy and renal impairment make routine CTPA unsafe and the chest radiograph is suitable.
  • Explain why the diagnostic question still needs an answer even when the usual imaging pathway is contraindicated.
  • Recognize right-mainstem intubation from post-intubation hypoxemia, markedly diminished left breath sounds, and a tube tip in the right main bronchus.
  • Withdraw the malpositioned tube to an appropriate depth, reassess bilateral ventilation and oxygenation, secure the tube, document the new depth, and confirm position.
  • Differentiate tube malposition from pneumothorax, obstruction, equipment failure, atelectasis, aspiration, mucus plugging, bronchospasm, or hemodynamic change when post-intubation deterioration persists.
  • Recognize severe distress, hypotension, unilateral absent breath sounds, and contralateral tracheal or mediastinal shift as a tension-pneumothorax pattern.
  • Activate immediate emergency escalation for clinically evident tension pneumothorax instead of waiting for additional imaging.
  • Recognize when a technically inadequate study should be repeated or replaced with another modality instead of being overinterpreted.
