Pulmonary Assessment • 2.1 Competency Guide

Advanced Blood Gas: Mixed Disorders, Monitoring Technology & Clinical Integration

Learn how to interpret arterial blood gases as part of the whole patient picture, recognize mixed acid-base disorders, identify unreliable samples, choose the right monitoring technology, and decide whether a patient is improving or deteriorating.

Before you begin

Arterial blood gas interpretation can feel like learning a new language. The numbers are important, but the goal is not simply to memorize whether one value is high or low. You are learning to connect the values to ventilation, acid-base balance, oxygenation, chronic baseline, sample quality, and the patient's current condition.

Big idea for Week 2.1: Before acting on a blood-gas result, ask: Is this the correct patient and sample? Were the oxygen or ventilator settings known? Are the numbers physiologically believable? What acid-base process is present? Is compensation appropriate? Does the result match the patient's condition and trend?

Vocabulary you will use this week: arterial blood gas, acidemia, alkalemia, partial pressure of carbon dioxide, bicarbonate, compensation, mixed acid-base disorder, acute, chronic, venous admixture, preanalytic error, analytic error, co-oximetry, capnography, end-tidal carbon dioxide, transcutaneous monitoring, point-of-care testing.

C1: Recall normal adult arterial blood-gas ranges and use a systematic sequence to identify acidemia or alkalemia, the primary respiratory or metabolic process, and the direction of compensation.

Start with context before the numbers

An arterial blood gas, commonly shortened to ABG, measures important values in arterial blood. Before interpreting it, verify the patient, sample source, collection time, oxygen device or ventilator settings, fraction of inspired oxygen, recent treatment, and prior baseline.

Core reference ranges for this course

Value Reference range What it represents
pH7.35–7.45Overall acid-base condition of the blood
Partial pressure of carbon dioxide35–45 mmHgRespiratory component; closely reflects alveolar ventilation
BicarbonateAbout 22–26 mEq/LMajor measured metabolic base component

The partial pressure of oxygen is also measured on an arterial blood gas, but it does not have one universal normal range for every patient. Age, altitude, fraction of inspired oxygen, and disease affect the expected value.

Step 1: determine the pH direction

A pH below 7.35 indicates acidemia, meaning the blood is more acidic than expected. A pH above 7.45 indicates alkalemia, meaning the blood is more alkaline than expected.

A pH within the normal range does not automatically mean the blood gas is normal. Compensation or two opposing disorders can bring the pH toward normal.

Step 2: identify the primary process

Carbon dioxide acts like a respiratory acid. When the partial pressure of carbon dioxide, commonly written as PaCO2, rises, pH tends to fall. When PaCO2 falls, pH tends to rise.

Bicarbonate, written as HCO3-, acts as a base. When bicarbonate rises, pH tends to rise. When bicarbonate falls, pH tends to fall.

Pattern Typical relationship
Respiratory acidosisLow pH with high PaCO2
Respiratory alkalosisHigh pH with low PaCO2
Metabolic acidosisLow pH with low HCO3-
Metabolic alkalosisHigh pH with high HCO3-

Step 3: look for compensation

Compensation is the body's attempt to move pH back toward normal. If the primary problem is metabolic, the lungs can change ventilation and therefore carbon dioxide. If the primary problem is respiratory, the kidneys can change bicarbonate over time.

Traditional course terms such as uncompensated, partially compensated, and fully compensated are useful for basic pattern recognition. In this unit, however, you will also learn to check whether the amount of compensation is actually appropriate.

Important: Acid-base classification and oxygenation are related but separate. Correctly naming respiratory acidosis does not tell you whether oxygenation is adequate.

Clinical example

Arterial blood gas: pH 7.28, PaCO2 58 mmHg, HCO3- 25 mEq/L. The pH shows acidemia. PaCO2 is elevated in the acidifying direction. Bicarbonate remains near the course reference range. At a basic level, this is uncompensated respiratory acidosis. The next step is still to assess the patient: mental status, respiratory effort, oxygen support, likely cause, and trend.

Putting C1 together
Use the same sequence every time: context → pH → primary process → compensation → oxygenation → clinical correlation.
C2: Apply expected-compensation relationships, including Winter's formula, to distinguish an appropriate compensatory response from an additional primary acid-base disorder.

Compensation has limits

The body compensates in predictable ways. If the measured compensation is much different from what is expected, there may be a second primary acid-base disorder.

Winter's formula for metabolic acidosis

When the primary problem is metabolic acidosis, the lungs should respond by increasing ventilation and lowering carbon dioxide. Winter's formula estimates the expected PaCO2:

Expected PaCO2 ≈ 1.5 × HCO3- + 8 ± 2 mmHg

If the measured PaCO2 is higher than the expected range, the patient is retaining more carbon dioxide than compensation should allow. That suggests an additional respiratory acidosis.

If the measured PaCO2 is lower than expected, the patient is blowing off more carbon dioxide than compensation alone would explain. That suggests an additional respiratory alkalosis.

Why a “normal” carbon dioxide can be abnormal

Suppose a patient has severe metabolic acidosis. The lungs should lower PaCO2 substantially to compensate. A PaCO2 of 40 mmHg may sit inside the usual reference range, but it can be dangerously high for that specific patient because the expected compensated value should be much lower.

Other expected-compensation relationships

For metabolic alkalosis, a practical estimate is:

Expected PaCO2 ≈ 40 + 0.7 × (HCO3- − 24), ± about 2 mmHg

Respiratory disorders also have expected bicarbonate changes. These help distinguish acute from chronic conditions:

  • Acute respiratory acidosis: HCO3- rises about 1 mEq/L for each 10-mmHg rise in PaCO2 above 40.
  • Chronic respiratory acidosis: HCO3- rises about 3.5–4 mEq/L for each 10-mmHg rise in PaCO2 above 40.
  • Acute respiratory alkalosis: HCO3- falls about 2 mEq/L for each 10-mmHg fall in PaCO2 below 40.
  • Chronic respiratory alkalosis: HCO3- falls about 4–5 mEq/L for each 10-mmHg fall in PaCO2 below 40.

Clinical example: diabetic ketoacidosis with ventilatory failure

A patient with diabetic ketoacidosis has pH 7.12, PaCO2 40 mmHg, and HCO3- 12 mEq/L. Winter's formula predicts an expected PaCO2 near 26 mmHg, plus or minus 2. The measured PaCO2 of 40 is much higher than expected. If the patient is also somnolent and breathing shallowly, this supports mixed metabolic and respiratory acidosis and raises concern for ventilatory failure and airway protection.

Clinical reasoning: The formula does not tell you the diagnosis causing the disorder. It tells you whether the body's compensatory response is appropriate.
Putting C2 together
Compare the measured response with the expected response. A result outside the expected range suggests another primary acid-base process, timing issue, or unreliable data.
C3: Integrate pH, PaCO2, HCO3-, clinical presentation, and baseline status to recognize simple, compensated, mixed, acute, chronic, and acute-on-chronic patterns without relying on pH alone.

A normal pH can hide an abnormal patient

A pH near normal may occur because the body has compensated over time, or because two opposing disorders are present at the same time. Never stop your interpretation just because pH is between 7.35 and 7.45.

Acute versus chronic respiratory disorders

Acute means the disturbance developed recently, so the kidneys have had little time to change bicarbonate. Chronic means the disorder has been present long enough for more renal compensation to develop.

A patient with chronic carbon dioxide retention may have an elevated PaCO2, elevated bicarbonate, and a pH close to normal. That may represent a compensated baseline rather than a new emergency.

Acute-on-chronic

Acute-on-chronic means a patient with a chronic abnormal baseline develops a new worsening on top of it. Compare the current pH, PaCO2, bicarbonate, symptoms, work of breathing, mental status, and prior blood gases.

Use the patient to test the interpretation

An acid-base label is not complete until it fits the patient. A rising PaCO2 means something different in a comfortable patient whose prior values are similar than in a newly drowsy patient with shallow respirations.

Example: A patient has pH 7.37, PaCO2 60 mmHg, and HCO3- 34 mEq/L. This is not a normal blood gas. The elevated carbon dioxide and bicarbonate suggest chronic respiratory acidosis with compensation. You still need prior values and the current clinical condition to decide whether this is stable baseline or part of an acute-on-chronic problem.
Common mistake: Do not assume every high PaCO2 is acute respiratory failure. Baseline and compensation matter.
Putting C3 together
Interpret the pattern, expected compensation, timing, baseline, and patient condition together. Do not rely on pH alone.
C4: Identify preanalytic problems involving patient or sample identification, air bubbles, delay, venous admixture, line contamination, clotting, excess heparin, temperature, and missing oxygen-device information.

A blood gas can be wrong before it ever reaches the analyzer

Preanalytic errors are problems that occur before the sample is analyzed. They can change the result enough to mislead treatment decisions.

Patient and sample identification

Verify the patient using the required identifiers and accurately label the sample. A perfectly analyzed specimen from the wrong patient is still clinically dangerous.

Air bubbles

Air in the syringe can allow gases to move between the sample and room air. This can alter oxygen and carbon dioxide values, especially when the patient's blood values differ greatly from room-air conditions.

Delayed analysis

Blood cells remain metabolically active after collection. If analysis is delayed, oxygen can decrease and carbon dioxide can increase. Handle and transport the specimen according to current laboratory policy.

Venous admixture

Venous admixture in this context means the sample contains venous rather than truly arterial blood, or a mixture that is not what was intended. An accidentally venous sample usually has a lower oxygen tension and may have a somewhat higher carbon dioxide level than the arterial sample.

Line contamination

A sample drawn from an arterial line can be contaminated by flush solution or medication if the line is not handled correctly. Follow the approved procedure for clearing the line before obtaining the sample.

Clotting and excess heparin

Clots can interfere with analyzer function and produce unreliable values. Too much liquid heparin can dilute the sample and alter results. Use the correct collection device and mixing procedure.

Missing oxygen information

A partial pressure of oxygen cannot be interpreted safely without knowing the oxygen device, flow or fraction of inspired oxygen, and timing. A PaO2 of 60 mmHg on room air means something very different from the same PaO2 on a very high oxygen concentration.

Clinical reasoning: If the result does not fit the patient, first ask whether the sample could be wrong before changing therapy based on the number.
Putting C4 together
Recognize common collection and handling errors and explain how they can make arterial blood-gas interpretation unsafe.
C5: Recognize analyzer, calibration, cartridge, quality-control, transcription, and physiologic-consistency problems that require the result to be withheld, verified, or recollected before interpretation.

The analyzer can also produce questionable data

Analytic errors occur during or after analysis. Examples include calibration problems, expired or damaged cartridges, failed quality control, analyzer malfunction, transcription errors, or results that are physiologically impossible.

Quality-control flags matter

If the analyzer reports a failed quality-control check or another instrument warning, do not ignore the message because the result “looks reasonable.” Follow the device and facility procedure for verification.

Physiologic consistency

Blood-gas values must make physiologic sense together. A result that is mathematically or physiologically impossible should not be treated as real simply because the machine printed it.

If a point-of-care analyzer produces a result that is impossible or wildly inconsistent with the patient's condition, withhold interpretation, verify the analyzer and sample, and recollect if needed.

Transcription errors

A correct result can become incorrect when numbers are entered into the wrong field, decimal points are misplaced, or results are assigned to the wrong patient or time. Verify critical results before using them to make a major treatment decision.

Safety point: A physiologically impossible result should trigger verification—not immediate treatment based on the printed number.
Putting C5 together
Recognize when instrument or documentation problems make a result unreliable and know when interpretation should be delayed until verification or recollection occurs.
C6: Select pulse oximetry, co-oximetry, capnography, transcutaneous monitoring, or point-of-care blood-gas analysis according to the variable being measured and the limitations of each technology.

Choose the monitor that answers the question

Different monitoring technologies measure different things. A common clinical mistake is assuming that one monitor answers every question about oxygenation and ventilation.

Technology What it provides Important limitation
Pulse oximetryContinuous estimate of peripheral oxygen saturationDoes not directly measure PaCO2, pH, hemoglobin concentration, or abnormal hemoglobin species
Co-oximetryMeasures different hemoglobin species, such as oxyhemoglobin, carboxyhemoglobin, and methemoglobinRequires a blood sample and does not replace continuous bedside assessment
CapnographyContinuous waveform and end-tidal carbon dioxide related to ventilation and perfusionEnd-tidal carbon dioxide may differ from arterial PaCO2, especially with dead space or poor perfusion
Transcutaneous monitoringNoninvasive trend estimate of oxygen and/or carbon dioxide through heated skin sensorsLag time, skin perfusion, temperature, and sensor placement affect accuracy
Point-of-care blood-gas analysisRapid blood-gas results near the bedsideStill depends on correct sample collection, cartridge function, calibration, and quality control

Capnography and end-tidal carbon dioxide

Capnography displays carbon dioxide throughout exhalation as a waveform. The carbon dioxide measured near the end of exhalation is called end-tidal carbon dioxide, commonly written as ETCO2.

End-tidal carbon dioxide is useful for continuous ventilation monitoring, but it is not identical to arterial PaCO2. The difference can widen when dead space increases or pulmonary blood flow decreases.

Co-oximetry answers a different oxygen question

Standard pulse oximetry can be misleading in certain abnormal hemoglobin states. Co-oximetry directly measures several hemoglobin species and is therefore important when carbon monoxide exposure or methemoglobinemia is suspected.

Clinical reasoning: Ask what variable you need. Saturation? Hemoglobin species? Ventilation trend? Continuous carbon dioxide waveform? Blood-gas values? Choose the technology that directly answers that question.
Putting C6 together
Know what each monitoring technology measures, what it does not measure, and when a different method is needed.
C7: Analyze serial ABG, SpO2, ETCO2, respiratory rate, work of breathing, mental status, oxygen support, and device tolerance to determine improvement, deterioration, or inadequate response.

The trend matters more than one isolated value

Serial means repeated over time. A single blood gas is a snapshot. Serial blood gases and bedside findings show direction: improving, unchanged, or worsening.

Compare the whole patient

When evaluating response to oxygen or ventilatory support, compare more than the PaCO2. Look at:

  • pH and PaCO2 trend;
  • oxygenation and oxygen requirement;
  • respiratory rate and work of breathing;
  • mental status;
  • end-tidal carbon dioxide trend;
  • device tolerance and synchrony;
  • hemodynamic stability and overall clinical appearance.

Improvement does not require every value to become normal

A patient with chronic hypercapnia may improve clinically while PaCO2 remains above the generic reference range. If pH is improving, respiratory effort is decreasing, mental status is better, oxygenation is stable, and the patient tolerates support, the overall trend may show success.

Clinical example: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease on bilevel support

A patient with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease begins bilevel positive-airway-pressure support with respiratory distress and acidemia. On repeat assessment, the pH moves toward normal, PaCO2 decreases somewhat but remains elevated, respiratory rate falls, accessory-muscle use decreases, mental status improves, and the patient is tolerating the mask. The correct interpretation is improvement—not treatment failure simply because PaCO2 has not reached 40 mmHg.

Competency-exam thinking: Judge treatment response from the direction of the whole trend, not whether one value has returned to a textbook normal range.
Putting C7 together
Use repeated blood gases, oxygenation, carbon dioxide monitoring, work of breathing, mental status, oxygen support, and patient tolerance together to judge response.
C8: Interpret results against the patient's chronic or population-specific baseline, including pregnancy and chronic hypercapnia, and communicate the most appropriate verification, reassessment, or escalation step.

Normal depends on the patient and situation

Reference ranges are useful, but patients are not identical. A chronic condition or normal physiologic state can change the expected baseline.

Chronic hypercapnia

Some patients chronically retain carbon dioxide. Their PaCO2 may be elevated while the kidneys retain bicarbonate and keep pH closer to normal. Compare current values with prior blood gases, home respiratory support, mental status, and usual symptoms.

Pregnancy

Pregnancy normally increases ventilation. This produces a mild chronic respiratory alkalosis with a lower PaCO2 than in a typical nonpregnant adult. The kidneys compensate by lowering bicarbonate. A value that appears “low” compared with the standard adult PaCO2 range may therefore be normal for pregnancy.

Communicate what you know and what still needs verification

A strong respiratory report includes the blood-gas pattern, oxygen or ventilator conditions, important trend, baseline comparison, and the action needed next. That action may be repeat sampling, analyzer verification, co-oximetry, capnography, continued support, provider notification, or urgent escalation.

Example communication: “The patient has chronic compensated hypercapnia at baseline. Current blood gas shows a lower pH and higher PaCO2 than prior values, with increasing somnolence and work of breathing. This represents an acute change and requires immediate ventilatory reassessment.”
Clinical reasoning: Compare the patient with the patient's own baseline whenever possible, not only with a generic reference range.
Putting C8 together
Know when a chronic or population-specific baseline changes interpretation, and communicate whether the next step is verification, continued monitoring, reassessment, or escalation.

Week 2.1: Blood gases are clinical data, not just numbers

A strong arterial blood-gas interpretation includes sample validity, acid-base pattern, expected compensation, oxygenation, chronic baseline, and the patient's current condition.

For the Unit 2 Competency Exam, take the next step

The competency exam will require you to decide whether the data are valid, recognize mixed disorders, and judge response from the whole patient trend.

  • Use Winter's formula to recognize mixed metabolic and respiratory acidosis in a patient with severe metabolic acidosis and inadequate ventilatory compensation.
  • Recognize that somnolence and shallow breathing make an inappropriately high PaCO2 especially dangerous.
  • Withhold interpretation of a physiologically impossible point-of-care result until the sample and analyzer are verified.
  • Choose recollection, analyzer verification, co-oximetry, capnography, or another monitoring method when the original data cannot answer the clinical question reliably.
  • Compare serial pH, PaCO2, oxygenation, respiratory rate, work of breathing, mental status, and device tolerance after an intervention.
  • Recognize improvement in a chronically hypercapnic patient even when PaCO2 remains above the generic reference range.
  • Distinguish a stable chronic baseline from an acute-on-chronic deterioration.
  • Communicate the blood-gas pattern, supporting data, reliability concerns, trend, and next action clearly.
