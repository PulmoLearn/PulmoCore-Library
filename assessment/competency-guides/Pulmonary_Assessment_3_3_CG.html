Pulmonary Assessment • 3.3 Competency Guide

Respiratory Failure Assessment & Support Escalation

Learn how to classify respiratory failure, identify the mechanism that is failing, recognize fatigue and airway-protection loss, select the right level of oxygen or ventilatory support, evaluate whether noninvasive support is succeeding, and respond safely to ventilator alarms and life-threatening complications.

Before you begin

Respiratory failure is not defined by one number. It is a clinical syndrome in which the respiratory system cannot maintain adequate oxygenation, adequate carbon dioxide elimination, or both. The same PaCO2 can represent a stable chronic baseline in one patient and a dangerous acute change in another.

The key question is not simply, “What does the arterial blood gas say?” It is: Which respiratory function is failing, how quickly is it failing, is the airway still protected, how much support is required, and is the current treatment reversing the problem?

Big idea for Week 3.3: Use the sequence classify the failure → identify the dominant mechanism → assess severity and airway protection → select support → define success and failure criteria → reassess early → escalate immediately when the current support is no longer safe or effective.

C1: Classify hypoxemic, hypercapnic, mixed, acute, chronic, and acute-on-chronic respiratory failure using the ABG, oxygen support, symptoms, and baseline data.

Classify the function that is failing

Type 1 hypoxemic respiratory failure is primarily an oxygenation problem. In this course framework, a PaO2 below approximately 60 mmHg with a normal or low PaCO2 supports Type 1 failure. The patient may still be ventilating rapidly enough to keep carbon dioxide normal or low while oxygen transfer is failing.

Type 2 hypercapnic respiratory failure is primarily a ventilation problem. Elevated PaCO2 with respiratory acidemia indicates inadequate alveolar ventilation. For example, PaCO2 68 mmHg with pH 7.28 strongly supports acute hypercapnic failure.

Mixed respiratory failure means oxygenation and ventilation are failing together. Severe hypoxemia plus hypercapnia and acidemia is especially high risk.

Acute, chronic, and acute-on-chronic

In chronic carbon dioxide retention, renal bicarbonate retention often brings the pH closer to normal. A patient may have PaCO2 60 mmHg, elevated HCO3-, and pH near 7.37 as a chronic compensated baseline.

A new rise in PaCO2 above that baseline with a falling pH indicates acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure. The current number must be compared with prior arterial blood gases, usual oxygen or noninvasive ventilation use, usual mental status, and baseline functional capacity.

The oxygen setting changes severity interpretation

PaO2 60 mmHg on room air is not equivalent to PaO2 60 mmHg on FiO2 0.80. Always interpret oxygenation with the device, flow or FiO2, and timing of the sample.

Course case: chronic baseline versus acute change

Baseline: pH 7.37, PaCO2 60 mmHg, PaO2 64 mmHg, HCO3- 35 mEq/L. Current: pH 7.28, PaCO2 78 mmHg, PaO2 54 mmHg, HCO3- 36 mEq/L with accessory-muscle use. The nearly unchanged bicarbonate cannot compensate for the acute rise in PaCO2. This supports acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure with worsening hypoxemia.

Clinical reasoning: Compare the patient with the patient's own baseline, not only with a reference range.
Putting C1 together
State whether oxygenation, ventilation, or both are failing; then state whether the problem is acute, chronic, or acute-on-chronic.
C2: Differentiate impaired gas exchange, intrapulmonary shunt, dead space, hypoventilation, and airway obstruction from integrated assessment and monitoring findings.

Why is gas exchange failing?

Two patients can have the same SpO2 and require completely different next steps. Mechanism helps explain why.

Intrapulmonary shunt: blood without ventilation

Shunt occurs when blood perfuses alveoli that are not ventilated. Collapsed, fluid-filled, or completely consolidated alveoli cannot load oxygen effectively. Atelectasis, dense pneumonia, severe pulmonary edema, and acute respiratory distress syndrome can produce shunt physiology.

True shunt often responds incompletely to oxygen alone because increasing FiO2 cannot ventilate an alveolus that is closed or flooded. Alveolar recruitment with positive end-expiratory pressure or CPAP, plus treatment of the underlying cause, may be needed.

Dead space: ventilation without perfusion

Dead space is the opposite mismatch: alveoli receive ventilation but little or no blood flow. Pulmonary embolism is the classic acute example.

Because less carbon dioxide is delivered by blood to ventilated alveoli, ETCO2 may fall. The difference between arterial PaCO2 and ETCO2 can widen. Low ETCO2 in shock or pulmonary embolism should not automatically be interpreted as simple hyperventilation.

Hypoventilation: the ventilatory pump or drive is failing

Opioids, sedatives, central nervous system injury, obesity hypoventilation, chest-wall restriction, neuromuscular weakness, and respiratory-muscle fatigue can reduce alveolar ventilation. PaCO2 rises, and pH falls if renal compensation has not had time to occur.

Airway obstruction: expiratory flow is limited

Asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, mucus, a foreign body, or an obstructed artificial airway can cause prolonged exhalation, wheeze, air trapping, increased work of breathing, and rising PaCO2.

Severe obstruction may produce a quiet or silent chest because airflow is so limited that little sound is generated. Less wheezing is not always improvement.

Shark-fin capnography

A sloped expiratory upstroke and plateau—the classic shark-fin shape—supports expiratory airflow obstruction and uneven, prolonged emptying. Interpret the waveform with breath sounds, expiratory time, ETCO2 trend, respiratory rate, and effort.

Mechanisms can coexist

A patient with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and pneumonia may simultaneously have obstruction, V/Q mismatch, shunt, fatigue, and hypoventilation. Stabilize the most dangerous failure first while continuing to identify and treat contributing mechanisms.

Mechanism memory: Shunt = blood without air. Dead space = air without blood. Hypoventilation = not enough effective ventilation. Obstruction = gas cannot exit efficiently.

Identify the dominant mechanism and choose the follow-up assessment that directly evaluates it.

SpO2 can look good while ventilation fails

Supplemental oxygen can maintain a normal SpO2 while PaCO2 rises to dangerous levels. Pulse oximetry does not measure ventilation, airway protection, hemoglobin concentration, cardiac output, or tissue oxygen utilization.

ETCO2 does not always equal PaCO2

In a healthy patient, ETCO2 is often slightly lower than PaCO2. The difference can widen when dead space increases, pulmonary perfusion falls, severe V/Q inequality develops, or sampling is poor.

A stable saturation on increasing support is deterioration

SpO2 94% on room air, 2 L/min, a nonrebreather mask, CPAP, or FiO2 1.0 represents very different severity. Always document the support level required to create the monitored value.

Validate conflicting data

If a result does not match the patient, check oximeter signal and perfusion, capnography sampling and circuit integrity, ABG timing and sample quality, patient effort during mechanics, and whether support settings changed between measurements.

Trend statement: “SpO2 increased from 88% to 93%, but FiO2 doubled, PaCO2 rose, pH fell, and the patient became drowsy” describes deterioration—not improvement.
Putting C3 together
Interpret every value with its device, timing, waveform or sample quality, baseline, and direction of change.
C4: Recognize early compensatory signs, respiratory-muscle fatigue, late pre-arrest findings, and the clinical meaning of a decreasing respiratory rate in a deteriorating patient.

A patient can look quieter because they are exhausted

Early compensation

Tachypnea, tachycardia, anxiety, restlessness, nasal flaring, retractions, accessory-muscle use, tripod positioning, diaphoresis, and inability to speak full sentences show significant respiratory stress while the patient may still be maintaining ventilation.

Fatigue

Respiratory muscles cannot sustain extreme work forever. Warning signs include shallower breaths, a weaker cough, reduced chest movement, decreasing air entry, paradoxical movement, decreasing speech strength, and a respiratory rate that begins to fall while gas exchange and mental status worsen.

A decreasing respiratory rate can be dangerous

If a patient was breathing 36/min with marked distress and later falls to 10/min while becoming drowsy with rising PaCO2, that is not improvement. It may indicate exhaustion and impending arrest.

Late pre-arrest findings

Obtundation, bradycardia, apnea or irregular respirations, cyanosis despite support, inability to handle secretions, and loss of airway protection are ominous findings that require immediate stabilization and airway escalation.

Severe obstruction may become quieter

A patient with severe asthma who goes from loud wheezing to minimal breath sounds while becoming fatigued may be moving less air, not improving.

Emergency priority: Bradycardia, apnea, rapidly declining consciousness, or inability to protect the airway should trigger immediate emergency support. Do not wait for a routine ABG.
Putting C4 together
Judge respiratory rate together with depth, effort, airflow, cough strength, gas exchange, and consciousness.
C5: Measure or interpret maximum inspiratory pressure in context and recognize a severely weak or rapidly declining MIP as evidence of impending ventilatory failure.

Maximum inspiratory pressure measures inspiratory-muscle force

Maximum inspiratory pressure, or MIP, measures the negative pressure a patient can generate during a maximal inspiratory effort against an occluded airway.

Because inspiration creates negative pressure, a more negative MIP represents stronger inspiratory muscles. A value becoming less negative represents weakness.

Course threshold

In this course framework, a MIP less negative than approximately -20 cm H2O is severely weak and raises concern for impending ventilatory failure. A MIP of -15 cm H2O is a high-risk value.

Trend is often more important than one value

A decline from -38 to -22 to -14 cm H2O over several hours demonstrates rapidly shrinking reserve. If PaCO2 is also rising, cough is weakening, and speech is becoming softer, controlled airway management may be safer than waiting for sudden exhaustion.

Neuromuscular failure can be deceptively quiet

Patients with Guillain-Barré syndrome, myasthenic crisis, or high spinal cord injury may not show dramatic wheezing or crackles. The problem is pump weakness. Serial respiratory mechanics, cough effectiveness, bulbar function, secretion clearance, and PaCO2 trend are crucial.

Validate the technique

Poor seal, pain, misunderstanding, submaximal effort, fatigue, equipment problems, or leak around an artificial airway can make MIP appear falsely weak. Repeat or verify when the patient is stable enough—but do not dismiss a worsening value that matches clinical deterioration.

Clinical reasoning: One MIP does not independently order intubation. Trend it with PaCO2, cough, vital capacity when available, bulbar function, secretion burden, mental status, and trajectory.
Putting C5 together
More negative = stronger. Less negative = weaker. Rapid decline plus worsening ventilation is especially concerning.
C6: Assess airway protection, cooperation, secretion management, emesis or aspiration risk, facial-interface suitability, and hemodynamic stability before selecting noninvasive support.

An acceptable ABG does not automatically make NIV safe

Noninvasive ventilation, or NIV, uses a mask or similar interface instead of an endotracheal tube. It can be highly effective when the patient can safely participate.

Airway protection

Assess level of consciousness, gag and cough effectiveness, ability to manage secretions, swallowing or bulbar function, and the ability to remove the mask if needed.

Vomiting and aspiration risk

A tight mask and positive pressure can be dangerous in a patient who is actively vomiting or cannot protect the airway. Emesis during NIV is a major failure condition because aspiration risk rises sharply.

Cooperation and interface tolerance

The patient must be able to follow commands and tolerate the interface. Severe agitation, facial trauma, burns, anatomy preventing an adequate seal, or inability to cooperate may make NIV ineffective or unsafe.

Secretions

NIV does not provide an artificial airway for suctioning. A patient with copious unmanageable secretions and a weak cough may be a poor candidate even if the blood gas would otherwise suggest bilevel support.

Hemodynamic stability

Positive pressure can reduce venous return and worsen blood pressure in susceptible patients. Severe or rapidly worsening hemodynamic instability may make mask-based support unsafe.

Major contraindications or failure risks

  • inability to protect the airway;
  • active vomiting;
  • apnea or cardiac arrest;
  • severe or rapidly worsening altered consciousness;
  • unmanageable secretions;
  • facial trauma or anatomy preventing a seal;
  • severe hemodynamic instability;
  • inability to cooperate.
Airway rule: Airway protection can mandate intubation even before the ABG becomes dramatically abnormal.
Putting C6 together
Before NIV, assess not only the gas values but whether the patient can safely live behind the mask.
C7: Distinguish an oxygenation problem from a ventilation problem and an airway-protection problem before choosing supplemental oxygen, CPAP, bilevel support, or invasive ventilation.

Oxygen alone

An alert patient with pH 7.42, PaCO2 40 mmHg, PaO2 58 mmHg, adequate spontaneous ventilation, and intact airway protection may initially require supplemental oxygen with close reassessment.

CPAP

Continuous positive airway pressure, or CPAP, provides one continuous level of positive pressure. It is useful when the main problem is oxygenation and alveolar recruitment while spontaneous ventilation remains adequate. Cardiogenic pulmonary edema with hypoxemia but normal pH and PaCO2 is a classic course example.

Bilevel positive-airway pressure

Bilevel support provides a higher inspiratory positive airway pressure, or IPAP, and a lower expiratory positive airway pressure, or EPAP. The pressure difference assists tidal ventilation, while EPAP helps maintain alveolar recruitment and oxygenation.

A cooperative patient with pH 7.29 and PaCO2 68 mmHg who can protect the airway may be a reasonable initial bilevel-support candidate.

Invasive ventilation

An endotracheal tube secures the airway and allows mechanical ventilation when the patient cannot safely maintain the airway, cannot ventilate adequately despite support, has apnea or arrest, develops severe fatigue, or fails NIV.

Support-selection rule: Oxygen raises inspired oxygen. CPAP primarily recruits alveoli. Bilevel support adds inspiratory assistance. Invasive ventilation secures the airway and can replace inadequate breathing.
Putting C7 together
Name the failing function before selecting the device.
C8: Select the initial support level that matches the patient condition while recognizing contraindications, limits, and escalation criteria.

Use the least invasive support that can reliably meet the need

Isolated hypoxemia

If ventilation is intact, pH and PaCO2 are normal, the patient is alert, and airway protection is intact, supplemental oxygen may be appropriate initially.

Recruitment-focused hypoxemia

When oxygenation failure is driven by alveolar collapse or fluid with preserved spontaneous ventilation, CPAP may add useful recruitment pressure.

Moderate acute hypercapnic failure

An alert, cooperative patient with moderate acute hypercapnic acidosis, intact cough, manageable secretions, stable hemodynamics, and preserved airway protection may receive a closely monitored bilevel trial.

Course pH warning threshold

A pH below approximately 7.25 is a strong warning for severe acute ventilatory failure in this course. It does not act alone. Airway protection, rate of deterioration, hemodynamics, reversibility, mental status, and local protocol determine whether a monitored NIV trial is safe or whether invasive airway management should be immediate.

Situations that favor invasive airway management

  • apnea or arrest;
  • obtundation or rapidly worsening mental status;
  • vomiting or high aspiration risk with loss of airway protection;
  • inability to manage secretions;
  • severe progressive fatigue;
  • refractory gas-exchange failure despite noninvasive support;
  • severe hemodynamic instability;
  • facial condition preventing a usable noninvasive interface.
Clinical reasoning: “Least invasive” does not mean “keep trying the mask.” It means choose the least invasive support that can actually meet the patient's need safely.
Putting C8 together
Match support intensity to gas exchange, mechanics, airway protection, hemodynamics, cooperation, and trajectory.
C9: Reassess the patient after support is initiated using symptoms, work of breathing, mental status, respiratory rate, SpO2 and oxygen need, ETCO2, ABG trend, tolerance, and hemodynamics.

Decide what success should look like before treatment starts

Goals of a successful NIV trial

  • less work of breathing;
  • improved alertness or stable mental status;
  • lower respiratory rate without evidence of fatigue;
  • improving pH;
  • falling PaCO2 in hypercapnic failure;
  • improved oxygenation on stable or decreasing FiO2;
  • stable hemodynamics;
  • acceptable interface tolerance;
  • effective secretion management and preserved airway protection.

Failure criteria

  • worsening pH or rising PaCO2;
  • refractory hypoxemia;
  • increasing oxygen requirement;
  • progressive fatigue or falling respiratory rate with shallow breathing;
  • agitation progressing to obtundation;
  • apnea;
  • vomiting or aspiration risk;
  • inability to clear secretions;
  • worsening hemodynamic instability.

Reassessment timing depends on severity

A severely ill patient starting NIV requires immediate and repeated bedside reassessment. Do not assume the next meaningful check can wait several hours. Clinical change can occur within minutes.

One improving value does not outweigh multiple worsening values

If SpO2 rises from 88% to 93% but the pH falls, PaCO2 rises, respiratory rate drops because of fatigue, and mental status worsens, the patient is failing support.

Reassessment principle: Trends determine whether the current level of support remains appropriate.
Putting C9 together
Define success and failure criteria up front, then trend the whole patient—not just the saturation.
C10: Recognize failure of a noninvasive-support trial and prioritize invasive airway management when gas exchange worsens, fatigue progresses, or airway protection is lost.

An appropriate NIV trial can later become unsafe

Starting NIV can be the correct decision at 0900 and stopping it for intubation can also be the correct decision at 0945. The patient's condition changed; therefore the support decision must change.

Course failure case

Initial bilevel-support ABG: pH 7.31, PaCO2 65 mmHg. Two hours later: pH 7.19, PaCO2 82 mmHg. The patient is now obtunded, respiratory rate 8/min, and has vomited into the mask.

Every major trend is worsening:

  • Acidemia: pH has fallen.
  • Ventilation: PaCO2 has risen.
  • Respiratory drive: respiratory rate has fallen to 8/min.
  • Consciousness: the patient is obtunded.
  • Airway protection: vomiting into the mask creates immediate aspiration risk.

The priority is immediate invasive airway management. Continuing bilevel support longer, simply increasing pressure, or switching to low-flow oxygen fails to correct the loss of airway protection and progressive ventilatory failure.

Do not let the ABG become the only intubation trigger

Airway protection can fail before extreme hypercapnia develops. Obtundation, active emesis, apnea, absent protective reflexes, or inability to manage secretions may independently require invasive airway control.

Prepare before collapse when possible

Progressive muscle weakness, steadily worsening gas exchange, declining consciousness, and NIV failure should prompt preparation for controlled airway management rather than waiting for respiratory arrest.

Safety priority: Do not allow an initially reasonable NIV plan to delay intubation after objective failure or airway-protection loss develops.
Putting C10 together
Recognize when noninvasive support has crossed from helpful to unsafe and escalate without delay.
C11: Assess a mechanically ventilated patient and circuit when an alarm occurs, distinguish common high- and low-pressure causes, and recognize a pressure-related emergency such as tension pneumothorax.

The patient comes before the alarm setting

When a ventilator alarm sounds, first assess the patient: chest rise, breath sounds, SpO2, ETCO2, color, mental status, blood pressure, heart rate, and evidence of distress. Then evaluate the circuit and ventilator.

Foundational ventilator variables

FiO2 controls inspired oxygen concentration. Respiratory rate contributes to minute ventilation. Tidal volume or inspiratory pressure determines the breath size or pressure assistance depending on mode. Positive end-expiratory pressure, or PEEP, helps maintain alveolar recruitment and oxygenation.

High-pressure alarm: resistance or compliance problem

Common causes include:

  • secretions in the artificial airway;
  • kinked tubing;
  • patient biting the endotracheal tube;
  • bronchospasm;
  • reduced lung or chest-wall compliance;
  • water or obstruction in the circuit;
  • pneumothorax.

Low-pressure alarm: loss of delivered pressure

Common causes include circuit disconnection, leak, cuff problem, loose connection, or accidental extubation.

Stable high pressure versus emergency high pressure

A high-pressure alarm with stable blood pressure, preserved oxygenation, coarse breath sounds, and visible secretions may point toward suctioning or circuit assessment.

A sudden high-pressure alarm with severe desaturation, hypotension, and unilateral breath-sound loss is a different situation. Suspect tension pneumothorax or another catastrophic obstruction.

Suspected tension pneumothorax

Positive-pressure ventilation can worsen a pneumothorax into a tension physiology. The combination of abrupt high pressure, unilateral breath-sound loss, falling SpO2, and hemodynamic collapse requires emergency action.

Do not solve this by raising the high-pressure alarm limit. Do not wait for routine imaging in an unstable classic pattern. Activate emergency response, support oxygenation and ventilation, and prepare for definitive decompression according to the team and protocol.

Backup resources

Know where a functional bag-valve device, oxygen source, battery backup, and emergency power are located. If ventilator function or the circuit cannot be trusted and the patient requires immediate ventilation, manual ventilation with appropriate oxygen may be necessary while the problem is corrected.

Major ventilator hazards

Mechanical ventilation can contribute to barotrauma, volutrauma, hemodynamic compromise, ventilator-associated pneumonia, artificial-airway injury, sedation complications, and equipment failure. Patient-safety and infection-prevention practices remain part of ventilator assessment.

Emergency rule: Sudden high pressure + unilateral absent breath sounds + hypotension + severe desaturation is a patient emergency, not an alarm-adjustment problem.
Putting C11 together
Assess the patient first, then circuit and ventilator; distinguish resistance/leak problems from pressure-related cardiopulmonary emergencies.
C12: Communicate respiratory-failure classification, supporting data, urgency, support recommendation, response to treatment, and emergency escalation using concise closed-loop communication.

Say what is failing, how urgent it is, and what needs to happen

A useful respiratory-failure report includes

  • respiratory-failure type and whether it is acute, chronic, or acute-on-chronic;
  • current oxygen or ventilatory support;
  • key ABG and ETCO2 trends;
  • work of breathing and respiratory-rate trajectory;
  • mental status and airway-protection status;
  • hemodynamics;
  • relevant imaging or mechanism clues;
  • response or failure of the current treatment;
  • specific support or emergency action recommended;
  • planned reassessment or confirmation that escalation roles are assigned.

Example: acute-on-chronic failure

“The patient has acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure with worsening hypoxemia. Baseline PaCO2 is 60 with pH 7.37; current PaCO2 is 78 with pH 7.28 and increased accessory-muscle use. The patient remains alert and can protect the airway. Recommend immediate evaluation for bilevel ventilatory support with early repeat assessment of pH, PaCO2, work of breathing, mental status, and tolerance.”

Example: failing NIV

“NIV has failed. pH fell from 7.31 to 7.19, PaCO2 rose from 65 to 82, respiratory rate is now 8, the patient is obtunded, and emesis is present in the mask. The airway is no longer protected. Activate immediate invasive airway management.”

Closed-loop communication

Closed-loop communication means the sender states the message or assignment, the receiver confirms it, and the sender verifies that the correct message was received. During emergencies, this reduces ambiguity about who is preparing equipment, supporting ventilation, calling the provider, or activating emergency response.

Example: ventilator emergency

“Sudden high-pressure alarm with SpO2 72%, hypotension, and absent left breath sounds. Suspect tension pneumothorax. I am supporting ventilation and oxygenation now. Activate the emergency team and prepare for immediate decompression.”
Communication rule: State the change, objective evidence, urgency, action already taken, and the help needed next.
Putting C12 together
Communicate enough evidence that the receiving clinician understands both why the patient is failing and why your recommended support level is appropriate.

Week 3.3: Support the function that is failing

Respiratory failure assessment moves from classification to action. Determine whether the patient is failing oxygenation, ventilation, airway protection, or more than one function. Then select the least invasive support that can safely meet the need and reassess early enough to recognize failure before arrest occurs.

baseline → failure type → mechanism → severity → airway protection → support choice → explicit goals/failure criteria → serial reassessment → escalation

Before the weekly quiz, you should be able to...

  • define Type 1 hypoxemic respiratory failure as primarily an oxygenation problem with PaO2 below approximately 60 mmHg and PaCO2 normal or low in the course framework;
  • recognize acute Type 2 hypercapnic respiratory failure from elevated PaCO2 with respiratory acidemia;
  • identify mixed respiratory failure when severe hypoxemia and hypercapnia occur together;
  • distinguish acute, chronic, and acute-on-chronic hypercapnia using pH, bicarbonate, prior ABG, oxygen support, and patient baseline;
  • define intrapulmonary shunt as perfusion of nonventilated alveoli and explain why recruitment may be needed;
  • identify pulmonary embolism as a classic cause of physiologic dead space;
  • recognize a shark-fin capnogram as a pattern of expiratory airflow obstruction;
  • explain why low ETCO2 may reflect low pulmonary perfusion rather than hyperventilation;
  • recognize obtundation, bradycardia, apnea, and airway-protection failure as late ominous signs;
  • interpret MIP -15 cm H2O as severe inspiratory-muscle weakness in the course framework;
  • identify supplemental oxygen for isolated hypoxemia with preserved ventilation and airway protection;
  • identify CPAP for recruitment-focused oxygenation failure when spontaneous ventilation is adequate;
  • identify an alert, cooperative patient with moderate acute hypercapnic acidosis and intact airway protection as a possible bilevel-support candidate;
  • recognize vomiting, obtundation, apnea, unmanageable secretions, or loss of airway protection as reasons NIV may be unsafe;
  • recognize common high-pressure and low-pressure ventilator-alarm causes; and
  • recognize sudden high pressure, unilateral breath-sound loss, hypotension, and desaturation as a tension-pneumothorax emergency pattern.

For the Unit 3 Competency Exam, take the next step

  • Compare a chronic hypercapnic baseline with current ABG values and bedside findings to identify acute-on-chronic respiratory failure rather than treating every elevated PaCO2 as equivalent.
  • Evaluate an alert patient with acute-on-chronic hypercapnic failure for noninvasive ventilatory support when airway protection, secretion handling, cooperation, and hemodynamics permit.
  • Define explicit response goals and failure criteria when beginning NIV.
  • Recognize NIV failure from worsening acidemia, rising PaCO2, falling respiratory rate with fatigue, obtundation, emesis, refractory hypoxemia, or hemodynamic deterioration.
  • Abandon a previously appropriate NIV trial when airway protection is lost and prioritize immediate invasive airway management.
  • Assess the patient before adjusting a ventilator alarm.
  • Distinguish stable high airway pressure caused by secretions or resistance from a high-pressure alarm associated with cardiopulmonary collapse.
  • Recognize suspected tension pneumothorax from sudden high pressure, severe desaturation, hypotension, and unilateral breath-sound loss.
  • Activate emergency response and support oxygenation and ventilation without waiting for routine imaging in an unstable tension-pneumothorax pattern.
  • Communicate respiratory-failure type, objective trend, airway status, current support, response, urgency, and the next recommended action using closed-loop communication.
National Board for Respiratory Care connection: The Respiratory Therapy Examination expects you to integrate blood gases, noninvasive monitoring, respiratory mechanics, imaging, physical assessment, and serial trends to classify respiratory failure, choose oxygen or ventilatory support, recognize failure of noninvasive support, troubleshoot mechanical ventilation, and respond to high-risk cardiopulmonary deterioration.
