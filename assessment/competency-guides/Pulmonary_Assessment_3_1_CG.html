Pulmonary Assessment • 3.1 Competency Guide

Diagnostic Tests, Respiratory Infections & Infection Prevention

Learn how respiratory therapists decide which diagnostic test is useful, collect and judge respiratory specimens, interpret laboratory and microbiology evidence, recognize infection patterns, support bronchoscopy and pleural procedures, and prevent healthcare-associated respiratory infection.

Before you begin

Infection assessment is rarely solved by one laboratory value or one test. A patient may have pneumonia with a normal white blood cell count. A culture may grow an organism that represents colonization instead of the cause of disease. A positive tuberculosis screening test may reflect prior infection rather than active contagious pulmonary tuberculosis. A specimen may look official because it has a laboratory label but still be clinically useless because it contains only saliva.

Respiratory therapists often stand at the point where bedside assessment, specimen collection, oxygenation change, secretion management, mechanical ventilation, imaging, and infection prevention come together. That makes test selection and data validation part of respiratory clinical judgment.

Big idea for Week 3.1: Follow the chain clinical question → best test or procedure → safe preparation → valid specimen or study → preliminary versus final result → patient correlation → prevention → next action.

C1: Select sputum testing, tuberculosis screening or diagnostic evaluation, bronchoscopy, thoracentesis, pleurodesis, CBC, chemistry, electrolyte, and microbiology studies according to the clinical question.

Start by naming the question you need answered

Diagnostic reasoning is stronger when you first state the problem in plain language. Are you trying to identify an organism? Determine whether a patient has been exposed to tuberculosis? Directly inspect an airway? Remove a mucus plug? Determine why pleural fluid is present? Relieve a large pleural effusion? Prevent recurrent pleural disease? Evaluate whether infection, anemia, renal dysfunction, or electrolyte disturbance is contributing to respiratory decline?

The test follows the question. Choosing an invasive procedure just because it provides “more information” can expose the patient to unnecessary risk without answering the actual problem.

Suspected bacterial lower respiratory infection

If bacterial pneumonia or another lower respiratory infection is suspected and the patient can provide a clinically usable lower-airway specimen, sputum Gram stain and culture can help. The Gram stain provides rapid preliminary information about bacterial class and morphology. Culture attempts to identify the organism. Susceptibility testing then estimates which antimicrobial drugs are likely to be active against that organism.

A productive cough alone does not automatically mean sputum testing is necessary. Ask whether identifying the organism could influence treatment and whether a true lower-airway specimen can actually be collected.

Tuberculosis: screening is not the same as diagnosing active disease

A tuberculin skin test or interferon-gamma release assay, commonly called an IGRA, evaluates immune sensitization to Mycobacterium tuberculosis. A positive screening test means the immune system has encountered tuberculosis antigens. It does not by itself prove that the patient currently has contagious pulmonary tuberculosis.

When active pulmonary tuberculosis is suspected from symptoms, exposure history, imaging, or other findings, the priorities change. Prompt airborne precautions are needed, along with diagnostic evaluation such as chest imaging and respiratory specimens for acid-fast bacillus testing and/or molecular testing according to policy.

Bronchoscopy: look directly inside the airway

Bronchoscopy allows a clinician to pass a flexible or rigid scope into the airway. Diagnostic bronchoscopy may be used to directly visualize the tracheobronchial tree, inspect lesions, and obtain bronchial washings, brushings, bronchoalveolar lavage specimens, or biopsies.

Bronchoscopy can also be therapeutic. It may remove a mucus plug, blood clot, foreign body, or retained secretion when less invasive airway-clearance methods are insufficient.

A sudden lobar collapse with focal diminished breath sounds, retained secretions, ineffective cough, and persistent hypoxemia suggests an airway-lumen problem. Bronchoscopy directly addresses the airway. Thoracentesis does not.

Thoracentesis: evaluate or remove pleural fluid

Thoracentesis places a needle or catheter into the pleural space to obtain or remove fluid. The procedure may be diagnostic, therapeutic, or both. Pleural fluid can be analyzed for cellular, biochemical, microbiologic, and malignant features. Removing a large effusion may also improve dyspnea and lung expansion.

Pleurodesis: reduce recurrence

Pleurodesis intentionally causes the pleural surfaces to adhere so that the potential pleural space is reduced. It may be considered in selected patients with recurrent pneumothorax or recurrent pleural effusion. It is not the same as draining the fluid or air that is already present.

CBC, chemistry, and electrolytes answer different physiologic questions

A complete blood count, commonly shortened to CBC, includes the white blood cell count and differential, hemoglobin, hematocrit, and platelet count. White blood cell information may support infection or inflammation. Hemoglobin and hematocrit help assess anemia and oxygen-carrying capacity. Platelets may influence bleeding risk before an invasive procedure.

Chemistry and electrolyte studies add another layer. Blood urea nitrogen and creatinine help assess kidney function and may influence medication or procedure risk. Glucose disturbances can contribute to altered mental status. Sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus abnormalities can contribute to weakness, dysrhythmia, confusion, or impaired respiratory-muscle performance.

Common mistake: The most invasive test is not automatically the best test. Use the least invasive method that safely answers the clinical question.
Putting C1 together
Name the question first. Then choose the test, specimen, or procedure that can actually answer it and identify the major safety concern before proceeding.
C2: Distinguish screening, diagnostic, therapeutic, and recurrence-prevention procedures and explain what information or outcome each provides.

Four different purposes

Screening identifies people who may need further evaluation. It does not necessarily establish active disease. Tuberculosis screening is a good example.

Diagnostic testing helps determine what problem is present. A sputum culture, pleural-fluid study, bronchoscopy specimen, or chest image may contribute diagnostic evidence.

Therapeutic procedures treat a current problem. Removing a mucus plug during bronchoscopy or draining symptomatic pleural fluid during thoracentesis are examples.

Recurrence-prevention procedures attempt to reduce the chance that the problem will return. Pleurodesis is the key course example.

Why this distinction matters

If a patient has a large symptomatic pleural effusion today, pleurodesis is not a substitute for evaluating or draining the current fluid when drainage is clinically indicated. Likewise, a positive tuberculosis screen does not replace diagnostic evaluation when active pulmonary disease is suspected.

Clinical reasoning: Before choosing a procedure, say what you expect it to accomplish: detect risk, identify disease, treat a current problem, or prevent recurrence.
Putting C2 together
Explain the purpose of the procedure and the result or outcome you expect from it.
C3: Prepare the patient and equipment for expectorated sputum collection using correct identification, hand hygiene, explanation, oral preparation, deep-cough coaching, labeling, containment, and transport.

Every collection step protects the result

Sputum collection is not “hand the patient a cup.” Each step protects either patient identification, specimen quality, infection prevention, or laboratory reliability.

  1. Verify the order. Confirm which test is requested, what specimen source is expected, and whether timing matters.
  2. Identify the patient using two approved identifiers. Never rely only on room number.
  3. Perform hand hygiene and apply required PPE. Standard precautions apply to all respiratory specimens. Add transmission-based precautions when indicated.
  4. Explain what sputum means. Tell the patient that material from deep in the chest is needed, not saliva from the mouth.
  5. Prepare the mouth. Plain water is typically used for oral rinsing when appropriate. Antiseptic mouthwash may interfere with organism recovery.
  6. Position the patient upright when possible. This supports deep breathing and cough effectiveness.
  7. Coach several deep breaths followed by a deep cough. The goal is to move lower-airway secretions upward.
  8. Collect directly into the sterile container. Avoid touching the inside of the lid or cup.
  9. Label immediately at the bedside. Include required patient identifiers, date/time, source, and collector information.
  10. Close, bag, and transport promptly. Use the approved leak-resistant biohazard transport process.

Why timing matters

When feasible, specimens are often collected before antimicrobial therapy begins because antibiotics may reduce organism recovery. However, severe infection changes the priority. Good specimen collection and urgent empiric treatment should occur rapidly and in parallel; testing should not create an unsafe delay in treatment.

What if the patient is intubated?

An intubated patient cannot provide a meaningful expectorated sputum sample through the mouth. A tracheal aspirate or another ordered lower-airway sample should be collected using the approved technique and labeled with the actual source.

Why this matters: A wrong-patient label or contaminated oral sample can generate a perfectly accurate laboratory analysis of the wrong material.
Putting C3 together
Know the collection sequence and the clinical reason behind each step.
C4: Evaluate whether a respiratory specimen represents the lower airway and determine when saliva, low volume, mislabeling, delay, contamination, or improper storage requires rejection or recollection.

Can this specimen answer the clinical question?

A specimen is useful only if it represents the intended anatomical source and reaches the laboratory without identification or handling problems.

Appearance gives the first clue

True sputum is generally thick, mucoid, or purulent material produced by a deep cough. Clear, watery material from the mouth is likely saliva. Saliva contains oral flora and may obscure the lower-airway organism you are trying to identify.

For routine bacterial culture, approximately 2–10 mL is commonly adequate, but the laboratory's own criteria control. A large cup of saliva is still a poor specimen.

Microscopic quality indicators

A specimen with many squamous epithelial cells often reflects contamination with oral material. Numerous neutrophils or inflammatory cells may support a lower-airway inflammatory process. The laboratory combines these findings with the specimen source and its own acceptance criteria.

Labeling errors are patient-safety errors

An unlabeled or mislabeled specimen should not simply be relabeled later from memory. Follow laboratory and facility policy. Recollection is often safer than attaching a patient identity to a specimen that cannot be verified.

Delay and temperature can change organism recovery

Microorganisms can die, multiply, or become overgrown by contaminating flora when transport is delayed or storage is improper. Follow the required transport time and temperature for the ordered test.

Colonization versus infection

Colonization means microorganisms are present without causing active tissue infection. This is especially important in patients with chronic airways, tracheostomies, or endotracheal tubes. Growth from a respiratory specimen does not automatically prove pneumonia.

Ask whether the organism fits the specimen quality, Gram stain, imaging, secretions, oxygen requirement, systemic findings, and patient trajectory.

Unit-exam case: In an intubated patient with suspected VAP, 0.5 mL of clear saliva collected from the mouth is invalid because it is the wrong source and does not represent the lower airway.
Putting C4 together
Evaluate source, appearance, microscopic quality, volume, identification, containment, transport, storage, and clinical fit before trusting the result.
C5: Interpret preliminary Gram-stain findings, organism morphology, culture identification, and antimicrobial sensitivity while distinguishing preliminary information from a definitive result.

Microbiology becomes more specific over time

Gram stain: rapid preliminary classification

Gram staining separates many bacteria according to cell-wall characteristics. Gram-positive organisms retain crystal violet and appear purple. Gram-negative organisms take up the counterstain and appear pink or red.

Shape adds useful early information. Cocci are round. Bacilli are rod-shaped. Arrangement can also matter: pairs, chains, clusters, or intracellular organisms may narrow the likely group.

A report of “purple cocci in clusters” therefore means Gram-positive cocci in clusters. It does not yet prove a specific species.

Culture: organism identification

Culture allows organisms in the specimen to grow so the laboratory can identify them. Interpretation still requires clinical judgment. Ask whether the organism is plausible, whether the specimen was good quality, whether the patient had antibiotics before collection, and whether colonization is possible.

Susceptibility: which antimicrobials are likely to work?

Susceptibility testing compares the organism with antimicrobial agents. A report may categorize an agent as susceptible, resistant, or another laboratory-defined category. These results guide the authorized prescriber and antimicrobial-stewardship team.

Why preliminary data still matter

A critically ill patient cannot always wait 48 hours for final culture and susceptibility data. If severe pneumonia is present and the Gram stain shows Gram-negative bacilli, the team may reasonably begin empiric broad-spectrum treatment while the final result is pending.

Common mistake: Do not write “culture confirmed” when you only have a Gram stain. State exactly which stage of microbiology is available.
Putting C5 together
Gram stain = rapid classification. Culture = organism identification. Susceptibility = likely antimicrobial activity. Always integrate specimen quality and the patient.
C6: Use CBC, differential, hemoglobin, hematocrit, chemistry, electrolyte, and clinical trends to evaluate infection severity, anemia, procedural risk, and alternative causes of deterioration.

Laboratory data provide clues, not automatic diagnoses

White blood cell count and differential

The white blood cell count may increase with infection or inflammation. But timing, age, immune suppression, medications, and severity influence the response. Some critically ill patients may have a normal or even low white blood cell count.

The differential describes the types of circulating white blood cells. Increased neutrophils and immature neutrophil forms may support a bacterial inflammatory response. This is sometimes referred to as a left shift.

Hemoglobin and hematocrit

Hemoglobin determines much of arterial oxygen-carrying capacity. A patient can have pneumonia and anemia simultaneously. If hemoglobin is severely low, a normal pulse-oximeter saturation does not guarantee normal oxygen content.

Platelets and procedural risk

Platelets contribute to clot formation. A low platelet count may increase bleeding risk for invasive procedures such as bronchoscopy with biopsy or thoracentesis. The procedural team considers platelets with coagulation studies, medication history, procedure type, and clinical urgency.

Chemistry and electrolytes can explain “respiratory” deterioration

Renal dysfunction may affect medication clearance and fluid balance. Severe potassium, magnesium, calcium, or phosphorus abnormalities may cause dysrhythmia, muscle weakness, or impaired respiratory function. Glucose disturbance may cause altered mental status. These problems may mimic or coexist with infection.

Use trends

A white blood cell count that rises from 8,000 to 16,000/µL at the same time that oxygen needs, respiratory rate, purulent secretions, and imaging abnormalities worsen is more meaningful than one isolated value.

Clinical reasoning: Ask whether the laboratory trend supports infection, reveals a competing explanation, or identifies a safety issue that changes the planned procedure.
Putting C6 together
Use CBC, chemistry, electrolytes, and trends to explain the patient's physiology rather than merely labeling values abnormal.
C7: Recognize classic and atypical infection presentations, including delirium, weakness, functional decline, tachypnea, and worsening oxygenation in older or immunocompromised patients.

Infection may present as a change from baseline

Classic pneumonia may include fever, cough, purulent sputum, pleuritic chest pain, tachypnea, elevated white blood cell count, focal crackles, hypoxemia, and a new infiltrate. But these findings are not required in every patient.

Older adults and immunocompromised patients may have a weaker fever or white blood cell response. Instead, the earliest clue may be new confusion, weakness, poor intake, lethargy, a fall, inability to perform usual activities, tachypnea, or a new oxygen requirement.

Delirium is an acute brain-function change

Delirium is an acute change in attention, awareness, or cognition. In a patient who was oriented yesterday and is confused today, treat the change as clinically meaningful until a cause is identified. Hypoxemia, infection, medication effect, pain, metabolic problems, dehydration, stroke, and other conditions may contribute.

Look for organ dysfunction

Infection plus new organ dysfunction raises concern for severe systemic illness. Concerning findings include altered mentation, hypotension, reduced urine output, elevated lactate when measured, worsening hypoxemia, increasing respiratory effort, or deteriorating perfusion.

Consider competing and coexisting diagnoses

Atelectasis, aspiration, pulmonary edema, mucus plugging, pulmonary embolism, medication effects, and delirium from noninfectious causes can imitate or coexist with pneumonia. Good assessment searches for both supporting and contradictory evidence.

Unit-exam pattern: An 84-year-old who was oriented yesterday but is now confused, RR 24/min, and SpO2 89% on room air deserves further respiratory and infection evaluation even if temperature is 37.2°C and WBC is 9,800/µL.
Putting C7 together
Recognize meaningful change from baseline and do not require fever or leukocytosis before taking infection seriously.
C8: Classify community-acquired, hospital-acquired, and ventilator-associated pneumonia using timing, setting, ventilation status, and the full clinical pattern.

Classification changes the clinical context

Community-acquired pneumonia

Community-acquired pneumonia, or CAP, begins outside the hospital or is present early enough in hospitalization that the current admission is not the source.

Hospital-acquired pneumonia

Hospital-acquired pneumonia, or HAP, develops at least 48 hours after admission and was not present or incubating on admission. In this course distinction, the patient is not mechanically ventilated.

Ventilator-associated pneumonia

Ventilator-associated pneumonia, or VAP, develops after endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation, commonly after at least 48 hours, and should fit a compatible clinical pattern rather than being assigned from time alone.

Evidence supporting suspected VAP can include a new or progressive infiltrate, purulent secretions, fever or white blood cell change, increasing oxygen requirement, and respiratory deterioration.

The organism does not determine the category

The same organism may cause community-acquired, hospital-acquired, or ventilator-associated infection. Classification is driven by timing, setting, ventilation status, and the clinical pattern.

VAP is not the same as VAE

Ventilator-associated event, or VAE, is a broader surveillance framework for sustained worsening in ventilator support or oxygenation. A VAE can result from infection or noninfectious problems such as pulmonary edema, atelectasis, or other deterioration. VAP is specifically a clinical infection diagnosis.

Quiz connection: Pneumonia beginning 72 hours after hospital admission in a patient who has not been intubated fits HAP when it was not present or incubating on admission.
Putting C8 together
Use a timeline. Ask where the pneumonia began, when it began, whether the patient is ventilated, and whether the clinical pattern supports infection.
C9: Integrate clinical severity with preliminary microbiology to explain why empiric broad-spectrum treatment may be started and why therapy should be narrowed when culture and sensitivity data permit.

Treat dangerous uncertainty without treating blindly forever

Empiric therapy means treatment begins before the exact organism and susceptibility profile are known. In severe infection, delaying appropriate antimicrobial therapy while waiting for final culture can increase risk.

Broad-spectrum therapy covers several likely organisms. The decision depends on illness severity, likely source, local resistance patterns, prior organisms or antibiotics, immune status, and preliminary microbiology.

For example, a critically ill patient with severe pneumonia and Gram-negative bacilli on a reliable preliminary Gram stain may need broad empiric coverage while culture and susceptibility are pending.

Why narrow therapy later?

Once the organism, susceptibility data, and patient response are clearer, treatment can often be narrowed. This is called de-escalation. Narrowing reduces unnecessary antimicrobial exposure, toxicity, resistance pressure, and disruption of normal flora.

Do not let a poor specimen drive antimicrobial decisions

If the specimen is contaminated or represents colonization, even a technically correct culture may not identify the cause of pneumonia. Specimen quality therefore affects antimicrobial interpretation.

Respiratory therapists do not independently prescribe antimicrobial therapy. Their role includes recognizing severity, obtaining high-quality specimens, reporting preliminary and final microbiology accurately, monitoring respiratory response, and communicating deterioration.

Clinical reasoning: “Start broad, then narrow” is not a memorized rule for every infection. It describes how severe illness and uncertainty can justify empiric coverage until reliable evidence permits targeted therapy.
Putting C9 together
Connect treatment breadth to severity and uncertainty, then connect narrowing to reliable organism identification, susceptibility, and clinical response.
C10: Prepare for and monitor bronchoscopy and thoracentesis, identify procedure-specific hazards, and recognize post-procedure findings that require urgent escalation.

Know the procedure before you monitor the procedure

Bronchoscopy preparation

Before bronchoscopy, verify the indication, consent status, fasting or nothing-by-mouth requirements according to policy, allergies, anticoagulant or coagulation concerns when relevant, baseline vital signs, oxygen requirement, airway risk, intravenous access, and planned monitoring.

The therapist may prepare oxygen delivery, suction, bronchoscopy adapters, monitoring equipment, airway equipment, specimen containers, and emergency support depending on the procedure and local role.

Bronchoscopy monitoring

Sedation and airway instrumentation can cause hypoventilation, hypoxemia, bronchospasm, bleeding, dysrhythmia, or airway obstruction. Monitor peripheral oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, work of breathing, ventilation when capnography is used, heart rate, blood pressure, level of consciousness, and airway patency.

After bronchoscopy

Continue monitoring until sedation effects resolve and airway protection is appropriate. Follow policy regarding oral intake after topical anesthesia and return of gag or swallowing safety. Watch for bleeding, respiratory distress, fever, or pneumothorax after biopsy when relevant.

Thoracentesis preparation

Verify the side and indication, consent, relevant imaging, coagulation and platelet concerns, current oxygenation, ability to sit or maintain the required position, and emergency readiness.

The intercostal artery, vein, and nerve run along the inferior rib margin. Procedural entry is generally made just superior to a rib to reduce injury to this neurovascular bundle. The clinician performing the procedure determines the exact site using imaging and procedural technique.

Post-thoracentesis pneumothorax

After fluid removal, sudden dyspnea, tachycardia, worsening hypoxemia, and markedly reduced breath sounds on the procedure side should raise immediate concern for pneumothorax.

Unit-exam emergency: Ten minutes after 1.2 L of pleural fluid is removed, HR 124/min, SpO2 84%, sudden dyspnea, and unilateral breath-sound loss require oxygenation support, rapid procedural-team notification, and urgent evaluation for pneumothorax—not routine observation.
Putting C10 together
Know what the procedure can change physiologically, what equipment and monitoring are needed, and which new findings require the procedure pathway to become an emergency pathway.
C11: Apply hand hygiene, PPE, isolation, specimen-handling, nebulizer hygiene, oral-care, ventilator-circuit, condensate-management, and ventilator-associated-event prevention practices.

Infection prevention works by interrupting transmission pathways

Standard precautions

Standard precautions apply to every patient. They include hand hygiene and appropriate protection when exposure to blood, body fluids, respiratory secretions, mucous membranes, or contaminated equipment is possible.

Transmission-based precautions

Additional precautions are selected according to the suspected route of transmission. Contact precautions reduce transmission by direct or indirect contact. Droplet precautions reduce exposure to larger respiratory droplets. Airborne precautions are used for pathogens that can remain suspended in small particles and travel through the air.

Suspected active pulmonary tuberculosis is the key course example requiring prompt airborne precautions. Isolation begins from credible suspicion; it does not wait for final culture.

Nebulizer and respiratory-equipment hygiene

Respiratory devices can carry moisture and secretions and therefore become transmission pathways. Follow single-patient-use, cleaning, disinfection, medication-preparation, and storage procedures for nebulizers and other reusable equipment.

Ventilator circuits: avoid unnecessary disruption

Routine unnecessary circuit disconnection can interrupt ventilation and expose the airway and environment to contaminated secretions. Circuit changes should follow clinical need and policy rather than arbitrary frequent replacement.

Condensate must drain away from the patient

Condensate is water that collects in ventilator tubing. It may contain microorganisms and secretions. If it drains toward the patient's airway, contaminated fluid can enter the respiratory tract.

Using appropriate precautions, drain condensate away from the patient into the designated trap or collection system while minimizing circuit disruption and following facility policy.

Prevent aspiration and secretion pooling

Ventilator-associated infection prevention may include hand hygiene, oral care according to protocol, head-of-bed positioning when appropriate, aspiration precautions, secretion management, safe cuff and circuit practices, and reducing unnecessary duration of invasive ventilation when clinically appropriate.

Do not memorize these as disconnected checklist items. Connect each action with the pathway it interrupts: hand transmission, contaminated equipment, aspiration, secretion pooling, circuit disruption, or prolonged ventilation.

Quiz connection: Condensate pooling near the patient should be drained away from the patient into the designated trap while using precautions and minimizing circuit disruption.
Putting C11 together
For every infection-prevention action, be able to explain what source, route, or opportunity for transmission it is designed to interrupt.
C12: Integrate symptoms, mental status, vital signs, oxygenation, secretions, imaging, laboratory trends, specimen quality, and recent procedures to determine the next assessment, communication, or emergency response.

The test result is only one piece of the patient

At this level, you should be able to decide whether the next step is more information, a better specimen, treatment support, infection-prevention action, provider communication, or emergency escalation.

Case 1: atypical infection and safe information gathering

An 84-year-old postoperative patient was oriented yesterday but is now confused. Respiratory rate is 24/min and SpO2 is 89% on room air. Temperature is 37.2°C and WBC is 9,800/µL.

The important finding is the change from baseline. Assess work of breathing, lung sounds, oxygen requirement, perfusion, medication changes, pain, aspiration risk, imaging, laboratory trends, and possible infection. Communicate the acute change. Do not dismiss the situation because fever and leukocytosis are absent.

Case 2: suspected VAP, invalid specimen, and prevention priorities

A patient has been intubated for 6 days. Temperature is 38.8°C, WBC 18,000/µL, secretions are purulent, FiO2 requirement is increasing, and imaging shows a new right-lower-lobe infiltrate. A cup labeled “sputum” contains 0.5 mL of clear saliva collected from the mouth.

The timeline and clinical findings support suspected VAP. The sample is invalid. Communicate the concern, obtain a properly labeled lower-airway specimen such as a tracheal aspirate according to policy, support timely empiric management while cultures are pending, and review ventilator-associated-event prevention practices.

Case 3: deterioration after thoracentesis

Ten minutes after thoracentesis, the patient develops sudden dyspnea, HR 124/min, SpO2 84%, and markedly diminished breath sounds on the procedure side.

The sequence and unilateral findings strongly suggest pneumothorax. Support oxygenation, rapidly notify the procedural team, and obtain urgent evaluation. The unstable patient does not wait for routine diagnostic sequencing.

A practical integration checklist

  1. What changed from baseline?
  2. How unstable is the patient right now?
  3. What respiratory support is required?
  4. What do symptoms, secretions, imaging, and labs suggest?
  5. Can the specimen or result be trusted?
  6. What alternative or coexisting diagnosis must be considered?
  7. What infection-control action is needed now?
  8. What must be communicated, recollected, repeated, or escalated next?

Communicate classification with evidence

Instead of saying “I think this is VAP,” provide the evidence: “The patient has been mechanically ventilated for 6 days and now has a new right-lower-lobe infiltrate, purulent secretions, fever, WBC 18,000/µL, and increasing FiO2 requirement. The mouth specimen contains clear saliva and does not represent the lower airway. A tracheal aspirate is needed according to policy.”

Clinical reasoning: Trends tell the story. Rising oxygen need + increasing respiratory rate + worsening secretions + new infiltrate + changing WBC + altered mental status is much more informative than one isolated abnormality.
Putting C12 together
Decide what information is reliable, what the pattern means, how urgent it is, and whether the safest next step is more assessment, recollection, communication, prevention action, or emergency support.

Week 3.1: Build evidence before you build conclusions

A diagnostic test is valuable only when it answers the right question and the data are trustworthy. A culture must come from the correct source. A pneumonia label must fit the timeline and clinical pattern. A procedure must be monitored for predictable complications. Infection precautions begin from credible risk, not only from final laboratory proof.

question → test → patient preparation → specimen quality → result stage → clinical fit → transmission risk → treatment/communication → reassessment

  • Recognize clinically meaningful atypical infection even when fever and WBC are normal.
  • Use change from baseline, oxygenation, respiratory rate, mental status, imaging, labs, and perfusion to guide the next assessment.
  • Recognize suspected VAP from ventilation duration plus a compatible clinical pattern rather than from intubation alone.
