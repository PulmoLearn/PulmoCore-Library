Pulmonary Assessment • 2.2 Competency Guide

Assessment of Oxygen: Hypoxemia, Tissue Hypoxia, Oxygen Content & Delivery

Learn how oxygen moves from the lungs to the cells, why a normal oxygen saturation can still hide life-threatening oxygen-delivery failure, and how to identify whether the problem is the lung, the oxygen carrier, the pump, or the cell.

Before you begin

Oxygen assessment is more than looking at the number on a pulse oximeter. Oxygen must enter the lungs, reach the alveoli, cross into arterial blood, bind to functioning hemoglobin, travel with enough blood flow, leave the blood at the tissues, and finally be used by the cells. Failure at any point can cause tissue injury.

Big idea for Week 2.2: Use the framework oxygen → carrier → pump → cell. First ask whether oxygen is getting into arterial blood. Then ask whether enough functioning hemoglobin is available to carry it, whether enough blood flow is delivering it, and whether the cells can actually use it.

Vocabulary you will use this week: hypoxemia, hypoxia, arterial oxygen tension, arterial oxygen saturation, peripheral oxygen saturation, oxygen content, oxygen delivery, alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, shunt, diffusion limitation, hemoglobin, cardiac output, perfusion, histotoxic hypoxia, carboxyhemoglobin, co-oximetry, oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve.

C1: Distinguish arterial hypoxemia from tissue hypoxia and explain why either condition may occur without the other.

Hypoxemia and hypoxia are not the same thing

Hypoxemia means that the oxygen tension in arterial blood is lower than expected for the patient and conditions. The partial pressure of arterial oxygen, written as PaO2, is measured from an arterial blood gas.

Hypoxia is a tissue-level problem. It means oxygen delivery to the tissues, or the cells' ability to use oxygen, is not adequate for metabolic demand.

What does the pulse oximeter actually tell you?

Peripheral oxygen saturation, written as SpO2, estimates the percentage of available hemoglobin binding sites occupied by oxygen. It does not measure how much hemoglobin the patient has. It does not measure cardiac output. It does not prove that cells can use oxygen.

Hypoxemia without obvious tissue failure

A patient with chronic lung disease may live with a lower-than-average PaO2 while maintaining adequate hemoglobin, blood flow, and organ function. The hypoxemia still matters, but it does not automatically mean immediate cellular failure is occurring.

Hypoxia with normal PaO2 and SpO2

A patient can have normal dissolved oxygen and normal displayed saturation yet still have life-threatening tissue hypoxia. This can happen with severe anemia, cardiogenic shock, carbon monoxide poisoning, or cyanide exposure.

Think of four checkpoints:
  1. Oxygen: Is oxygen loading into arterial blood?
  2. Carrier: Is there enough functioning hemoglobin?
  3. Pump: Is enough blood reaching the tissues?
  4. Cell: Can the tissues use the oxygen delivered?

Clinical example

Two patients have a PaO2 of 92 mmHg and an SpO2 of 97%. One has hemoglobin 14 g/dL and normal perfusion. The other has hemoglobin 5.8 g/dL after major blood loss and is pale, tachycardic, weak, and short of breath. Their oxygen tension and saturation look similar, but the second patient has far less oxygen-carrying capacity.

Clinical reasoning: Never allow PaO2 or SpO2 alone to answer the question, “Are this patient's tissues receiving enough usable oxygen?”
Putting C1 together
Know that hypoxemia is an arterial blood finding while hypoxia is a tissue problem. A patient can have one without the other.
C2: Classify hypoxemic, anemic, ischemic or stagnant, and histotoxic hypoxia using PaO2, saturation, hemoglobin, perfusion, exposure history, and clinical findings.

Four major mechanisms of tissue hypoxia

To classify tissue hypoxia, identify the first place the oxygen pathway fails. A patient may have more than one mechanism at the same time.

Mechanism Where failure begins Classic example
Hypoxemic hypoxiaOxygen loading into arterial blood is inadequatePneumonia with low PaO2 and low saturation
Anemic hypoxiaToo little functioning hemoglobin is availableSevere blood-loss anemia
Ischemic or stagnant hypoxiaBlood flow is inadequateCardiogenic shock with low cardiac output
Histotoxic hypoxiaCells cannot use delivered oxygenCyanide toxicity

Hypoxemic hypoxia: a lung-loading problem

In hypoxemic hypoxia, the problem begins with oxygen entering arterial blood. PaO2 is low and saturation is usually reduced. Causes include pneumonia, pulmonary edema, acute respiratory distress syndrome, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, shunt, diffusion limitation, hypoventilation, and low inspired oxygen.

Anemic hypoxia: a carrier problem

In anemic hypoxia, oxygen may enter the lungs and arterial blood normally, but there is not enough functioning hemoglobin to carry an adequate amount. Severe anemia is the classic example. PaO2 may be normal, and SpO2 may also look normal because the hemoglobin that is present can still be highly saturated.

Carbon monoxide creates a special functional carrier problem because it occupies hemoglobin binding sites and changes the behavior of the remaining sites.

Ischemic or stagnant hypoxia: a pump or flow problem

In ischemic or stagnant hypoxia, oxygen content may be perfectly acceptable, but too little blood reaches the tissues. Cardiogenic shock, severe pump failure, profound circulatory failure, or a regional vascular obstruction can cause this pattern.

Histotoxic hypoxia: a cellular-use problem

In histotoxic hypoxia, oxygen reaches the tissues but cellular metabolism cannot use it effectively. Cyanide is the classic board-style example because it interferes with mitochondrial oxygen utilization.

Treatment-thinking rule: Increasing oxygen concentration may be lifesaving support, but it does not replace missing hemoglobin, restore a failing cardiac output, or unblock cyanide-inhibited cellular metabolism. Treat the failing component.

Clinical example

A patient after gastrointestinal bleeding has PaO2 91 mmHg, SpO2 97%, hemoglobin 5.8 g/dL, heart rate 128/min, pallor, weakness, and exertional dyspnea. The primary oxygen problem is anemic hypoxia. Oxygen enters the blood, but there are too few carriers.

Putting C2 together
Identify the first failed link: lung loading, hemoglobin carriage, blood flow, or cellular use. Then match assessment and treatment priorities to that failure.
C3: Recognize early compensatory signs and late high-risk signs of inadequate oxygen delivery and select the appropriate urgency of reassessment or escalation.

The body tries to compensate before it fails

Early warning signs: RAT

The course uses RAT to help remember early compensatory findings:

  • R – Restlessness
  • A – Anxiety
  • T – Tachycardia and tachypnea

Tachycardia means an increased heart rate. Increasing heart rate can help increase cardiac output when stroke volume is maintained. Tachypnea means an increased respiratory rate and may be an attempt to improve gas exchange or respond to metabolic stress.

New restlessness or anxiety should not automatically be dismissed as behavioral. It can be an early neurologic warning sign of inadequate oxygenation, ventilation, perfusion, glucose disturbance, medication effect, pain, or another physiologic problem.

Late high-risk signs: BED-C

The course uses BED-C for late, dangerous findings:

  • B – Bradycardia, especially after earlier tachycardia
  • E – Extreme restlessness or altered mental status
  • D – Severe dyspnea or decreasing respiratory effort
  • C – Cyanosis

Bradycardia means an abnormally slow heart rate. In a deteriorating hypoxic patient, a heart rate that falls after compensatory tachycardia can indicate myocardial hypoxia and impending arrest.

Cyanosis is a blue or gray discoloration associated with increased deoxygenated hemoglobin. It is a late and unreliable sign. Severe anemia can make cyanosis less visible even when tissue oxygen delivery is critically impaired.

A falling respiratory rate can be a bad sign

If a distressed patient improves after treatment and the respiratory rate falls while work of breathing and mental status improve, that is reassuring. If the respiratory rate falls because the patient is exhausted, becomes drowsy, and takes shallow breaths, the patient may be progressing toward respiratory failure.

Emergency priority: New bradycardia, cyanosis, altered consciousness, severe dyspnea, or decreasing respiratory effort in a worsening patient requires immediate assessment and escalation rather than routine monitoring.

Clinical example

A patient was initially tachycardic and tachypneic. Later the patient becomes difficult to arouse, heart rate drops from 118 to 46/min, breathing becomes shallow, and the lips look dusky. This is not improvement. Compensation is failing.

Putting C3 together
Recognize early compensation before late failure develops, and judge the meaning of any change by the patient's overall trajectory and organ function.
C4: Calculate or interpret the alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient and use it to distinguish extrapulmonary causes of hypoxemia from intrapulmonary gas-exchange impairment.

Where is oxygen being lost?

The alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient, commonly called the A-a gradient, compares the estimated oxygen pressure in the alveoli with the measured oxygen pressure in arterial blood.

The oxygen cascade

At sea level on room air, oxygen pressure is highest in the atmosphere and gradually decreases as oxygen is humidified, enters the alveoli, crosses into arterial blood, and is extracted by tissues. Approximate teaching reference points are:

  • Atmospheric oxygen pressure: about 159 mmHg
  • Humidified inspired oxygen pressure: about 150 mmHg
  • Alveolar oxygen pressure: about 100 mmHg
  • Arterial PaO2: commonly about 80–100 mmHg in a healthy young adult
  • Mixed venous oxygen pressure: about 40 mmHg

These are teaching reference points, not universal normal values. Age, altitude, fraction of inspired oxygen, ventilation, and disease change them.

The alveolar gas equation

To estimate alveolar oxygen pressure, written as PAO2, use:

PAO2 = FiO2 × (PB − PH2O) − PaCO2 / R

At sea level, water-vapor pressure is approximately 47 mmHg and the respiratory quotient, R, is commonly approximated as 0.8. On room air, a useful estimate is:

PAO2 ≈ 150 − PaCO2 / 0.8

Calculate the gradient

P(A-a)O2 = PAO2 − PaO2

What does the result mean?

A small or expected A-a gradient suggests oxygen is moving from the alveoli to arterial blood reasonably well. If PaO2 is still low, consider an extrapulmonary explanation such as hypoventilation or low inspired oxygen.

An increased or widened A-a gradient suggests oxygen is available in the alveoli but is not reaching arterial blood as expected. This points toward an intrapulmonary gas-exchange problem such as ventilation-perfusion mismatch, shunt, or diffusion limitation.

Compare two patients:

Patient A: opioid-related hypoventilation, calculated PAO2 65 mmHg and PaO2 60 mmHg. The gradient is only 5 mmHg, supporting hypoventilation.

Patient B: pneumonia, PAO2 100 mmHg and PaO2 55 mmHg. The gradient is 45 mmHg, supporting intrapulmonary gas-exchange impairment.

A young adult on room air often has an A-a gradient below roughly 10–15 mmHg, but expected values rise with age and vary with clinical conditions and fraction of inspired oxygen. Use the course or facility reference standard rather than treating one cutoff as universal.

Do not calculate blindly: If the fraction of inspired oxygen is unknown or changing, the blood-gas sample is questionable, or ventilator conditions are unstable, the calculated gradient may be misleading.
Putting C4 together
Use the A-a gradient to answer a localization question: is the primary problem getting oxygen into the alveoli, or transferring oxygen from the alveoli into arterial blood?
C5: Calculate and interpret arterial oxygen content using hemoglobin, arterial saturation, and dissolved oxygen, and compare patients whose PaO2 or SpO2 values appear similar.

How much oxygen is actually in arterial blood?

Arterial oxygen content, written as CaO2, estimates the total amount of oxygen carried in arterial blood. Most oxygen is bound to hemoglobin. A much smaller amount is dissolved directly in plasma.

CaO2 = (1.34 × Hgb × SaO2) + (0.003 × PaO2)

Hgb means hemoglobin concentration in grams per deciliter. SaO2 means measured arterial oxygen saturation. Enter saturation as a decimal: 90% becomes 0.90. The final CaO2 unit is milliliters of oxygen per deciliter of blood.

The hemoglobin-bound term dominates

Each gram of fully saturated hemoglobin carries about 1.34 mL of oxygen. Because the dissolved term is very small at ordinary PaO2 values, hemoglobin concentration and saturation contribute most of the arterial oxygen content.

Worked example

Hemoglobin = 10 g/dL, SaO2 = 90% or 0.90, PaO2 = 60 mmHg:

CaO2 = (1.34 × 10 × 0.90) + (0.003 × 60)
CaO2 = 12.06 + 0.18
CaO2 ≈ 12.2 mL O2/dL

Why anemia can be hidden by saturation

A patient with hemoglobin 6 g/dL and SaO2 98% can have far less oxygen content than a patient with hemoglobin 16 g/dL and SaO2 89%. Saturation tells you how full the available hemoglobin sites are. It does not tell you how many hemoglobin molecules are available.

Quiz and exam priority: When patients have similar PaO2 or SpO2, compare hemoglobin. Severe anemia may create the greatest oxygen-content risk even when the saturation display looks reassuring.
Putting C5 together
Know the CaO2 equation, use saturation as a decimal, keep the units, and identify which part of the equation is limiting oxygen content.
C6: Calculate and interpret systemic oxygen delivery using CaO2 and cardiac output and recognize perfusion failure when oxygen content is adequate.

Oxygen content is only useful if blood is moving

Systemic oxygen delivery, written as DO2, estimates how much oxygen is delivered to the systemic circulation each minute. It combines the oxygen content of the blood with cardiac output, which is the amount of blood the heart pumps each minute.

DO2 = CaO2 × cardiac output × 10

The factor of 10 converts the CaO2 unit from milliliters per deciliter to milliliters per liter when cardiac output is expressed in liters per minute. The final result is milliliters of oxygen per minute.

Continue the worked example

If CaO2 is 12.2 mL/dL and cardiac output is 3.0 L/min:

DO2 = 12.2 × 3.0 × 10
DO2 ≈ 366 mL O2/min

In this case, both oxygen content and systemic oxygen delivery are reduced.

Normal content with poor delivery

A patient can have normal PaO2, normal saturation, and normal hemoglobin but still have poor oxygen delivery if cardiac output is critically low. This is the “pump” problem.

Bedside findings that support poor perfusion include hypotension, weak pulses, delayed capillary refill, cool skin, low urine output, altered mental status, and rising lactate. Lactate is useful supporting information but is not specific for one type of hypoxia.

Clinical example

After a myocardial infarction, a patient has PaO2 90 mmHg, SaO2 97%, hemoglobin 14 g/dL, blood pressure 76/50 mmHg, cool extremities, confusion, and cardiac output 2.0 L/min. Arterial oxygen content may be acceptable, but oxygen delivery is low because blood flow is inadequate. The primary problem is circulatory failure—not lack of oxygen in the alveoli.

Clinical reasoning: Normal oxygen content cannot compensate for critically inadequate blood flow. Ask whether the pump is delivering the oxygen that is available.
Putting C6 together
Calculate DO2, identify whether content or flow is the limiting factor, and validate the number with bedside perfusion findings.
C7: Interpret right and left shifts of the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve and explain how affinity influences tissue unloading without replacing content and perfusion assessment.

Hemoglobin must both load and release oxygen

The oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve describes the relationship between oxygen tension and hemoglobin saturation. It also helps explain affinity, meaning how tightly hemoglobin holds oxygen.

The upper and lower parts of the curve

The upper portion of the curve is relatively flat. At higher PaO2 values, moderate changes in oxygen tension may cause only small changes in saturation. This means an SpO2 in the high 90s cannot tell you whether PaO2 is simply normal or extremely high.

The lower portion is steep. Once oxygen tension falls into this region, a small further decrease can produce a much larger drop in saturation.

Right shift: easier oxygen unloading

A right shift means hemoglobin has a lower affinity for oxygen and releases it more easily to tissues. The course emphasizes these right-shift conditions:

  • increased temperature;
  • increased carbon dioxide;
  • decreased pH, or acidosis;
  • increased 2,3-diphosphoglycerate, commonly shortened to 2,3-DPG.

Left shift: tighter oxygen binding

A left shift means hemoglobin has a higher affinity for oxygen and holds it more tightly. Conditions include decreased temperature, decreased carbon dioxide, increased pH, decreased 2,3-DPG, fetal hemoglobin, and carbon monoxide exposure.

A higher saturation is not always better tissue unloading

With a left shift, hemoglobin may remain highly saturated because it is reluctant to release oxygen. That can make the monitor appear reassuring while tissue unloading is impaired.

Quiz priority: Increased temperature + increased PaCO2 + decreased pH shifts the curve to the right and promotes oxygen unloading.

Curve position does not replace assessment of hemoglobin concentration, arterial oxygen content, cardiac output, perfusion, or cellular utilization. Easier unloading cannot solve severe anemia or shock.

Putting C7 together
Right shift = lower affinity and easier unloading. Left shift = higher affinity and tighter binding. Always interpret the curve with content and perfusion.
C8: Recognize when pulse oximetry or PaO2 is misleading, select co-oximetry or other confirmatory data, and prioritize treatment for carbon monoxide exposure, anemia, shock, cyanide exposure, or lung disease.

When “normal oxygen numbers” are misleading

Carbon monoxide exposure

Carbon monoxide binds strongly to hemoglobin and forms carboxyhemoglobin, commonly written as COHb. This removes functional oxygen-binding sites and also shifts the remaining oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve to the left, making oxygen harder to unload.

PaO2 can remain normal because it measures dissolved oxygen in plasma, not how much carbon monoxide is attached to hemoglobin. Conventional two-wavelength pulse oximetry can also look falsely reassuring because it cannot reliably distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin.

Use co-oximetry when abnormal hemoglobin is suspected

Co-oximetry uses multiple wavelengths to measure different hemoglobin species, including oxyhemoglobin, carboxyhemoglobin, and methemoglobin. It is the appropriate confirmatory test when carbon monoxide poisoning or another dyshemoglobinemia is suspected.

Carbon monoxide emergency principle: A credible carbon monoxide exposure should be treated with high-concentration oxygen while co-oximetry is obtained. Do not delay treatment because PaO2 or SpO2 appears normal.

A house-fire patient may have more than one oxygen problem

Smoke inhalation can produce lung injury, carbon monoxide exposure, and cyanide toxicity at the same time. A patient may therefore have a lung-loading problem, a hemoglobin-carrier problem, and a cellular-use problem simultaneously.

A fire victim with confusion, soot around the airway, SpO2 100%, PaO2 96 mmHg, hypotension, and rising lactate should not be observed just because the oxygen display looks normal. Priorities include high-concentration oxygen, airway assessment and protection, co-oximetry, communication of concern for carbon monoxide and possible cyanide exposure, and support of perfusion.

Match the treatment to the failed link

Primary problem Main assessment/treatment direction
Lung-loading failure / hypoxemiaProvide appropriate oxygen support and treat the gas-exchange or ventilation cause
Severe anemiaRestore oxygen-carrying capacity and control blood loss as indicated
Shock / low cardiac outputRestore circulation and perfusion while supporting oxygenation
Carbon monoxide exposureHigh-concentration oxygen plus co-oximetry and further exposure-specific management
Cyanide exposureSpecific antidotal treatment and supportive care according to emergency protocol

Controlled oxygen in chronic hypercapnia risk

Some patients at risk for hypercapnic respiratory failure may have a prescribed or protocol oxygen-saturation target, commonly 88–92% in selected patients. Oxygen should be titrated while ventilation and clinical response are monitored. Do not use the oversimplified idea that every patient with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease breathes only because of a “hypoxic drive.”

Do not withhold needed oxygen from a severely hypoxemic patient because of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease history. Use the order, protocol, blood-gas or capnography data, baseline, and patient condition.

Reassessment closes the loop: After treatment, reassess the failing component. A patient may improve in saturation but still have poor hemoglobin, worsening shock, rising carbon dioxide, abnormal mental status, or cellular toxin exposure.
Putting C8 together
Know when the monitor can mislead you, choose the test that measures the missing information, and treat the mechanism that is actually failing.

Week 2.2: Follow oxygen all the way to the cell

An oxygen-saturation number is only one checkpoint in a much longer pathway. Safe respiratory assessment means recognizing whether oxygen loading, hemoglobin carriage, blood flow, or cellular use is failing.

Use this sequence as you enter the PulmoLearn lesson: assess the patient → check oxygen loading → check hemoglobin carrier → check the pump and perfusion → consider cellular use → calculate when useful → identify the failing link → treat and reassess.

Before the weekly quiz, you should be able to...

  • distinguish hypoxemia from tissue hypoxia;
  • identify pneumonia with low PaO2 and low saturation as hypoxemic hypoxia;
  • identify severe anemia with normal PaO2 and SpO2 as anemic hypoxia;
  • identify cardiogenic shock with low cardiac output as ischemic or stagnant hypoxia;
  • identify cyanide as the classic cause of histotoxic hypoxia;
  • recall restlessness, anxiety, tachycardia, and tachypnea as early compensatory signs;
  • recognize new bradycardia, cyanosis, altered mental status, and severe dyspnea as late high-risk signs;
  • explain that a widened alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient suggests ventilation-perfusion mismatch, shunt, or diffusion limitation;
  • identify hemoglobin concentration and arterial saturation as the major determinants of CaO2;
  • recognize that a patient with severe anemia can have the lowest arterial oxygen content despite a normal saturation;
  • explain why lower cardiac output reduces DO2 even if CaO2 does not change; and
  • recognize increased temperature, increased PaCO2, and decreased pH as right-shift conditions that promote oxygen unloading.

For the Unit 2 Competency Exam, take the next step

The competency exam will ask you to uncover oxygen-delivery problems that are hidden by apparently reassuring PaO2 or SpO2 values.

  • Compare patients with similar PaO2 and SpO2 values and identify severe anemia as the greatest oxygen-content risk.
  • Calculate CaO2 using (1.34 × Hgb × SaO2) + (0.003 × PaO2).
  • Calculate DO2 using CaO2 × cardiac output × 10.
  • Interpret the course calculation case with Hgb 10 g/dL, SaO2 90%, PaO2 60 mmHg, and cardiac output 3 L/min as CaO2 about 12.2 mL/dL and DO2 about 366 mL/min, with both content and delivery reduced.
  • Recognize carbon monoxide exposure despite normal PaO2 and conventional SpO2.
  • Choose high-concentration oxygen and co-oximetry when carbon monoxide exposure is suspected.
  • Identify whether the failed component is oxygen loading, hemoglobin carriage, circulation, or cellular utilization before choosing the treatment priority.
  • Recognize mixed oxygen-delivery problems in which lung injury, anemia, shock, or toxins coexist.
