Pulmonary Assessment • 1.3 Competency Guide

SOAP Notes & Clinical Documentation

Learn how respiratory therapists turn patient findings into clear, accurate documentation that explains what happened, what the data mean, what was done, how the patient responded, and what should happen next.

Before you begin

Clinical documentation is not simply “writing down what happened.” It is part of patient care. Another clinician may use your note to decide whether to continue a treatment, change oxygen support, call a provider, or reassess the patient urgently.

Big idea for Week 1.3: A strong respiratory note tells a clear clinical story. Ask: What did the patient report? What did I measure or observe? What do those findings support? What did I do? How did the patient respond? What should happen next?

Vocabulary you will begin using this week: documentation, charting, Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan, chronology, objective wording, defensible documentation, treatment response, tolerance, adverse response, reassessment, refusal, provider notification, late entry, addendum, correction, audit trail.

C1: Organize respiratory symptoms, history, measured findings, clinical interpretation, and planned actions into accurate Subjective, Objective, Assessment, and Plan sections.

What is a SOAP note?

A SOAP note is a common way to organize clinical information. The letters stand for Subjective, Objective, Assessment, and Plan. Each section has a different purpose.

Section What belongs here? Example
SubjectiveWhat the patient or caregiver reports“My chest feels tight and I am more short of breath today.”
ObjectiveWhat you measure, observe, or verifyRespiratory rate 30 breaths/min, accessory-muscle use, peripheral oxygen saturation 88% on room air, bilateral expiratory wheezes
AssessmentWhat the available findings supportIncreased work of breathing with low oxygen saturation and airflow-obstruction findings
PlanWhat will be done nextAdminister ordered oxygen and bronchodilator therapy, reassess respiratory status, notify provider if condition worsens

Subjective does not mean “unimportant”

A patient's symptoms are valuable clinical information. Shortness of breath, chest tightness, fatigue, dizziness, pain, and treatment tolerance are all important. They are called subjective because they are reported by the patient rather than directly measured by the clinician.

Objective means measured or directly observed

Objective findings include vital signs, peripheral oxygen saturation, oxygen device and setting, breath sounds, work of breathing, cough quality, sputum characteristics, mental status, blood-gas values, and imaging results that have been verified.

Assessment is your supported interpretation

The Assessment section is where you explain what the available findings mean. This is not permission to invent a medical diagnosis that has not been established. For example, “wheezing with increased work of breathing and low oxygen saturation” is supported by the data. “Patient definitely has pneumonia” would be inappropriate if the evidence does not establish that diagnosis.

Plan tells the team what happens next

The Plan may include therapy, monitoring, patient education, provider communication, consultation, additional assessment, or escalation. A strong plan also says what will be reassessed and what would cause the plan to change.

Clinical reasoning: The Objective findings should support the Assessment, and the Plan should respond to the Assessment. If those sections do not connect, the note is weak.

Clinical example

Subjective: “I feel more short of breath than I did this morning.”
Objective: Respiratory rate 28 breaths/min, accessory-muscle use present, peripheral oxygen saturation 89% on room air, bilateral expiratory wheezes.
Assessment: Increased work of breathing with low oxygen saturation and airflow-obstruction findings.
Plan: Administer ordered oxygen and bronchodilator therapy, reassess symptoms, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, breath sounds, and work of breathing, and notify the provider if the patient does not improve.

Putting C1 together
Be able to sort patient information into Subjective, Objective, Assessment, and Plan—and make sure the four sections tell one connected respiratory story.
C2: Use specific, objective, timely, accurate, and legally defensible documentation that avoids vague labels, judgmental language, unsupported motives, and unverified diagnoses.

Write what happened, not what you assume

Clinical documentation must be accurate enough that another clinician can understand the patient's condition even if they were not present. That means using specific observations rather than vague labels.

Compare these examples:

Weak: “Patient breathing bad.”
Stronger: “Respiratory rate 32 breaths/min, accessory-muscle use present, peripheral oxygen saturation 87% on room air, speaking in short phrases.”

Objective language reduces bias

Avoid labels such as “difficult,” “noncompliant,” “manipulative,” or “uncooperative.” These words can suggest motive and may influence how future clinicians view the patient. Instead, describe the behavior you observed and the reason the patient gave.

Instead of “patient was noncompliant,” write: “Patient removed nebulizer after 3 minutes and stated, ‘The treatment makes me nauseated.’ Education provided regarding purpose of treatment. Patient declined to restart. Provider notified.”

Include enough detail to make the finding reproducible

When the details affect interpretation, include values, units, location, laterality, oxygen device and setting, time, and observable behavior. “Crackles” is less useful than “fine inspiratory crackles at both posterior bases.”

Timeliness matters

Document close enough to the event that the sequence remains reliable. If documentation is delayed, follow the approved late-entry process so the record makes clear when care occurred and when the note was entered.

Do not copy forward without checking

Copy-forward means bringing information from an earlier note into a new note. This can save time, but it becomes dangerous if old oxygen settings, breath sounds, or patient findings remain in the record after the condition changes. Every copied finding must be reviewed and verified.

Legally defensible does not mean “write more”

Legally defensible documentation is truthful, specific, chronological, attributable to the person who entered it, and consistent with the known record. More words do not automatically make a note safer. Inaccurate or copied details may actually increase risk.

Beginner rule: Describe the patient, not your opinion of the patient.
Putting C2 together
Use specific, measurable, neutral wording. Document only what actually happened and verify copied or unusual information before signing.
C3: Document respiratory therapy, oxygen support, relevant pre- and post-assessment findings, patient tolerance, education, response, and reassessment needs.

A treatment is not complete until the response is assessed

Respiratory therapy documentation should show more than the fact that a treatment was given. It should show the patient's condition before therapy, what intervention was performed, what changed afterward, whether the patient tolerated it, and what needs to happen next.

Pretreatment baseline

Before therapy, record the findings that will help you decide whether the treatment works. Depending on the treatment, this may include heart rate, respiratory rate, peripheral oxygen saturation, oxygen device and setting, breath sounds, work of breathing, cough, sputum, dyspnea, mental status, and relevant safety concerns.

Intervention details

Document what was actually performed. Include the medication or therapy, dose or setting when applicable, delivery device, duration when relevant, oxygen support, and any significant change from the planned treatment.

Post-treatment reassessment

Reassess the same important variables you measured before treatment. A response is easiest to interpret when the conditions are similar.

For example, if peripheral oxygen saturation rises after a breathing treatment but the oxygen flow was also increased, you cannot assume the bronchodilator alone caused the improvement. Document the oxygen change so the next clinician can interpret the result correctly.

What to compare Before treatment After treatment
SymptomsChest tightness, dyspnea, coughBetter, unchanged, or worse?
Respiratory rateBaseline rateDirection and amount of change
OxygenationPeripheral oxygen saturation plus oxygen supportRepeat saturation on documented support
Breath soundsLocation and qualityImproved, unchanged, worsened, or new finding
Work of breathingAccessory-muscle use, speech, effortLess, same, or greater effort

Response and tolerance are different

Treatment response asks whether the therapy helped the respiratory problem. Tolerance asks whether the patient experienced unwanted effects or was able to complete the therapy safely. A treatment can improve wheezing but still cause palpitations, tremor, nausea, dizziness, or another adverse effect.

Patient education belongs in the record when it affects care

Document clinically relevant education such as treatment purpose, device technique, expected effects, safety instructions, or when to report symptoms. When possible, record how understanding was checked, such as teach-back or return demonstration.

Clinical reasoning: “Treatment given” does not prove “treatment effective.” Effectiveness requires comparison of relevant pre- and post-treatment findings.

Clinical example

Before albuterol, the patient has heart rate 104 beats/min, respiratory rate 26 breaths/min, peripheral oxygen saturation 90% on 2 liters/min by nasal cannula, diffuse expiratory wheezing, and moderate dyspnea. After treatment, respiratory rate is 22 breaths/min, saturation is 94% on the same oxygen setting, and wheezing is reduced. The patient reports easier breathing but has mild tremor. The note should show both the respiratory improvement and the adverse effect.

Putting C3 together
Document the baseline, intervention, comparable post-treatment findings, patient-reported response, objective response, tolerance, education, and next reassessment step.
C4: Develop assessment statements that explain what the available data support and plans that specify intervention, monitoring, communication, and follow-up.

The Assessment and Plan show your clinical reasoning

The Assessment and Plan sections are where the note moves beyond data collection. The Assessment explains what the findings support. The Plan explains what should happen because of those findings.

A strong Assessment is evidence based

Use language such as “findings support,” “findings are consistent with,” or “concern for” when the evidence supports a respiratory pattern but does not prove a final diagnosis.

Example:

Objective: Respiratory rate 30 breaths/min, bilateral expiratory wheezes, accessory-muscle use, peripheral oxygen saturation 88% on room air.
Supported Assessment: Increased work of breathing with airflow-obstruction findings and low oxygen saturation.
Unsupported Assessment: “Severe asthma exacerbation caused by pollen” when the history and diagnosis have not established that cause.

A strong Plan uses action words

Useful plan verbs include administer, monitor, reassess, educate, notify, recommend, clarify, and escalate. The plan should answer what will happen next rather than simply restating the problem.

Include monitoring and follow-up

A plan is incomplete if it says “give treatment” but does not explain what will be checked afterward. State what response will be monitored, when the patient will be reassessed, and which findings would require earlier provider notification or escalation.

Chronology matters

Chronology means the order in which events happened. The note should allow the reader to tell what was assessed first, what intervention occurred, how the patient responded, who was notified, and what remained to be done.

Board-style thinking: A good plan is not simply “continue treatment.” It should connect the intervention to a measurable reassessment and a clear failure or escalation trigger.
Putting C4 together
Make the Assessment fit the evidence, and make the Plan fit the Assessment. State the intervention, monitoring, communication, reassessment, and escalation needs.
C5: Document treatment refusal, adverse response, deterioration, provider notification, and record correction through an objective and transparent process.

Unexpected events need complete documentation

Treatment refusal

Patients may refuse treatment. Your note should describe the event objectively rather than judging the patient. Include what therapy was offered, the patient's current respiratory condition, the reason the patient gave, education provided, any required notification, and the follow-up plan.

Example refusal documentation:

“Scheduled nebulizer treatment offered. Patient states, ‘It makes me feel shaky and I do not want it right now.’ Respiratory rate 22 breaths/min, peripheral oxygen saturation 93% on 2 liters/min nasal cannula, mild bilateral wheezing present. Purpose and expected effects reviewed. Patient continues to decline. Nurse/provider notified according to policy. Reassessment planned.”

Adverse response

An adverse response is an unwanted or harmful effect that occurs during or after care. Examples may include chest pain, marked tachycardia, severe tremor, worsening shortness of breath, desaturation, dizziness, altered mental status, or paradoxical worsening of airflow.

Document the baseline, what treatment was being given, when the adverse response occurred, the new findings, what action you took, who was notified, and how the patient was reassessed.

Deterioration

If the patient worsens, the record should make the change visible. Include the new symptoms or findings, change from baseline, oxygen support, vital signs, work of breathing, mental status, actions taken, communication, and response.

Provider notification

Do not write only “provider aware.” Document the clinically important reason for notification, the time, who was contacted, the information reported, and the response or instruction when applicable under local policy.

Correcting the medical record

Mistakes can happen. Do not hide, erase, or secretly replace an error. Follow the approved correction process so the original record and correction remain transparent. Electronic records usually preserve an audit trail, which is a record of who entered or changed information and when.

An addendum is additional information entered after the original note. A late entry documents information after the expected documentation time. Both should follow facility policy and clearly distinguish the time of the care from the time the documentation was entered.

Safety priority: If the patient is deteriorating, treat the patient and communicate urgently first. Complete documentation immediately after the critical actions when feasible. Do not delay emergency care to create a perfect note.
Putting C5 together
Refusal, adverse response, deterioration, notification, and corrections must be documented objectively, completely, and transparently.
C6: Critique a respiratory note for omissions, unclear timing, inconsistent data, unsupported conclusions, failure to reassess, or failure to communicate the next safe step.

Can another clinician safely use this note?

One of the best ways to judge documentation is to imagine that you are the next respiratory therapist arriving several hours later. Could you understand what the problem was, what treatment was delivered, whether it worked, whether the patient had an adverse response, and what you are supposed to do next?

Common documentation problems

Problem Why it matters
Missing oxygen settingA saturation cannot be interpreted correctly without knowing the support being used.
No pretreatment baselineThere is nothing reliable to compare with the post-treatment findings.
No post-treatment reassessmentThe note cannot show whether therapy helped or caused harm.
Unsupported diagnosisThe conclusion may go beyond the evidence or the therapist's scope.
Unclear timingThe reader cannot tell whether the result occurred before or after therapy or deterioration.
Copied information that conflicts with current findingsThe record becomes unreliable and may mislead the next clinician.
No next stepThe team does not know what should be monitored, reassessed, or escalated.

The “tolerated well” problem

A note that says only “treatment given, tolerated well” is usually incomplete. It does not tell the next therapist what the patient looked like before therapy, whether breath sounds changed, whether oxygenation improved, whether heart rate increased, or whether the patient still needs the same treatment plan.

Critiquing an incomplete note

Incomplete note:

“Albuterol given. Patient better. Tolerated well.”

Questions you should ask:
  • What were the pretreatment findings?
  • What dose and delivery method were used?
  • What does “better” mean?
  • Were breath sounds, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and symptoms reassessed?
  • Was the oxygen setting unchanged?
  • Were adverse effects checked?
  • What happens next?

Use the note to support continuity of care

Continuity of care means that care remains connected as responsibility moves from one clinician or shift to another. Documentation supports continuity when the next person can understand the current problem, response to treatment, and next safe step without guessing.

Competency-exam thinking: When you critique a note, look for missing evidence, missing reassessment, unsupported conclusions, unclear timing, and failure to communicate what happens next.
Putting C6 together
Ask whether the note is accurate, specific, chronological, supported by evidence, complete enough to show treatment response, and clear about the next safe action.

Week 1.3: Make your clinical reasoning visible

Good documentation allows another clinician to reconstruct what happened. It connects the patient's report, your observations, your interpretation, your intervention, the patient's response, and the next action.

Use this sequence as you move into the PulmoLearn lesson: patient report → measured findings → supported interpretation → action → reassessment → communication → next step.

Before the weekly quiz, you should be able to...

  • identify what belongs in the Subjective, Objective, Assessment, and Plan sections of a respiratory SOAP note;
  • distinguish patient-reported symptoms from measured or directly observed findings;
  • recognize that the Assessment should be a supported respiratory interpretation rather than an unverified diagnosis;
  • recognize that the Plan includes treatment, monitoring, education, communication, reassessment, and escalation needs;
  • replace vague or judgmental wording with specific, objective descriptions;
  • identify the pretreatment and post-treatment data needed to evaluate response;
  • distinguish treatment delivery from treatment effectiveness;
  • recognize that “tolerated well” does not prove therapeutic benefit;
  • document treatment refusal using the patient's stated reason, education, notification, and follow-up plan;
  • document adverse response, deterioration, and provider notification objectively;
  • recognize risks from copied or outdated documentation; and
  • follow approved correction, addendum, and late-entry processes instead of hiding errors.

For the Unit 1 Competency Exam, take the next step

The competency exam will require you to use documentation as evidence of clinical reasoning rather than simply label the SOAP sections.

  • Build a complete SOAP note from a patient scenario and make the sections logically connect.
  • Identify which post-treatment findings are still needed before effectiveness can be claimed.
  • Recognize when an apparent improvement cannot be attributed to one treatment because another variable, such as oxygen support, changed at the same time.
  • Critique refusal documentation for missing patient condition, stated reason, education, notification, or reassessment.
  • Identify unsupported conclusions, missing timing, missing reassessment, and contradictions with current data.
  • Document deterioration and urgent communication without delaying patient care.
  • Choose wording that is clinically useful, neutral, and defensible.
  • Make the next monitoring, communication, or escalation step clear to the next clinician.
National Board for Respiratory Care connection: The Respiratory Therapy Examination expects you to gather and interpret patient information, evaluate treatment response, communicate clinically important findings, and support safe changes in the care plan. Clear documentation is how much of that reasoning becomes visible to the rest of the health-care team.
