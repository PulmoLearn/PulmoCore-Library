Pulmonary Assessment • 3.2 Competency Guide

Therapist-Driven Protocols, Severity Scoring & Care-Plan Modification

Learn how respiratory therapists use approved protocols to assess, initiate, modify, de-escalate, discontinue, or recommend respiratory care while staying within scope, documenting the rationale, matching therapy to the actual respiratory problem, and reassessing whether the plan is working.

Before you begin

A therapist-driven protocol gives qualified respiratory therapists defined authority to make specified respiratory-care decisions when approved criteria are met. That authority may include initiating, changing, decreasing, or discontinuing certain therapies—but only within the boundaries of the protocol, institutional policy, professional scope, competency, and required medical-staff authorization.

A protocol is not an automatic treatment schedule. The therapist still has to assess the patient, decide which respiratory problem is actually present, choose a modality that addresses that problem, check contraindications, evaluate the response, document the reasoning, and recognize when the patient is outside protocol limits.

Big idea for Week 3.2: Use the sequence assess → identify the problem → apply the approved criteria → choose the therapy → implement → evaluate → reassess → modify or escalate.

Core vocabulary: therapist-driven protocol, protocol authority, scope of practice, severity score, PRN, BID, TID, QID, Q4H, fraction of inspired oxygen, aerosol medication, airway clearance, lung expansion, ventilatory support, positive expiratory pressure, intrapulmonary percussive ventilation, incentive spirometry, continuous positive airway pressure, noninvasive ventilation, Implementation, Evaluation, Reassessment, de-escalation, protocol modification.

C1: Explain the purpose, scope, authorization, and safety limits of a therapist-driven respiratory care protocol.

What a therapist-driven protocol actually authorizes

A therapist-driven respiratory care protocol links specific assessment findings to specific approved actions. It may authorize a qualified respiratory therapist to perform defined assessment steps, select from approved modalities, adjust frequency or support, discontinue therapy when criteria resolve, and notify the provider when escalation or clarification is required.

The protocol also defines who may use it, which patients are eligible, what criteria support each action, what documentation is required, when reassessment must occur, and when the therapist must stop the protocol pathway and contact the provider or emergency team.

Protocol authority is bounded

The therapist remains responsible for practicing within professional scope, demonstrated competency, facility policy, the active protocol, and any required prescriber authorization. A protocol does not allow a therapist to invent a treatment that is not included or ignore a contraindication because the total score seems high.

Assessment comes before action

Before applying a protocol action, verify the patient, current clinical problem, relevant history, allergies, oxygen support, recent trends, respiratory therapies already in use, response to prior treatment, contraindications, and present physical findings.

The protocol does not remove judgment

Scoring tools make decisions more consistent, but the therapist must still recognize unreliable data, rapidly changing physiology, treatment intolerance, and emergency findings that a numeric score may not fully capture.

Instability overrides routine scoring

Severe respiratory distress, exhaustion, apnea, altered mental status, inability to protect the airway, hemodynamic instability, rapidly rising support requirements, or impending arrest requires immediate stabilization and escalation. Do not finish a routine score while the patient is failing.

Course rules versus clinical-site rules

This course uses specific severity ranges and a specific oxygen escalation threshold so students can practice structured reasoning. Actual facilities may use different criteria. In clinical practice, the active institutional protocol controls.

Clinical example: A patient previously scored 22 and received QID therapy. Two days later the patient is comfortable, on minimal oxygen, wheezing has resolved, cough is effective, and the new score is 4. Protocol care means reassessing each original indication and de-escalating appropriately—not automatically continuing the old schedule.
Putting C1 together
A protocol standardizes approved decisions. It does not replace bedside assessment, individual accountability, provider communication, or emergency escalation.
C2: Organize a complete patient report using demographics, chief complaint, relevant history, current assessment, diagnostic results, current respiratory therapies, response to therapy, and the weaning or modification plan.

A complete report answers eight questions

A respiratory patient report should be complete enough for the next clinician to understand the patient's current status and care plan without drowning the listener in unrelated information.

  1. Who is the patient? Include age and relevant admission or location context.
  2. Why is the patient here? State the chief complaint or main respiratory problem.
  3. What history changes interpretation? Include relevant diagnoses, home respiratory medications, tobacco or exposure history, baseline oxygen, functional status, and prior response to therapy.
  4. What is happening now? Report respiratory rate, heart rate, SpO2 and device, work of breathing, mental status, breath sounds, cough effectiveness, secretions, and tolerance.
  5. What do diagnostics show? Include relevant imaging, arterial blood gases, laboratories, pulmonary-function information, or cultures and state whether they are new or trending.
  6. What respiratory therapies are being delivered? Include modality, medication and dose if applicable, frequency, oxygen setting, and last treatment.
  7. How has the patient responded? Report benefit, no response, deterioration, tolerance, and adverse effects.
  8. What happens next? State the modification, weaning, reassessment, discharge, home-care, or escalation plan.

Relevant does not mean incomplete

Do not recite the entire chart. Include facts that change respiratory assessment or care. For example, chronic home oxygen, severe anxiety about masks, a weak cough, recent surgery, or prior bronchodilator response may matter more than unrelated remote history.

“Response to therapy” is not “therapy was given”

The phrase “Albuterol completed” describes treatment delivery. A response statement would say whether wheezing, dyspnea, respiratory rate, airflow, oxygenation, or adverse symptoms changed after the treatment.

Handoff test: If another therapist heard only your report, could they explain why each current therapy is being used, whether it is working, and what should happen next?
Putting C2 together
Build a concise but complete respiratory story from identity through the next modification or weaning decision.
C3: Classify respiratory documentation correctly as Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Planning, Implementation, Evaluation, or Reassessment within the course SOAPIER framework.

SOAPIER shows the whole clinical loop

LetterMeaningExample
SSubjective“My chest feels tight.”
OObjectiveRR 26/min, SpO2 88% on room air, expiratory wheezing
AAssessmentFindings support bronchospasm with hypoxemia
PPlanningAdminister protocol-approved bronchodilator and oxygen; reassess
IImplementationAlbuterol administered by nebulizer; oxygen started at 2 L/min nasal cannula

The source determines Subjective versus Objective

A patient's statement remains subjective even after it is written in the chart. “I feel less short of breath” is subjective. SpO2 94% on 2 L/min is objective.

Assessment is not an unsupported diagnosis

The respiratory therapist documents the interpretation supported by the findings: “retained secretions with ineffective cough,” “findings support bronchospasm,” or “atelectasis remains a lung-expansion indication.”

Implementation, Evaluation, and Reassessment are different

Implementation records what you actually did. Evaluation records the immediate result. Reassessment states what the new result means for future care and when the patient should be checked again.

Documentation trap: “Albuterol given; tolerated well” contains Implementation but provides little meaningful Evaluation and no Reassessment plan.
Putting C3 together
Use SOAPIER to show the reasoning path from patient report and measurement through action, result, and the next decision point.
C4: Calculate or interpret the course respiratory-care severity score and match the total score with PRN, BID/TID, QID, or Q4H plus PRN frequency ranges.

The score organizes severity; it does not choose every treatment

The course severity tool considers five areas separately: history and condition, oxygen therapy, airway clearance, lung expansion, and aerosol medication. Current findings are scored and combined to guide treatment frequency or intensity.

Course frequency ranges

Total scoreCourse frequency rangeMeaning
1–5PRNAs needed when an indication is present
6–15BID or TIDTwo or three times daily
16–25QIDFour times daily
Above 26Q4H plus PRNEvery 4 hours plus as needed

PRN means pro re nata, or as needed. BID means twice daily. TID means three times daily. QID means four times daily. Q4H means every four hours.

Score current data, not copied history

Verify present oxygen requirement, respiratory rate, breath sounds, cough, secretion burden, imaging, inspiratory ability, current therapy, and recent response. A score based on yesterday's patient can produce today's wrong treatment frequency.

Frequency and modality are two separate decisions

A score of 18 places the patient in the QID intensity range, but it does not prove the patient needs bronchodilator, airway clearance, lung expansion, and oxygen therapy. Each category still requires its own supporting indication.

Trend the score in both directions

A rising score can support increased frequency or further evaluation. A falling score may support de-escalation. But the patient must still be reassessed before a therapy is reduced or discontinued.

Do not let the score hide an emergency: Severe hypoxemia, apnea, altered mental status, inability to protect the airway, or hemodynamic instability can require immediate escalation before the score is completed.
Putting C4 together
Know the course ranges exactly, use current inputs, trend the score, and remember that the score guides frequency while individual findings determine which categories are indicated.
C5: Interpret oxygen-device capabilities, the minimum safe flow for a simple face mask, the clinical meaning of rising oxygen requirements, and the course FiO2 escalation threshold.

The amount of support required is part of the assessment

Low-flow nasal cannula

A low-flow nasal cannula is comfortable and useful for many stable patients with modest oxygen needs. Its delivered fraction of inspired oxygen, written as FiO2, is variable because room air is entrained around the cannula. The actual inspired concentration changes with respiratory rate, tidal volume, inspiratory flow, mouth breathing, fit, and patient demand.

Simple face mask

A simple face mask must receive enough oxygen flow to flush exhaled carbon dioxide from the mask. In this course, the common minimum is 5 L/min. Using a tightly fitted simple mask at an inadequately low flow can promote carbon dioxide rebreathing.

Target SpO2 does not equal stable oxygenation

If SpO2 is 92% but the patient has progressed from 2 L/min nasal cannula to a non-rebreather mask over two hours, the patient is worsening. The displayed saturation is being maintained only because support has increased substantially.

The course threshold of more than 44% FiO2

Under this course protocol, needing more than an estimated 44% FiO2 to maintain the target saturation triggers higher-level ventilatory-support evaluation. It is a decision point, not an automatic intubation rule.

The next support could involve high-flow oxygen, continuous positive airway pressure, bilevel noninvasive ventilation, or invasive ventilation depending on the cause, gas exchange, work of breathing, fatigue, mental status, airway protection, hemodynamics, and response to less invasive support.

Why the 44% value is approximate

Low-flow oxygen systems do not deliver a perfectly fixed FiO2. Treat the course threshold as a protocol trigger while still assessing the actual patient and device performance.

Clinical reasoning: When oxygen needs rise, do more than change the device. Verify the saturation signal, reassess work of breathing, ventilation, mental status, airway protection, and the mechanism of deterioration.
Putting C5 together
Know device limits, document the support level with every saturation, and treat rising oxygen requirement as worsening patient data.
C6: Differentiate an oxygenation problem, bronchospasm, retained-secretions problem, lung-expansion problem, and ventilatory-support problem from the assessment findings.

Name the physiologic problem before naming the treatment

ProblemSupporting findingsTherapy direction
OxygenationLow SpO2 or PaO2, increasing oxygen requirement, gas-exchange impairmentOxygen/support assessment and treatment of the cause
BronchospasmExpiratory wheeze, prolonged expiration, reduced airflow, known reactive/obstructive disease, objective bronchodilator responseBronchodilator assessment
Retained secretionsThick/copious sputum, coarse breath sounds, weak or ineffective cough, mucus plugging, difficulty expectoratingAirway-clearance pathway
Lung expansionAtelectasis, low inspiratory capacity, postoperative splinting, diminished basilar aeration, recurrent collapseLung-expansion pathway
Ventilatory supportRising PaCO2, respiratory acidosis, fatigue, shallow ventilation, severe work of breathing, altered mental status, apnea, inability to protect airwayHigher-level support evaluation

Coarse crackles are not automatically secretion retention

Coarse breath sounds plus thick sputum and a weak cough strongly support retained secretions. Crackles from pulmonary edema, fibrosis, or consolidation may not improve with airway-clearance therapy. Correlate the sound with cough, sputum, imaging, and response.

Pneumonia is not automatically a bronchodilator indication

Albuterol relaxes airway smooth muscle. Pneumonia causes infection and inflammation of lung tissue and does not, by itself, prove bronchospasm. If the patient has no wheeze, prolonged expiration, obstructive history, or bronchodilator response, routine bronchodilator therapy is less supported.

Atelectasis is primarily a lung-expansion problem

Collapsed or poorly recruited alveoli support a lung-expansion strategy. If a mucus plug and secretion retention are also present, airway clearance may be needed too. Do not assign both categories unless both problems are supported.

Use negative findings too

No wheezing weakens the bronchospasm indication. A strong effective cough and minimal secretions weaken the airway-clearance indication. Clear imaging weakens the lung-expansion indication.

Key habit: Before selecting a modality, say one sentence: “The problem I am treating is _____, supported by _____.”
Putting C6 together
Separate oxygenation, airflow, secretion, expansion, and ventilation problems rather than treating a diagnosis label.
C7: Select a therapy category or modality that addresses the identified problem while considering patient cooperation, interface tolerance, safety, and device limitations.

A correct indication can still have the wrong modality

Once the respiratory problem is identified, choose the simplest safe modality that can address it. Patient ability, airway status, hemodynamics, interface tolerance, secretion burden, inspiratory demand, and contraindications may change the device choice.

Airway-clearance options

  • Coached cough or huff cough: useful when the patient can participate and generate sufficient expiratory flow.
  • Postural drainage and percussion: use positioning and external percussion to assist secretion movement when indicated and tolerated.
  • Oscillating positive expiratory pressure: combines expiratory resistance with oscillation; requires patient participation and adequate expiratory effort.
  • High-frequency chest-wall oscillation: uses an external vest to mobilize secretions and requires less mouthpiece coordination.
  • Suctioning: removes secretions when the patient cannot clear them effectively through the airway.
  • Mechanical insufflation-exsufflation: assists cough in selected patients with severe cough-muscle weakness.
  • Intrapulmonary percussive ventilation: provides small rapid gas pulses that can mobilize secretions and provide positive-pressure expansion.

Lung-expansion options

Incentive spirometry requires an alert, cooperative patient who can perform sustained inspiration. Continuous positive airway pressure, or CPAP, provides continuous distending pressure that can help recruit or stabilize alveoli. Other positive-pressure or hyperinflation methods may be selected based on severity and patient capability.

Aerosol medication delivery

When bronchodilator therapy is indicated, delivery may use a nebulizer or metered-dose inhaler, commonly shortened to MDI, depending on patient coordination, severity, inspiratory ability, interface, available equipment, and discharge plan. Correct MDI technique can be very effective; a nebulizer is not automatically superior.

Bland aerosol is not a bronchodilator

A bland aerosol may hydrate dry secretions and support mucociliary function. It does not relax bronchial smooth muscle and does not replace a strategy that actually mobilizes retained lower-airway secretions.

Check contraindications and tolerance

Before positive-pressure, oscillatory, or aggressive clearance therapy, consider untreated pneumothorax or air-leak risk, hemodynamic instability, active bleeding, recent surgery, nausea or aspiration risk, severe fatigue, inability to cooperate, and modality-specific contraindications.

Clinical reasoning: Patient ability changes the modality, not the underlying indication. The patient may still need airway clearance even if they cannot use an active PEP device.
Putting C7 together
Match the device mechanism to the verified problem, then check whether this particular patient can safely and effectively use it.
C8: Recognize when multiple protocol categories are independently supported and when one dual-purpose modality may safely address more than one indication.

One patient can have several different respiratory problems

Consider an alert patient with SpO2 88% on room air, bilateral expiratory wheezing, thick sputum with a weak cough, and right-lower-lobe atelectasis on chest radiograph.

This one patient has four independently supported categories:

  • Oxygenation: SpO2 88% on room air.
  • Aerosol medication: expiratory wheezing supports bronchospasm assessment.
  • Airway clearance: thick sputum plus weak cough supports impaired secretion clearance.
  • Lung expansion: radiographic atelectasis supports reduced alveolar recruitment.

Do not count the same clue as every problem

Pneumonia may produce hypoxemia, consolidation, and secretions, but pneumonia alone does not prove bronchospasm, airway-clearance failure, or a need for a particular device.

Dual-purpose therapy can reduce unnecessary duplication

Intrapulmonary percussive ventilation, commonly shortened to IPV, combines oscillatory secretion mobilization with positive-pressure lung expansion. In an appropriate alert, cooperative patient with both retained secretions and atelectasis, one IPV treatment may address both indications.

But each indication remains separate

Using one dual-purpose modality does not mean the two problems become one problem. Document the evidence for secretion retention and the evidence for atelectasis separately. Reassess both responses separately.

Dual-purpose does not mean universally safe

Confirm airway stability, patient cooperation, hemodynamic tolerance, interface fit, barotrauma risk, and modality-specific contraindications. If IPV is unsafe or poorly tolerated, select other therapies that address the same underlying indications.

Efficiency principle: Avoid stacking multiple therapies when one safe modality can meet two verified needs—but never force one device to treat a problem outside its mechanism.
Putting C8 together
Identify every supported problem independently, then decide whether separate or dual-purpose therapy is safest and most efficient.
C9: Initiate, increase, decrease, discontinue, or recommend therapy according to the approved protocol, current severity, treatment response, and patient trajectory.

Protocol care moves in both directions

Students often think protocols are mainly used to start or increase therapy. Equally important is knowing when to reduce or stop therapy that is no longer indicated.

Initiate

Start a protocol therapy when current findings meet the approved criteria, the patient is an appropriate candidate, and required safety checks are satisfied.

Increase

Increase frequency, intensity, or support when severity rises, the response is inadequate, oxygen or ventilatory needs increase, or the patient moves into a higher protocol range—provided the patient remains within the protocol pathway.

Decrease

Reduce frequency or support when the score and the specific indication improve and the patient remains stable during reassessment.

Discontinue

Stop a scheduled therapy when its indication has resolved and the protocol allows discontinuation. A therapy should not remain simply because it was appropriate on admission.

Recommend

When the needed action is outside protocol authority, communicate a recommendation using the objective findings, response trend, and specific therapy or escalation pathway supported by the patient.

De-escalation case

On admission, a patient had a severity score of 20 and received QID oxygen, IPV, and Albuterol. On day 3, the score is 4, SpO2 is 96% on 1 L/min, breath sounds are clear, cough is strong, imaging is clear, and wheezing has resolved.

The correct response is stepwise de-escalation: perform a monitored room-air trial with documented reassessment; discontinue scheduled IPV when secretion and expansion indications have resolved; and change Albuterol to PRN or the appropriate home plan if it remains indicated.

Why not stop everything at once?

A low score supports less intensive care, but safe de-escalation still requires reassessment, separate review of each indication, communication, and a follow-up plan.

Clinical reasoning: De-escalation is an active clinical decision. Unnecessary therapy can cause adverse effects, waste time, and hide the fact that the patient's needs have changed.
Putting C9 together
Modify care according to current findings and trajectory—not according to what the patient needed yesterday.
C10: Perform and document focused pre-treatment and post-treatment reassessment, including benefit, no response, adverse response, and the need for additional evaluation or escalation.

A treatment is not evaluated until you compare before and after

Pre-treatment assessment

Choose measurements that reflect the problem you are treating. Common variables include symptoms, respiratory rate, heart rate, SpO2 and oxygen setting, work of breathing, breath sounds, airflow, cough effectiveness, sputum, inspiratory capacity, mental status, and device tolerance.

Post-treatment assessment

Reassess the same meaningful variables under comparable conditions when possible. If wheezing improves after Albuterol, that supports bronchodilator benefit. If secretions mobilize and cough becomes more effective after airway-clearance therapy, that supports clearance benefit.

Benefit

Document the measurable improvement: less wheezing, lower respiratory rate, improved inspiratory capacity, improved SpO2 on the same or less oxygen, better secretion clearance, improved breath sounds, or improved comfort.

No response

No improvement should prompt questions: Was the technique correct? Was the device appropriate? Is the original respiratory problem still the best explanation? Does the patient need another modality or higher-level evaluation?

Adverse response

Bronchodilators may cause tachycardia, tremor, palpitations, or chest discomfort. Positive-pressure or oscillatory therapies may produce desaturation, fatigue, discomfort, hemodynamic change, air leak, or barotrauma. Oxygen interfaces may fail to meet demand or be poorly tolerated.

Reassessment determines the next action

The result may support continuing, spacing out, increasing, stopping, changing the technique, verifying the diagnosis, or escalating care.

Documentation rule: “Tolerated well” is not enough. State what happened to the respiratory problem and whether any adverse effect occurred.
Putting C10 together
Compare the same meaningful findings before and after treatment, then let the response guide the next protocol decision.
C11: Communicate a concise protocol-based recommendation using objective findings, response to prior therapy, the relevant score or criteria, and a specific reassessment plan.

A recommendation should show the evidence and the next decision point

A useful recommendation answers four questions:

  1. What is the respiratory problem?
  2. Which objective findings support it?
  3. What protocol action or recommendation follows?
  4. When and how will the patient be reassessed?

Include the response to prior therapy

If the patient had three scheduled bronchodilator treatments with no wheezing before or after, that response matters. If IPV improved sputum mobilization and basilar aeration, that response matters too.

Include the relevant score or criteria, not just the score

“Score is 18” is incomplete. State that the score is in the QID range and identify which categories have supporting findings.

Example recommendation

“The patient scores 18, which is in the course QID range. SpO2 is 88% on room air, bilateral expiratory wheezing is present, cough is weak with thick sputum, and imaging shows right-lower-lobe atelectasis. These findings support oxygen, bronchodilator assessment, airway clearance, and lung expansion. I recommend protocol-directed therapy for all four supported categories with focused reassessment after treatment and repeat scoring at the protocol interval.”

Escalation communication

If the patient exceeds protocol limits, communicate the instability, support level, gas exchange, work of breathing, mental status, response, and the higher-level support being considered.

Communication goal: Another clinician should be able to see why your recommendation is reasonable and what data will determine the next change.
Putting C11 together
State the problem, evidence, protocol basis, recommended action, and reassessment plan concisely.
C12: Clarify an unsupported or potentially inappropriate respiratory order professionally while continuing to advocate for the therapy that is supported by the patient assessment.

Professional clarification is part of patient safety

A provider order should not be ignored, silently refused, or followed uncritically when the current assessment makes the indication unclear or suggests another respiratory problem needs attention.

Use reassessment before challenging the order

Verify the patient, symptoms, breath sounds, respiratory pattern, cough, sputum, oxygenation, history, medication use, imaging, and prior response. Make sure your concern is based on current data rather than assumption.

Unsupported scheduled Albuterol case

A patient with pneumonia has an order for Albuterol QID. Assessment shows coarse crackles, thick sputum, and a weak cough but no wheezing, prolonged expiration, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease history, or known bronchodilator response.

The evidence supports a retained-secretion/airway-clearance problem. It does not clearly support bronchospasm. The appropriate response is to reassess, communicate the findings, clarify the indication for scheduled Albuterol, and recommend the secretion-directed therapy supported by the patient.

Do not overstate your conclusion

The absence of wheezing does not prove that bronchodilator therapy can never be appropriate. There may be history or objective data you have not yet identified. That is why the response is professional clarification—not confrontation.

A useful communication structure

Assessment: “The patient has coarse crackles, thick sputum, and a weak ineffective cough. There is no current wheezing or prolonged expiration.”

Concern: “I do not currently find evidence of bronchospasm to support scheduled QID Albuterol.”

Recommendation: “The findings support airway-clearance therapy. Can we clarify the bronchodilator indication and consider protocol-directed secretion clearance with reassessment?”

If the patient is unstable, safety comes first

Order clarification should not delay emergency treatment for severe hypoxemia, apnea, respiratory fatigue, altered mental status, hemodynamic instability, or another urgent problem.

Professional principle: Effective protocol practice is neither blind compliance nor unsupported refusal. It is reassessment, evidence-based recommendation, collaborative communication, documentation, and follow-up.
Putting C12 together
Clarify the unsupported part of the plan while continuing to advocate for the therapy that the current assessment actually supports.

Week 3.2: The protocol does not make the decision—you do

The protocol provides structure, criteria, and authority. The respiratory therapist supplies the current assessment, identifies the actual physiologic problem, checks safety, chooses the appropriate modality, evaluates the response, and decides whether the care plan should continue, increase, decrease, stop, or move beyond the protocol pathway.

current assessment → supported problem category → severity/frequency → modality → safety check → implementation → evaluation → reassessment → modification/communication

Before the weekly quiz, you should be able to...

  • explain that a therapist-driven protocol authorizes qualified therapists to assess, initiate, modify, or discontinue defined therapies within approved criteria;
  • identify the eight major components of a complete respiratory patient report;
  • distinguish Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Planning, Implementation, Evaluation, and Reassessment;
  • recognize that an immediate pre/post-treatment comparison belongs in Evaluation;
  • recall the course severity ranges: 1–5 PRN, 6–15 BID/TID, 16–25 QID, and above 26 Q4H plus PRN;
  • identify a score of 4 as PRN, 18 as QID, and 30 as Q4H plus PRN;
  • recognize that acceptable SpO2 on rapidly increasing oxygen still represents worsening support need;
  • explain that more than 44% FiO2 is a course escalation trigger for higher-level support assessment, not automatic intubation;
  • recall that a simple face mask commonly requires at least 5 L/min;
  • identify thick secretions, coarse crackles, and a weak ineffective cough as a secretion-clearance indication;
  • select IPV as a possible dual-purpose modality for an appropriate cooperative patient with both retained secretions and atelectasis; and
  • clarify a scheduled bronchodilator when bronchospasm is unsupported and recommend the therapy supported by the assessment.

For the Unit 3 Competency Exam, take the next step

  • Classify all independently supported protocol categories in one patient instead of choosing only one “main” therapy.
  • Recognize oxygenation, aerosol medication, airway clearance, and lung expansion as four supported categories when low SpO2, wheezing, retained secretions/weak cough, and atelectasis coexist.
  • Interpret a falling severity score together with resolution of the individual treatment indications.
  • Perform a monitored room-air trial with reassessment when oxygen need has fallen to minimal support and the patient is stable.
  • Discontinue scheduled IPV when secretion and expansion indications have resolved rather than continuing it because it was previously appropriate.
  • Reduce Albuterol to PRN or the appropriate home plan when bronchospasm has resolved and continued therapy remains appropriate.
  • Recognize that low score does not mean abruptly stop all respiratory care without reassessment or communication.
  • Distinguish secretion retention from bronchospasm in a pneumonia patient with coarse crackles, thick sputum, and weak cough but no wheezing or prolonged expiration.
  • Clarify an unsupported scheduled bronchodilator and recommend evidence-based airway-clearance therapy.
  • Communicate the objective findings, protocol criteria, response to prior therapy, action requested, and next reassessment point.
