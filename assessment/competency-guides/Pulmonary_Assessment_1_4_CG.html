Pulmonary Function Testing Basics

Learn how respiratory therapists prepare patients and equipment for pulmonary function testing, coach an acceptable forced vital capacity maneuver, recognize invalid results, and decide when testing should continue, be repeated, or stop for safety.

Before you begin

Pulmonary function testing is not one single test. It is a group of procedures that answer different questions about airflow, lung volume, gas transfer, respiratory-muscle strength, ventilatory capacity, and exercise limitation.

Big idea for Week 1.4: Before interpreting a pulmonary function result, first decide whether the test was the right test, whether the patient was prepared correctly, whether the equipment was working properly, whether the maneuver was performed correctly, and whether the patient remained safe.

Vocabulary you will begin using this week: spirometry, forced vital capacity, forced expiratory volume in one second, lung volumes, residual volume, total lung capacity, diffusing capacity, maximum inspiratory pressure, maximum expiratory pressure, maximum voluntary ventilation, cardiopulmonary exercise testing, acceptability, repeatability, calibration, quality control, contraindication.

Different tests answer different questions

The term pulmonary function testing refers to several tests that evaluate how the lungs and breathing system work. You should not think of them as interchangeable. Each test focuses on a different part of respiratory function.

Test What it evaluates Why it may be ordered
SpirometryHow much air a patient can move and how quickly it can be exhaledTo evaluate airflow obstruction and basic ventilatory patterns
Lung-volume testingVolumes that simple spirometry cannot directly measure, including residual volume and total lung capacityTo confirm restriction or identify air trapping and hyperinflation

Maximum expiratory pressureExpiratory-muscle strengthWhen cough strength or expiratory weakness is a concern

Spirometry does not measure everything

Spirometry is one of the most common pulmonary function procedures. It measures air that the patient can move in and out of the lungs. It does not directly measure the air that remains in the lungs after the strongest possible exhalation. That remaining air is called residual volume.

Lung volumes help confirm restriction

Total lung capacity is the total amount of air in the lungs after the deepest possible inhalation. If true restriction is suspected, total lung capacity is important because simple spirometry alone cannot confirm that total lung capacity is actually reduced.

Diffusing capacity asks whether gas can cross the lung membrane

The diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide, commonly abbreviated later as DLCO, estimates how well gas moves from the alveoli into pulmonary capillary blood. It is not the same thing as oxygen saturation and does not simply measure airflow.

Pressure tests evaluate respiratory-muscle strength

Maximum inspiratory pressure, commonly shortened to MIP, measures inspiratory-muscle strength. Maximum expiratory pressure, commonly shortened to MEP, measures expiratory-muscle strength. They answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable values.

Clinical reasoning: Start with the physiologic question. Are you trying to assess airflow, lung size, gas transfer, muscle strength, ventilatory capacity, or exercise response? Then choose the test that answers that question.

C2: Explain forced vital capacity as a maximal inhalation followed by an immediate, forceful, and sustained exhalation while recognizing that residual volume remains in the lungs.

What is forced vital capacity?

Forced vital capacity, commonly shortened to FVC, is the total amount of air a patient can exhale forcefully after taking the deepest possible breath in.

The maneuver begins near total lung capacity, meaning the lungs are as full as possible. The patient then exhales as hard, fast, and completely as possible toward residual volume, the air that remains in the lungs even after maximal exhalation.

The patient never exhales every milliliter of air

Residual volume cannot be voluntarily exhaled. That means forced vital capacity is not the total amount of air physically present in the lungs. It is the portion that can be moved between maximal inhalation and maximal exhalation.

The first second also matters

Forced expiratory volume in one second, commonly written later as FEV1, is the amount of air exhaled during the first second of the forced maneuver. A hesitant or slow start can falsely reduce this value, which is why the patient must begin exhaling immediately and forcefully.

A low forced vital capacity does not automatically mean restriction

Forced vital capacity can be low because the lungs are truly restricted, but it can also be low because the patient did not inhale fully, stopped exhaling too early, leaked around the mouthpiece, had pain, became tired, misunderstood the directions, or could not produce a maximal effort.

This is one of the most important lessons in pulmonary function testing: quality first, interpretation second.

Quiz connection: Forced vital capacity is a maximal inhalation followed by an immediate, hard, sustained exhalation. Residual volume remains in the lungs and is not measured directly by simple spirometry.

C3: Review the order, indication, baseline condition, demographics, precautions, and patient readiness needed before pulmonary function testing.

A valid test begins before the patient blows into the device

Before testing, verify the patient's identity, the requested procedure, and the clinical question. A test should not be performed simply because a machine is available. You need to know why the test is being done and whether the patient is safe and able to perform it.

Review the patient's current condition

Assess current symptoms, baseline respiratory status, recent worsening, chest pain, dizziness, recent procedures, and whether the patient is acutely ill. A cooperative patient is not automatically a safe patient.

Screen for precautions and contraindications

A contraindication is a condition that makes a procedure unsafe or inappropriate. Pulmonary function laboratories follow facility policies for conditions such as unstable cardiovascular symptoms, severe acute illness, recent surgery or certain procedures, significant hemoptysis, pneumothorax risk, or other situations in which maximal pressure and effort may be unsafe.

Demographic information affects predicted values

Pulmonary function reports often compare a patient's measured values with reference values predicted for someone with similar characteristics. Accurate age, standing height, and sex as required by the selected reference equation and facility policy are important inputs.

If height is entered incorrectly, the computer may calculate an incorrect predicted value even when the patient performs a perfect maneuver. A computer can calculate from bad information just as easily as from correct information.

Important principle: A technically excellent breathing maneuver can still produce a misleading report if the wrong patient, wrong demographics, wrong test, or wrong clinical conditions are entered.

Assess whether the patient can perform the maneuver

The patient must understand the instructions, form a tight seal around the mouthpiece, inhale fully, begin exhaling rapidly, and continue the effort long enough. Hearing difficulty, language barriers, cognitive limitations, pain, weakness, and fatigue may require changes in teaching or may limit test quality.

Medication and activity instructions depend on the purpose of testing

Some tests require specific preparation related to bronchodilators, smoking, exercise, meals, oxygen, or other factors. Follow the order, laboratory protocol, and facility policy. Do not independently withhold a needed respiratory medication simply because you think it might affect the test.

Competency-exam thinking: If the entered height is wrong, the raw measured volume may be real, but the percent-predicted interpretation can be wrong. Verify demographic inputs before interpreting the report.
Putting C3 together
Verify identity, indication, demographics, precautions, baseline condition, and ability to perform the test before beginning.
C4: Prepare pulmonary function equipment using correct setup, infection-control, calibration-verification, and quality-control practices according to manufacturer and facility policy.

The equipment must be trustworthy too

Pulmonary function testing depends on both patient performance and equipment performance. If the device is set up incorrectly or is measuring volume inaccurately, even a perfect patient effort can produce an invalid result.

Calibration and calibration verification

Calibration is the process of adjusting a device so its measurement matches a known standard. Calibration verification checks whether the device is still measuring accurately within the required range. Exact procedures depend on the equipment, manufacturer instructions, laboratory standards, and facility policy.

Quality control

Quality control refers to routine checks that help ensure the testing system remains accurate and reliable. This may include equipment checks, verification procedures, leak checks, software review, maintenance, environmental checks when required, and documentation of problems.

Infection prevention

Patients breathe forcefully through pulmonary function equipment, so infection-control procedures are important. Use approved filters, mouthpieces, cleaning processes, hand hygiene, personal protective equipment when indicated, and laboratory procedures designed to prevent cross-contamination.

Do not “work around” a failed check

If calibration verification or another required quality check fails, do not continue patient testing and assume the results are close enough. Troubleshoot according to policy and manufacturer guidance before producing clinical results.

Clinical reasoning: Before asking whether a low value is caused by disease, first ask whether the equipment and testing process were capable of producing a reliable value.

Know why equipment setup, infection prevention, calibration verification, and quality control are essential before clinical interpretation.
C5: Coach an acceptable forced vital capacity maneuver using clear patient-centered instruction, demonstration, positioning, tight mouthpiece seal, maximal inhalation, rapid start, and continued exhalation.

Good coaching is part of the measurement

Spirometry is highly effort dependent. The patient's result depends partly on how well the respiratory therapist explains and coaches the maneuver. A confusing instruction can create poor data even when the patient's lungs are unchanged.

A simple coaching sequence

  1. Explain and demonstrate. Tell the patient exactly what will happen and show the maneuver when helpful.
  2. Position safely. Seat the patient upright with stable support according to laboratory policy.
  3. Use a tight mouthpiece seal. Air leaking around the mouthpiece lowers measured volume.
  4. Inhale completely. The patient must begin from the deepest possible breath.
  5. Blast the air out immediately. The start should be rapid and forceful, not hesitant.
  6. Keep going. Encourage the patient to continue exhaling until the maneuver is complete or the test should be stopped for safety.

Why the start matters

A slow or hesitant start especially affects the first second of the test and can reduce confidence in the forced expiratory volume in one second. Coaching should encourage an immediate explosive start.

Why the end matters

The patient must continue exhaling long enough to approach the true end of forced expiration. Stopping too soon can make forced vital capacity look falsely low.

Useful coaching language: “Take the biggest breath you possibly can. Seal your lips tightly around the mouthpiece. Now BLAST the air out hard and fast—keep going, keep going, keep going...”
Clinical reasoning: The therapist is not just an observer during spirometry. Clear, consistent coaching is part of producing a valid measurement.
Putting C5 together
Be able to coach the full sequence from maximal inhalation through a rapid start and sustained exhalation.
C6: Recognize hesitation, slow start, cough, leak, incomplete inhalation, early termination, glottic closure, obstruction of the mouthpiece, and variable effort and explain how each can distort results.

When a low value is caused by the maneuver

Not every low pulmonary function value reflects disease. Some low values are caused by the way the maneuver was performed. This is why you must watch the patient and evaluate the tracing rather than accepting the printed number automatically.

Problem How it can affect the result
Hesitation or slow startCan reduce the volume exhaled in the first second and distort early flow measurements
CoughCan interrupt flow and distort the tracing, especially early in the maneuver
Leak or poor mouth sealAllows exhaled air to escape without being measured
Incomplete inhalationThe patient does not begin at true maximal lung volume, lowering measured forced vital capacity
Early terminationThe patient stops before exhalation is complete, making forced vital capacity falsely low
Glottic closureClosure at the upper airway interrupts airflow even though air remains in the lungs
Mouthpiece obstructionTongue, teeth, or poor positioning can block airflow into the device

Early termination is a major exam concept

Imagine that a patient performs three maneuvers that all begin strongly but stop after only about two seconds. The computer prints a forced vital capacity that is 58% of predicted. The number may look abnormal, but the maneuver did not continue long enough to measure the patient's true exhalable volume.

The appropriate response is to correct the technique problem and obtain acceptable testing when safe—not to immediately label the patient as having restrictive lung disease.

Quality rule: If the maneuver is invalid, the correct clinical response is usually to correct the problem and repeat the maneuver when safe—not to interpret the bad data.

C7: Evaluate maneuver acceptability and repeatability before interpreting raw or percent-predicted values and distinguish a quality-related low forced vital capacity from a pattern that warrants complete pulmonary function testing.

Acceptability and repeatability come before diagnosis

Acceptability means an individual maneuver meets the required quality expectations. Repeatability means repeated acceptable maneuvers agree closely enough that the result is reproducible.

A single apparently abnormal maneuver should not be treated as strong evidence if the effort is poor or inconsistent. The therapist first asks whether the maneuver was technically usable.

Raw values versus percent predicted

A raw value is the actual measured result, such as a forced vital capacity of 3.2 liters. A percent-predicted value compares the measured value with a reference value calculated from the patient's demographic information and the selected reference equation.

Percent predicted can be misleading when demographics are wrong, the reference equation is inappropriate, or the maneuver itself is poor quality.

A low forced vital capacity is a finding, not a final diagnosis

A low forced vital capacity may reflect true restriction, but it can also be caused by air trapping, incomplete inhalation, early termination, pain, weakness, misunderstanding, or submaximal effort.

If acceptable, repeatable spirometry shows a low forced vital capacity, complete lung-volume testing may be needed to determine whether total lung capacity is actually reduced. This helps confirm or rule out true restriction.

Three-step interpretation habit:
1. Was the maneuver acceptable?
2. Was the result repeatable?
3. Only then: what physiologic pattern might the valid data support?
Competency-exam thinking: A low forced vital capacity after obvious early termination should be repeated. A low forced vital capacity after acceptable, repeatable spirometry may justify complete lung-volume testing to evaluate true restriction.
Putting C7 together
Do not interpret physiology until you have decided that the testing process produced acceptable, repeatable data.
C8: Monitor patient tolerance, apply stop criteria, initiate appropriate assessment and escalation, and resume testing only when the patient is stable and policy supports continuation.

The test is never more important than the patient

Pulmonary function maneuvers can require maximal effort. Some patients may cough, feel lightheaded, become fatigued, or experience other symptoms. The respiratory therapist must continuously observe the patient and stop testing when concerning symptoms develop.

Know the difference between expected discomfort and a warning sign

Temporary coughing or mild lightheadedness may occur during forceful breathing. However, new chest pressure, near-fainting, severe dizziness, marked distress, or another concerning change should not be ignored simply because the test is incomplete.

Chest pressure and presyncope require stopping

Presyncope means feeling as though you may faint. If a patient develops new chest pressure and presyncope during testing, stop the maneuver, assess the patient, obtain appropriate vital signs and monitoring, communicate the change, and escalate according to facility policy and the patient's condition.

Safety priority: Do not encourage a patient with new chest pressure, severe dizziness, or near-fainting to “finish one more maneuver.” Stop testing and assess the patient.

Testing resumes only when appropriate

A stopped test should not automatically be restarted after a few minutes. Resume only when the patient is stable, the concerning symptoms have been evaluated, and the facility's protocol or provider direction supports continuation.

Document what happened

Document the maneuver being performed, the patient's symptoms, relevant measured findings, the decision to stop, the immediate assessment, who was notified, the response, and whether testing was discontinued or later resumed.

Clinical example

During repeated forced vital capacity maneuvers, a patient suddenly reports chest pressure and says, “I feel like I might pass out.” The correct response is to stop testing immediately, support the patient safely, reassess vital signs and symptoms, obtain additional help or escalation as indicated, and document the event. Completing the test is no longer the priority.

Putting C8 together
Monitor the patient throughout testing, recognize when symptoms require the procedure to stop, assess and escalate appropriately, and resume only when it is safe and allowed by policy.

Week 1.4: Quality first, interpretation second

Pulmonary function testing is only useful when the right test is selected, the patient and equipment are prepared correctly, the maneuver is performed well, and the patient remains safe.

Use this sequence as you enter the PulmoLearn lesson: identify the clinical question → select the test → verify patient readiness → verify equipment quality → coach the maneuver → judge acceptability and repeatability → interpret only valid data → stop when safety requires.

For the Unit 1 Competency Exam, take the next step

The competency exam will ask whether you can determine if pulmonary function data are trustworthy and what should happen next.

  • Recognize an artificially low forced vital capacity caused by early termination or incomplete effort.
  • Correct the quality problem and repeat testing when safe instead of immediately assigning a disease pattern.
  • Recognize when acceptable, repeatable low forced vital capacity warrants complete lung-volume testing to evaluate restriction.
  • Stop pulmonary function testing when new chest pressure, presyncope, or another serious symptom develops.
  • Prioritize patient assessment and escalation over completion of the procedure.
  • Recognize that incorrect height or other demographic data can invalidate percent-predicted interpretation even when the raw maneuver is technically good.
  • Distinguish patient-performance error from equipment or quality-control failure.
  • Recommend the next diagnostic or assessment step based on valid data rather than one isolated printed number.
