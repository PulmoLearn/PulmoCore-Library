Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to distinguish between X-ray, CT, MRI, PET scan, V/Q scan, and echocardiogram imaging modalities; describe the RT role during TEE procedures; identify consolidation, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, and pulmonary edema on a chest X-ray; interpret basic V/Q scan findings; apply radiation safety principles; and select the appropriate imaging modality for a given clinical scenario.

1. Know the imaging modalities
Understand what each test shows, how it works, and when it is used clinically.
2. Interpret the chest X-ray
Recognize consolidation, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, pulmonary edema, and normal findings.
3. Understand advanced imaging
Know the RT role during echocardiography/TEE and interpret basic V/Q scan results.
4. Apply radiation safety
Follow time, distance, shielding, dosimetry, contrast, MRI, transport, and sedation precautions.
5. Validate image quality
Check projection, rotation, inspiration, penetration, motion, coverage, identity, and comparison before interpreting.
6. Describe before diagnosing
State location, laterality, density, distribution, volume effect, pleura, and associated findings.
7. Evaluate devices early
Identify high, low, or right-mainstem ET tube position and complete reassessment after correction.
8. Act according to urgency
Withhold unsupported conclusions, repeat inadequate studies, or escalate immediately when the patient is unstable.

Imaging Modalities — What Each Test Shows

Each imaging modality uses a different physical principle to reveal different types of tissue. Selecting the right study for the right clinical question is as important as interpreting the result. Knowing these distinctions guides appropriate test ordering and patient preparation.

X-RAY

Principle: Ionizing radiation passes through the body. Dense structures (bone, fluid, metal) absorb X-rays and appear white. Air appears black.

Best for: Bone structures, lung opacities, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, cardiomegaly, endotracheal tube position, line placement

Limitations: Two-dimensional. Soft tissue detail poor. Contrast (swallowed or injected) needed to visualize internal GI or vascular structures.

CT

Principle: Multiple X-ray beams rotate 360° around the body, creating transverse "slices." Computer reconstructs 3D images from the cross-sections.

Best for: Brain bleeding, stroke, pulmonary embolism (CT-PA), aortic dissection, trauma, detailed lung/lobe anatomy, metastatic disease

RT relevance: Lobar and segmental anatomy on CT guides bronchoscopy approach. CT-PA is the gold standard for PE diagnosis.

MRI

Principle: Magnetic fields align hydrogen protons; radiofrequency pulses knock them out of alignment. The energy released as they realign is detected to create images. No ionizing radiation.

Best for: Soft tissue (brain tumors, spinal cord, ligaments, joints), cardiac MRI, demyelinating disease

Limitations: Slow (30–90 min). Loud. Claustrophobic. Cannot use with ferromagnetic metal implants. Patients requiring life support need MRI-compatible equipment.

ECHO

Principle: High-frequency sound waves (ultrasound) reflect off cardiac structures. The returning echoes create real-time images of the moving heart.

Best for: Cardiac function, ejection fraction, valve disease, wall motion abnormalities, pericardial effusion, emergency hemodynamic assessment

No ionizing radiation. Portable — can be performed at the bedside. Real-time imaging of heart motion.

PET

Principle: Nuclear medicine. Radioactive tracer (typically FDG — glucose analog) is injected. Metabolically active cells (especially cancer) absorb more tracer, emitting detectable gamma rays.

Best for: Cancer staging and detection, recurrence assessment, infection localization, brain function mapping

Note: Often combined with CT (PET-CT) to correlate metabolic activity with anatomical location.

V/Q

Principle: Nuclear medicine. Two-part study: inhaled radionuclide shows VENTILATION; injected radionuclide shows PERFUSION. V/Q mismatch (ventilated but not perfused) suggests PE.

Best for: Pulmonary embolism workup when CT-PA is contraindicated (renal failure, contrast allergy, pregnancy)

RT role: Support the ordered ventilation-delivery system and patient monitoring according to facility scope, nuclear-medicine procedure, and policy.

CT Scan & MRI — Advanced Structural Imaging

CT and MRI provide far greater anatomical detail than plain X-ray. Each has specific clinical advantages, patient preparation requirements, and safety considerations the RT must know — particularly when managing patients who require monitoring during these studies.

CT Scan — Key Points

  • Fast: Chest CT takes ~10 seconds — ideal for unstable patients
  • High-resolution lung anatomy: Identifies individual lung lobes, segments, nodules, ground-glass opacities, consolidation, air trapping
  • CT Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA): Directly evaluates pulmonary arterial filling defects and is a common primary study for PE when the patient can safely undergo transport and iodinated contrast.
  • Brain CT: First-line for acute stroke and head trauma — rapidly identifies hemorrhage
  • Contrast and renal considerations: Review prior reactions and renal function. When CTPA risk is unacceptable, choose an appropriate alternative such as V/Q imaging when clinically suitable.
  • Radiation exposure: Chest CT = ~7 mSv (vs. CXR = 0.02 mSv)

MRI — Key Points

  • No ionizing radiation — uses magnetic field and radiofrequency pulses
  • Superior soft tissue contrast — brain, spinal cord, ligaments, tumors
  • MRI safety screening required: Verify every implant and device. Some are MRI conditional under specific field strength, position, programming, and monitoring requirements; others are unsafe.
  • Duration: 30–90 minutes — patients with dyspnea or anxiety may need sedation
  • RT equipment: Ventilators, IV pumps, and monitoring must be MRI-compatible (non-ferromagnetic)
  • Gadolinium contrast (MRI contrast) used for some studies — different from CT iodinated contrast

Echocardiogram & Transesophageal Echocardiogram (TEE)

The echocardiogram is the primary tool for real-time cardiac structural and functional assessment. Respiratory therapists frequently assist with echocardiography, particularly during TEE — where conscious sedation monitoring is a direct RT responsibility.

External Echocardiogram (TTE)

Transducer placed on chest wall. Non-invasive. No sedation required. Can be performed portably at the bedside during emergencies. Limited image quality in patients with obesity, COPD (hyperinflated lungs), or chest wall abnormalities.

Emergency use: Immediately assesses cardiac tamponade, cardiac arrest with PEA (pulseless electrical activity), massive PE, acute valvular emergencies.

Trans-Esophageal Echo (TEE)

Flexible probe with ultrasound transducer placed in the esophagus (directly behind the heart). Superior image quality — no lung or rib interference. Requires conscious sedation.

RT responsibilities during TEE: Support airway and sedation monitoring according to assigned role and policy, including oxygenation, ventilation, hemodynamics, consciousness, suction readiness, and response to airway compromise.

Cardiac Structures Assessed by Echocardiogram

Atria

Left and right atrial size. Enlarged left atrium suggests mitral valve disease, AFib, or left ventricular failure. Enlarged right atrium suggests right heart strain (pulmonary hypertension, PE, right heart failure).

Ventricles

Ejection Fraction (EF): Interpret the reported value using the laboratory reference and clinical context. A reduced EF supports impaired systolic function (heart failure with reduced EF). Wall motion abnormalities indicate ischemia or infarction territories. LV hypertrophy from chronic hypertension.

Valves

Aortic valve: Stenosis (high gradient, thickened leaflets) vs. regurgitation (blood flowing backward). Mitral valve: MVP, stenosis, regurgitation. Doppler color flow shows direction and velocity of blood through valves.

Great Vessels & Pressures

Aortic root diameter. Pulmonary artery pressure estimation (RVSP = right ventricular systolic pressure) — elevated in pulmonary hypertension. Pericardial effusion around the heart. IVC size and collapsibility (preload estimate).

PET Scan — Metabolic Activity Imaging

Positron Emission Tomography (PET) scans belong to the field of nuclear medicine. Unlike anatomical imaging (CT, MRI, X-ray), PET reveals metabolic activity — making it uniquely powerful for cancer detection, because cancer cells consume far more glucose than normal cells.

How PET Works — Nuclear Medicine Principle

A radioactive tracer (most commonly FDG — fluorodeoxyglucose, a radioactive glucose analog) is injected intravenously. Cells that are metabolically active (especially cancer cells) absorb more FDG. The radioactive fluorine-18 emits positrons, which collide with electrons and produce gamma rays detected by the PET scanner ring. Areas of high uptake appear "hot" (bright) on the image.

Clinical Applications
  • Cancer staging: Whole-body survey for metastatic disease — sees all metabolically active cancer at once
  • Treatment response: Does the tumor still have metabolic activity after chemo/radiation?
  • Recurrence detection: Distinguish scar tissue from active tumor
  • PET-CT fusion: Combines metabolic information (PET) with anatomical location (CT) in one study
  • Brain mapping: Epilepsy focus localization, dementia type differentiation
Important Limitation — False Positives and Negatives: Any metabolically active tissue can show high FDG uptake — not just cancer. Infection, inflammation, recent surgery, and even normal brain and heart tissue are "hot." Conversely, slow-growing or well-differentiated tumors may show low FDG uptake (false negative). PET findings must always be interpreted in clinical context.

Ventilation/Perfusion (V/Q) Scan — Diagnosing Pulmonary Embolism

The V/Q scan is the primary alternative to CT-PA for pulmonary embolism diagnosis. It directly visualizes the match — or mismatch — between where air goes (ventilation) and where blood flows (perfusion) in the lungs. A classic PE produces a region that is ventilated normally but has absent perfusion.

Ventilation Scan (V)

Patient inhales a radioactive gas or aerosol (technetium-99m DTPA aerosol or xenon-133 gas). Images show which areas of the lung are receiving airflow.

RT role: Support the ordered inhaled-radiopharmaceutical delivery process, infection prevention, patient coaching, respiratory support, and monitoring according to facility scope and policy.

Conceptual normal: Relatively even, symmetric distribution interpreted with technique, radiology criteria, and clinical context.

Perfusion Scan (Q)

A different radioactive tracer is injected intravenously (technetium-99m macroaggregated albumin — MAA). The particles lodge in small pulmonary capillaries proportionally to blood flow. Images show which areas receive blood flow.

PE finding: Wedge-shaped area of absent or reduced perfusion — the clot blocks blood flow to that lung segment while ventilation continues normally.

Mismatch pattern: A ventilated region with reduced perfusion increases concern for pulmonary embolism, but interpretation also depends on clinical probability, the chest radiograph, defect size and number, and other causes.

The ABCDE Systematic Approach

A — Airway: Is the trachea midline? Tracheal deviation toward the side of pathology suggests atelectasis/fibrosis. Deviation away suggests tension pneumothorax or large effusion. Check ET tube position — tip should be 3–5 cm above the carina.
B — Bones and Soft Tissue: Rib fractures, vertebral abnormalities, soft tissue emphysema (air in subcutaneous tissue), breast shadows, pacemaker leads, surgical clips.
C — Cardiac: Cardiothoracic ratio (heart width : chest width). Normal = <50% on PA view. >50% = cardiomegaly. Check cardiac silhouette borders — loss of a border suggests adjacent lung pathology (silhouette sign).
D — Diaphragm: Are both hemidiaphragms visible and well-defined? Elevated right hemidiaphragm = atelectasis or subphrenic pathology. Blunted costophrenic angles = pleural effusion or fluid.
E — Everything Else: Lung fields — bilateral symmetry? Any opacities, hyperlucencies, or vascular markings changes? Check for tubes, lines, and foreign bodies. Lung margins — is there a visible pleural edge (pneumothorax)?

Key Radiographic Findings

CONSOLIDATION
Airspace filling — white opacity in lung parenchyma
White (opaque) area within the lung field. Air-space is filled with fluid, pus, blood, or cells. Key finding: air bronchograms — dark air-filled bronchi visible within the white opacity (proves the bronchi are patent and it is the alveoli that are filled). Seen in: pneumonia, aspiration, ARDS, hemorrhage, tumor.
PNEUMOTHORAX
Air in the pleural space — collapsed lung
Visible lung edge (visceral pleural line) with no lung markings beyond it. The space between the lung edge and chest wall is darker (air, not lung tissue). Key clinical concern: tension pneumothorax — tracheal deviation away from the affected side, distended neck veins, hemodynamic collapse. Emergency needle decompression before waiting for CXR confirmation.
PLEURAL EFFUSION
Fluid in the pleural space — blunted costophrenic angle
Blunted (filled-in) costophrenic angle — the sharp V-shape at the lung base is replaced by a curved "meniscus sign." Large effusions show a complete opacification of one hemithorax with tracheal deviation away. Bilateral effusions in CHF appear relatively symmetric. Causes: CHF, pneumonia (parapneumonic), malignancy, TB, trauma, post-cardiac surgery.
PULM. EDEMA
Bilateral, perihilar/batwing pattern — CHF
Bilateral interstitial or alveolar opacities with perihilar (around the hila) distribution — "butterfly" or "batwing" pattern. Kerley B lines: horizontal lines at the lung bases representing fluid in the interlobular septa. Cardiomegaly (CTR >50%) usually present. Pleural effusions common. Redistribution of blood flow to upper lobes (cephalization) in early CHF.
ATELECTASIS
Opacity with volume loss
Look for displaced fissures, elevated hemidiaphragm, crowded vessels or ribs, hilar movement, or mediastinal shift toward the affected side. Volume loss helps distinguish atelectasis from consolidation.
HYPERINFLATION
Increased volume and obstructive air trapping
Flattened diaphragms, increased lung volume, hyperlucency, and a narrow elongated cardiac silhouette may support COPD or severe air trapping when correlated with examination.
SUPINE LIMITS
Pleural air and fluid may redistribute
A supine pneumothorax may produce a deep sulcus sign or subtle basal hyperlucency rather than an apical line. A supine effusion may appear as diffuse hazy opacity rather than a classic meniscus.
NORMAL
Normal chest X-ray criteria
Clear, symmetric lung fields. Trachea midline. Cardiac silhouette <50% of chest width. Sharp costophrenic angles bilaterally. No pleural effusion, pneumothorax, or airspace opacities. Pulmonary vascular markings visible and symmetric. Diaphragm well-defined. ET tube (if present) tip 3–5 cm above carina.

Radiation Safety — Protecting Yourself and Your Patients

Medical radiation is cumulative over a career. Healthcare workers who are frequently present during X-ray and fluoroscopy procedures face higher occupational exposure than the general public. The three pillars of radiation protection — distance, shielding, and time — must be applied consistently.

D
Distance — Minimum 8 Feet (2.4 m)

Radiation intensity follows the inverse square law: double the distance = ¼ the exposure. At 8 feet from the primary X-ray beam, occupational exposure drops dramatically. During portable imaging, non-essential personnel should leave the exposure area or maximize distance according to facility radiation-safety policy and operator instruction.

Practical rule: Do not remain beside the patient during exposure unless direct care cannot safely pause. Use distance, barriers, and shielding according to the procedure and local policy.
L
Lead Apron with Thyroid Guard

When proximity to the radiation source is unavoidable (fluoroscopy, emergency imaging), lead aprons (0.25–0.5 mm lead equivalency) protect radiosensitive organs (gonads, bone marrow, breast tissue). Thyroid glands are particularly radiosensitive — a thyroid collar/shield must be worn whenever a lead apron is donned.

Key point: Lead aprons do NOT protect the neck and thyroid unless the thyroid collar is also worn. Many radiation-related thyroid cancers in healthcare workers are attributed to inadequate thyroid shielding.
B
Barrier Protectors for Continuous Imaging

During fluoroscopy, interventional procedures, or continuous bedside imaging, portable lead glass barriers provide shielding when stepping back 8 feet is not feasible. The RT stands behind the barrier while managing the airway or monitoring the patient.

TEE and fluoroscopy: When monitoring a patient during an interventional procedure with continuous fluoroscopy, always position yourself behind the barrier unless direct patient intervention is required.
C
Cumulative Exposure — Career Risk

Radiation effects are cumulative. Occupational limits and monitoring requirements are governed by regulation and institutional radiation-safety policy. Wear the assigned dosimeter correctly and review reported exposure. Healthcare workers wear dosimetry badges (film badge or TLD) to track exposure over time. Risk assessment is essential — each exposure should be justified by clinical benefit.

Patient risk-benefit: For patients, the risk of diagnostic radiation must be weighed against the benefit of the information obtained. For RT students and staff: consistent protection habits developed early protect you across a 30-year career.

RT administers inhaled radionuclide (ventilation phase) and monitors the patient throughout.
Situation 4 — Radiation Safety: Step back 8 feet during portable CXR. If unable to leave — lead apron AND thyroid collar (never one without the other). Inverse square law: every doubling of distance reduces exposure 4-fold.

FAQ

Radiology Assessment — Common Questions

What is the "silhouette sign" on a chest X-ray?

The silhouette sign occurs when two structures of the same radiographic density (whiteness) touch — making the border between them disappear. On a CXR, the heart, aorta, and diaphragm are normally distinct because adjacent aerated lung surrounds them. When air is replaced by fluid, pus, or mass (consolidation), the border of the adjacent structure is lost. Example: Right middle lobe pneumonia causes loss of the right heart border (the right heart and the consolidated RML become the same density and merge). This helps localize which lobe or segment is affected even before CT.

How is V/Q scan different from CT-PA for diagnosing PE?

CT-PA (CT Pulmonary Angiography) directly visualizes the pulmonary vessels — a clot is seen as a filling defect in the contrast-enhanced vessel. It is fast, definitive, and allows assessment of clot burden and location. It requires IV iodinated contrast and delivers ~7 mSv of radiation to the chest. V/Q scan indirectly suggests PE by showing areas with normal ventilation but absent perfusion — it doesn't directly see the clot. V/Q delivers lower radiation (especially to the fetus in pregnancy) and can be done without IV contrast. CT-PA is more sensitive and specific and is the first-line study when feasible. V/Q is preferred when CT-PA is contraindicated.

What is the cardiothoracic ratio and how is it measured?

The cardiothoracic ratio (CTR) is the widest diameter of the cardiac silhouette divided by the widest internal diameter of the chest (measured at the same level). Normal CTR on a PA (posterior-anterior) view: <0.50 (less than 50%). CTR >0.50 = cardiomegaly. Important caveat: AP (anterior-posterior) portable films magnify the heart due to beam divergence — they appear to show cardiomegaly even with a normal heart. The CTR should only be formally assessed on PA films taken in the radiology department, not on portable AP ICU films.

Why does cardiac output affect how well the echocardiogram evaluates certain structures?

Cardiac output significantly impacts echocardiographic findings. Patients with poor cardiac output (cardiogenic shock) may have underestimated ejection fraction because the heart is squeezing against high afterload. Patients who are volume-depleted have very different ventricular filling patterns. Valvular gradients (the pressure difference across a valve) are flow-dependent — a normal cardiac output creates a different gradient than a low output state. The IVC (inferior vena cava) size and how much it collapses with breathing estimates preload (volume status). These dynamic factors require the echocardiographer to integrate the findings with the patient's overall hemodynamic state.

When should the RT NOT follow the 8-foot distance rule during an X-ray?

The only justified exception is when direct patient intervention is occurring and cannot be interrupted — for example, if the patient is coding and a portable CXR is taken during resuscitation efforts. In this scenario, the RT managing the airway cannot step back. The solution: don lead apron AND thyroid collar, and position yourself on the side furthest from the primary X-ray beam (behind the patient if possible). However, for routine portable CXRs in stable patients, stepping back 8 feet is always the correct answer and takes less than 5 seconds — clinical care can safely pause for the exposure duration.

What is the first sentence of a chest-radiograph interpretation?

A technical-quality statement identifying projection and position, rotation, inspiration, penetration, motion, coverage, identity, and whether comparison is available.

Why should the therapist describe before diagnosing?

Location, laterality, density, distribution, volume effect, pleural findings, devices, and interval change are objective. They reduce overcalling and help separate consolidation, atelectasis, pleural disease, edema, and technical artifact.

What must be reassessed after moving an ET tube?

Tube depth and security, bilateral chest rise and breath sounds, waveform capnography, oxygenation, airway pressures, exhaled volumes, patient response, and repeat confirmation as required.

When should imaging be withheld or repeated?

When projection, rotation, inspiration, penetration, motion, coverage, identity, or device visibility prevents the clinical question from being answered. State the limitation and choose repeat imaging, another projection, ultrasound, CT, bedside reassessment, or urgent intervention as appropriate.

Summary — Radiologic Assessment at a Glance

Modalities Quick Reference: X-ray = bones/lungs/tubes. CT = brain bleed/PE/trauma (fast). MRI = soft tissue/spinal cord (slow, no metal). Echo = cardiac function/tamponade (bedside). PET = cancer metabolism. V/Q = PE when CT contraindicated.
CXR ABCDE: Airway (trachea, ETT). Bones/Soft tissue. Cardiac (CTR <50%). Diaphragm (costophrenic angles). Everything else (lungs, lines, foreign bodies).
CXR Findings: Consolidation = white opacity + air bronchograms. PTX = visible pleural line, absent markings. Effusion = blunted costophrenic angle, meniscus. Edema = bilateral butterfly + cardiomegaly + Kerley B lines.
Imaging Safety: Minimize time, maximize distance, use barriers and assigned shielding, wear dosimetry correctly, verify contrast and MRI risk, and preserve respiratory support during transport.
Quality Before Pathology: Projection, rotation, inspiration, penetration, motion, coverage, identity, time, and prior comparison determine which conclusions are valid.
Device Rule: Review tubes and lines early. Correct malposition and reassess the whole patient; the action is incomplete until position and physiology are revalidated.
