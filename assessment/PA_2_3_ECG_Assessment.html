ECG Assessment — Equipment, Interpretation, Chest Pain & Artifact

The electrocardiogram is the most powerful non-invasive cardiac diagnostic tool available at the bedside. Respiratory therapists read ECGs daily — to assess cardiac rhythm during bronchoscopy, to recognize ischemia triggering a COPD exacerbation, or to identify why a patient's hemodynamics are deteriorating. This lesson builds systematic ECG assessment skills from equipment to interpretation to chest pain diagnosis.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to identify clinical indications for ECG monitoring, distinguish between cardiac monitor and 12-lead ECG systems, correctly place limb and precordial leads, interpret ECG grid measurements, name each wave component and what it represents, apply the 5-step systematic analysis, characterize normal sinus rhythm, identify major rhythm abnormalities, recognize ischemia vs. injury vs. infarction findings on the 12-lead, and identify and correct common ECG artifacts.

1. Equipment and lead placement
Know the difference between 3-lead, 5-lead, and 12-lead systems and where each electrode belongs.
2. ECG grid and wave anatomy
Convert boxes to time and voltage, and identify P wave, QRS complex, T wave, and intervals.
3. Systematic 5-step interpretation
Apply the regularity → rate → P:QRS ratio → QRS width → PR interval framework to any rhythm strip.
4. Chest pain and artifact recognition
Distinguish ischemia, injury, and infarction on the 12-lead. Identify and eliminate artifact sources.
5. Prepare and validate
Verify identity, skin preparation, electrode contact, lead identity, paper speed, gain, and patient position.
6. Correlate electricity with circulation
Use pulse, pressure, mentation, perfusion, symptoms, and multiple data streams before assigning urgency.
7. Correct and reacquire
Resolve artifact or lead reversal and restore reliable surveillance rather than silencing or ignoring the signal.
8. Communicate validated findings
Report symptom timing, patient condition, lead pattern, comparison, support, and requested escalation.

ECG Indications — When Does the Patient Need Monitoring?

The ECG is ordered when clinical findings suggest a cardiac cause for the patient's symptoms, or when cardiac monitoring is needed to guide treatment and detect deterioration.

01 Chest Pain / Pressure

Radiation to arm, jaw, or back; pressure, squeezing, or tightness. May be atypical in women and diabetic patients.

→ Rule out MI / ischemia

02 Palpitations

Fluttering, racing, or skipping heartbeat; awareness of heartbeat. May be intermittent or sustained.

→ Rule out arrhythmia

03 Syncope / Pre-syncope

Fainting, near-fainting, or sudden unexplained dizziness. Sudden onset without warning is especially concerning.

→ Rule out cardiac etiology

04 Dyspnea

At rest or with minimal exertion; orthopnea or paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea. May indicate cardiac failure.

→ Assess cardiac component

05 Diaphoresis

Unexplained sweating unrelated to exertion or fever. Often accompanies chest pain in ACS presentations.

→ Autonomic activation sign

06 Edema

Bilateral lower extremity pitting edema; may include jugular venous distension (JVD) and ascites.

→ Rule out right heart failure

07 Pre-op Clearance

Cardiac risk assessment required before any surgical procedure. Baseline ECG documents current status for comparison.

→ Obtain when indicated by procedure, risk, symptoms, or policy

08 Procedural Monitoring

Bronchoscopy, procedural sedation, or mechanical ventilation. Continuous rhythm surveillance required by AARC guidelines.

→ Continuous rhythm surveillance

Cardiac Symptoms
  • Chest pain / pressure / tightness — radiation to arm, jaw, or back suggests MI
  • Palpitations — awareness of own heartbeat, "fluttering," skipped beats
  • Syncope or pre-syncope — fainting or near-fainting; may indicate arrhythmia
  • Diaphoresis — unexplained sweating, especially with chest discomfort
Respiratory and Systemic Symptoms
  • Dyspnea at rest or minimal exertion — can indicate cardiac failure or PE
  • Lower extremity edema — may indicate right or left heart failure
  • Cyanosis — suggests severe desaturation requiring cardiac evaluation
  • Any hemodynamic instability — hypotension, tachycardia without obvious cause
RT-Specific Monitoring: Bronchoscopy, moderate sedation, unstable transport, mechanical ventilation, and other high-risk situations may require continuous ECG and hemodynamic surveillance according to patient condition, institutional policy, procedure standards, and the monitoring environment.

ECG Equipment — Monitors, Electrocardiographs & Oscilloscopes

Three categories of equipment are used for cardiac monitoring in respiratory care. Each serves a different clinical purpose, uses a different number of leads, and produces different information.

Bedside Patient Monitor
Continuous Multi-Parameter
  • Displays ECG, SpO₂, capnography & BP simultaneously
  • 3-lead or 5-lead continuous rhythm monitoring
  • Audible & visual alarms for rate limits and arrhythmias
  • Color-coded vitals for at-a-glance assessment
  • Standard in ICU, step-down, and PACU units
Cardiac Telemetry
Wireless Ambulatory ECG
  • Small wireless transmitter worn on patient chest
  • Real-time transmission to central nursing station
  • 3-lead or 5-lead electrode setup on chest wall
  • Patient can ambulate freely — not tethered to bedside
  • Used on step-down and cardiac telemetry floors
12-Lead ECG Machine
Diagnostic ECG with Printed Report
  • 10 electrodes → 12 simultaneous electrical views
  • Produces printed strip with automated interpretation
  • Standard: 25 mm/sec speed, 10 mm/mV calibration
  • Required for chest pain, MI workup, pre-op evaluation
  • Portable unit brought to the patient bedside
SystemLeadsElectrodesPrimary UseRT Application
Oscilloscope / Cardiac Monitor1–3 leads displayed3 electrodes (RA, LA, LL)Continuous rhythm monitoringICU, bronchoscopy suite, transport monitoring
Cardiac Telemetry1–3 leads (wireless)3–5 electrodes on chest wall, wireless transmitterContinuous ambulatory rhythm monitoringStep-down unit, cardiac floors — patient can ambulate freely
12-Lead Electrocardiograph12 simultaneous leads10 electrodes (4 limb + 6 chest)Complete cardiac electrical evaluationChest pain assessment, pre-procedure clearance, MI diagnosis
Key Distinction: A cardiac monitor shows rhythm in real time but is not diagnostic for MI alone. A 12-lead ECG captures a diagnostic snapshot from 12 electrical perspectives. It supports recognition of ischemia, injury, infarction, and conduction patterns; NSTEMI requires integration with symptoms, serial ECGs, and cardiac biomarkers.

Patient Preparation, Skin Preparation & Signal Quality

Reliable interpretation begins before the first waveform appears. Verify the patient, explain that the ECG records rather than delivers electricity, preserve privacy, position the patient comfortably, and reduce skin-electrode impedance.

StepActionWhy It Matters
1. Verify and explainUse two identifiers, explain the procedure, expose only the needed area, and support the torso and limbs.Prevents wrong-patient data and reduces motion or anxiety.
2. Prepare the skinClip excessive hair if needed; remove oil or lotion; dry perspiration; gently abrade loose surface cells according to policy.Improves adhesion and lowers impedance.
3. Choose stable sitesAvoid wounds, bony prominences, large muscle groups, wet skin, and excessive respiratory motion when possible.Reduces wandering baseline and somatic artifact.
4. Inspect suppliesReplace dry or expired electrodes, check cables and snaps, and secure lead wires.Prevents intermittent contact and signal loss.

The ECG Grid — Time, Voltage, and Wave Anatomy

Every ECG measurement depends on understanding the grid. The horizontal axis measures time; the vertical axis measures voltage (amplitude).

Time (Horizontal Axis)
  • 1 small box = 0.04 seconds
  • 5 small boxes = 1 large box = 0.20 seconds
  • 5 large boxes = 1 second
  • 30 large boxes = 6 seconds
Voltage/Amplitude (Vertical Axis)
  • 1 small box = 0.1 mV
  • 2 small boxes = 0.2 mV
  • 10 small boxes = 1 mV (standard calibration)
  • 5 mm = 0.5 mV; ST significance depends on lead, sex, age, symptoms, contiguous pattern, and institutional criteria

Wave Components — What Each Deflection Represents

P
Wave — Atrial Depolarization
Small rounded hump before the QRS. Represents electrical activation of both atria. Should be upright and uniform in Lead II, one per QRS. Normal duration: <0.12 sec. Normal amplitude: <2.5 mm.
Q
Wave — Initial Septal Depolarization
Small downward deflection at the START of the QRS. May be absent normally. Significant (pathological) Q wave: >0.04 sec wide or >25% of R wave height — indicates prior myocardial infarction.
R
Wave — Ventricular Depolarization (Upward)
The tall upward deflection — most prominent feature of the QRS. Represents ventricular activation. R wave height and progression across V1→V6 is diagnostically important.
S
Wave — Terminal Ventricular Depolarization (Downward)
Downward deflection immediately after the R wave. Normal QRS duration (Q through S): <0.12 seconds (<3 small boxes). Wide QRS (>0.12 sec) indicates ventricular origin or bundle branch block.
T
Wave — Ventricular Repolarization
Broader rounded hump after the QRS. Should be upright and asymmetric. Inverted T waves indicate ischemia. Peaked T waves suggest hyperkalemia.
ST
Segment — Isoelectric Baseline
From end of S wave to start of T wave. Should lie at the isoelectric baseline. Elevation ≥1 mm in ≥2 contiguous leads = myocardial injury (STEMI pattern). ST depression = ischemia or NSTEMI.

The 5-Step Systematic ECG Analysis

Begin with the patient and tracing quality. Then apply the same descriptive steps in the same order before assigning a rhythm label.

Step 0 — Patient and tracing quality: Verify pulse, responsiveness, pressure, perfusion, symptoms, lead contact, selected lead, speed, gain, and reproducibility.
1
Regularity — Is the rhythm regular or irregular?
Compare all R-R intervals. Regular: all intervals within 2 small boxes. Irregular: intervals vary more than 2 small boxes. If irregular → use the 6-second method for rate.
2
Rate — What is the heart rate?
Regular: 300 ÷ large boxes between R waves  |  Regular (precise): 1500 ÷ small boxes between R waves  |  Any rhythm: Count QRS in 6 seconds × 10  |  Sequence method: 300–150–100–75–60–50
3
P to QRS Ratio — Is there one P wave before each QRS?
Normal: 1 P wave before each QRS (1:1 ratio). Missing P waves = junctional or ventricular origin. Multiple P waves per QRS = heart block. No P waves + irregular = atrial fibrillation.
4
QRS Width — Is the QRS narrow or wide?
Narrow (<0.12 sec): Usually supraventricular conduction through the His-Purkinje system. Wide (≥0.12 sec): May reflect ventricular origin, bundle-branch block, aberrancy, pacing, metabolic effects, or pre-excitation; width alone does not establish the diagnosis.
5
PR Interval — Is AV conduction normal?
Measured from start of P wave to start of QRS. Normal: 0.12–0.20 seconds (3–5 small boxes). Short (<0.12) = pre-excitation (WPW). Prolonged (>0.20) = 1st degree AV block. Variable = 2nd or 3rd degree block.

Place the 5 Steps in the Correct Sequence

Are the R-R intervals consistent, or does the rhythm vary?
Is there exactly one P wave before each QRS complex?
Measure from start of P wave to start of QRS — is it 0.12–0.20 sec?
Using large box, small box, or 6-second method — what is the beats per minute?
Measure the QRS complex — is it less than or greater than 0.12 seconds (3 small boxes)?

Normal Sinus Rhythm — The Baseline for All Comparison

Every rhythm is defined by comparing it to NSR. When all five steps fall within normal limits, the rhythm is normal sinus rhythm.

StepNSR CriterionIf Abnormal → Suspect
1. RegularityRegular — R-R intervals consistent (±2 small boxes)Arrhythmia, AFib, PVCs, blocks
2. Rate60–100 beats per minute<60 = bradycardia; >100 = tachycardia
3. P:QRS RatioOne P wave before each QRS — upright, uniform in Lead IIMissing P = junctional; multiple P = block; no P = AFib/VFib
4. QRS Width<0.12 seconds (<3 small boxes) — narrow≥0.12 sec = BBB, ventricular rhythm, accessory pathway
5. PR Interval0.12–0.20 seconds (3–5 small boxes)>0.20 = 1st degree AV block; variable = 2nd/3rd degree block

Abnormal Rhythms — Recognition and Response

Abnormal rhythms are identified by comparing each of the 5 steps against NSR criteria.

Sinus Bradycardia
<60 bpm
Regular, P before each QRS, narrow QRS, normal PR. Rate <60. Causes: vagal stimulation, beta-blockers, hypothyroidism. Treat if symptomatic (atropine, pacing).
Sinus Tachycardia
>100 bpm
Regular, P before each QRS, narrow QRS, normal PR. Rate >100. Causes: pain, fever, hypoxia, hypovolemia, PE. Treat the underlying cause.
Atrial Fibrillation
Irreg. irreg.
Irregularly irregular. No visible P waves — replaced by chaotic "f" waves. Narrow QRS unless BBB. Rate varies 60–160. Risk: stroke from atrial thrombus. Rate control or rhythm control + anticoagulation.
Atrial Flutter
Sawtooth F waves
Regular "F" waves at 250–350/min (sawtooth baseline). Ventricular rate depends on AV block ratio (2:1, 3:1, 4:1). QRS narrow. Flutter waves are organized and regular; AFib is chaotic.
SVT
150–250 bpm
Regular, narrow QRS, rate 150–250 bpm. P waves hidden in or after QRS. Sudden onset/offset. Assess symptoms and hemodynamic stability, confirm tracing quality, notify the team, and follow the appropriate rapid-tachycardia pathway. A narrow QRS supports a supraventricular mechanism but does not replace full assessment.
Ventricular Tachycardia
>100 bpm, wide
Regular, wide bizarre QRS (>0.12 sec), rate >100 bpm, no visible P waves. A wide QRS supports ventricular origin but is not by itself diagnostic; patient stability and full rhythm features remain essential. VT may occur with or without a pulse. Pulse and signs of instability determine the emergency pathway; activate the appropriate response rather than selecting treatment from the strip alone.
Ventricular Fibrillation
No QRS — chaos
Completely chaotic, disorganized waveforms — no recognizable QRS. Patient pulseless. CPR + Defibrillation immediately. VFib is a shockable rhythm.
Asystole
Flatline
Complete electrical silence — flat line on monitor. No P waves, no QRS. Patient pulseless. Confirm the patient, leads, gain, and a second lead while activating the pulseless emergency pathway. Asystole is not a shockable rhythm.

Diagnosing Chest Pain — Ischemia, Injury, and Infarction

The 12-lead ECG allows localization of cardiac pathology and differentiation of the three stages of coronary artery disease presentation.

1. Ischemia — Inverted T Waves

What is happening: Reduced blood flow — cells are alive but electrically abnormal due to oxygen deprivation.

ECG finding: T wave inversion in affected leads.

Location clue: V1–V4 → anterior. II, III, aVF → inferior.

Context required: T-wave inversion may reflect ischemia but also strain, conduction abnormalities, electrolyte changes, or normal variants. Compare symptoms, contiguous leads, prior ECGs, and biomarkers.

2. Myocardial Injury — ST Elevation

What is happening: Active severe ischemia — cells are injured but not yet dead. This is the STEMI pattern — a cardiac emergency.

ECG finding: ST segment elevation ≥1 mm in ≥2 contiguous leads.

Pattern recognition: Evaluate new ST elevation in anatomically contiguous leads using sex-, age-, and lead-specific criteria, symptoms, acquisition quality, and comparison data. Follow institutional STEMI criteria.

Time-sensitive emergency: New contiguous ST elevation with compatible symptoms requires immediate communication and activation of the institutional acute coronary pathway.

3. Myocardial Infarction — Pathological Q Waves

What is happening: Complete infarction — myocardial tissue has died. Dead zone cannot conduct electricity → deep Q waves.

ECG finding: Pathological Q waves: >0.04 sec wide AND >25% of R wave height in ≥2 contiguous leads.

NSTEMI: Elevated troponin + symptoms, but NO ST elevation. ST depression or T wave changes.

Context required: Pathologic Q waves may indicate prior or established infarction but do not determine current acuity alone.

Acute Coronary Symptoms: Prompt 12-Lead, Serial Comparison & Closed-Loop Communication

Sinus rhythm on telemetry does not exclude myocardial ischemia or injury. Obtain a diagnostic 12-lead promptly while assessing stability and continuing monitoring. Do not delay acquisition for troponin or an exhaustive history.

Communicate: symptom and onset, vital signs, oxygen or respiratory support, validated lead changes, comparison with prior or serial tracings, patient stability, and the requested urgent response.

ECG Artifact — Recognizing and Eliminating False Signals

Artifact is any electrical signal on the ECG that does not originate from the heart. Every RT should be able to identify the source and correct it before reporting or acting on an abnormal tracing.

Wandering Baseline

Appearance: The isoelectric baseline drifts slowly up and down.

Causes: Patient breathing, loose or dry electrodes, diaphoresis

Fix: Reapply electrodes to areas with less respiratory movement. Clean and dry skin before placement.
Muscle Tremor / Somatic Noise

Appearance: Irregular high-frequency "fuzzy" noise — can mimic VFib.

Causes: Patient shivering, anxiety, tremor (Parkinson's), movement

Fix: Ask patient to lie still. Warm the patient. Check patient pulse before treating "VFib."
AC Interference (60 Hz Noise)

Appearance: Perfectly regular, fine, consistent buzzing — 60 cycles per second. Very regular unlike muscle tremor.

Causes: Nearby electrical equipment, ungrounded equipment, poor electrode contact

Fix: Unplug nearby electrical devices. Use ECG filter. Check lead wire integrity.
Reversed or Misplaced Leads

Appearance: Lead I shows inverted P wave and QRS — should be upright.

Most common: RA and LA electrodes swapped — Lead I is globally negative.

Fix: Verify electrode placement. NEVER report an ECG you suspect has reversed leads. Reapply correctly and repeat.
The Critical Safety Rule: Assess responsiveness, pulse, pressure, breathing, perfusion, symptoms, and the tracing at once. An alert patient with a strong pulse contradicts true VF, but a pulseless or unstable patient requires immediate emergency action while the ECG source is verified. Patient-first applies in both directions.

Lead Reversal, Misplacement & Reporting Only Validated Data

A newly negative Lead I with unexpectedly positive aVR, unchanged precordial leads, no symptom change, and a different prior tracing strongly suggests right-arm/left-arm reversal. The solution is not to interpret around the error.

Correct and reacquire
Verify identity, all four limb electrodes, cable labels, anatomic sites, speed, gain, and body position; then obtain a new tracing.
Communicate validated findings
Document the suspected error, correction, reacquired result, patient assessment, and relevant communication according to policy.

Case Summary — Four Situations, Key Lessons

Situation 1 (Artifact): Assess the patient before the monitor. An alert patient eating breakfast cannot be in VFib. Check pulse, check leads. Never shock artifact.
Situation 2 (STEMI): ST elevation ≥1 mm in ≥2 contiguous leads = STEMI. Leads II, III, aVF = inferior STEMI (RCA). Activate STEMI protocol immediately.
Situation 3 (SVT during bronchoscopy): Regular narrow-complex tachycardia at 150–250 bpm = SVT. A narrow QRS supports a supraventricular mechanism in this scenario, but rhythm diagnosis and urgency still require the full tracing and patient assessment. Pause procedure, notify physician, vagal maneuver, prepare adenosine.
Situation 4 (Reversed leads): Globally inverted Lead I in a well patient = RA/LA reversal. Never report a 12-lead with suspected lead reversal. Reapply correctly and repeat.

ECG Assessment — Common Questions

What is the difference between a 3-lead monitor, 5-lead monitor, and 12-lead ECG?

A 3-lead monitor uses only three electrodes (RA, LA, LL) and displays 1–2 leads for continuous rhythm monitoring. It cannot diagnose MI or ischemia. A 5-lead system adds the RL ground and one precordial electrode for rudimentary ST monitoring. The 12-lead ECG uses 10 electrodes (4 limb + 6 precordial) to produce 12 simultaneous electrical views — required for MI diagnosis, ischemia localization, and conduction abnormality evaluation.

Why do we use Lead II as the standard monitoring lead?

Lead II records the electrical difference between the right arm (RA, negative) and left leg (LL, positive). This axis runs nearly parallel to the mean cardiac electrical vector — upper right to lower left — so P waves and QRS complexes appear tall and upright. Tall upright waveforms are easier to count and analyze. P waves are particularly well-visualized in Lead II, making arrhythmia interpretation more reliable.

How do I tell VT from SVT with aberrant conduction? Both can be rapid and wide.

Key features favoring VT: (1) AV dissociation — P waves independent from QRS complexes. (2) Capture beats — occasional narrow QRS when a normal impulse briefly gets through. (3) Fusion beats — QRS that is a blend of normal and ventricular conduction. (4) QRS extremely wide (>0.16 sec). (5) Concordance — all chest leads deflect in the same direction. If you cannot distinguish SVT-with-aberrancy from VT in a hemodynamically unstable patient, treat as VT — "when in doubt, treat as VT."

What are pathological Q waves, and how are they different from normal Q waves?

Small narrow Q waves are normal in many leads — they represent initial septal depolarization. Pathological Q waves are defined as: >0.04 seconds wide AND/OR >25% of the height of the R wave in the same lead. They indicate an electrical window through dead myocardium. Pathological Q waves typically indicate prior MI and can persist permanently.

What is the angle of Louis and why is it important for electrode placement?

The sternal angle (angle of Louis) is the horizontal ridge where the manubrium meets the body of the sternum — felt as a palpable bump. The 2nd rib articulates here. This is the most reliable bony landmark for counting down to the 4th intercostal space where V1 and V2 are placed. Never estimate — always count from the angle of Louis.

Why must speed and gain be checked before measuring?

Box dimensions represent different time or voltage when settings change. A tracing at a nonstandard speed or gain can make intervals and amplitudes appear abnormal unless the actual settings are used.

What is the endpoint after identifying artifact?

Correct the cause, reacquire a reliable tracing, continue appropriate surveillance, reassess the patient, and document or communicate the event when it affected care.

Can a normal telemetry rhythm exclude acute coronary syndrome?

No. Telemetry answers a rhythm-surveillance question in selected leads. Persistent or high-risk symptoms require a diagnostic 12-lead, comparison with prior or serial tracings, biomarkers, and urgent clinical evaluation.

Does a wide QRS prove ventricular tachycardia?

No. Wide-complex tachycardia may represent VT, SVT with aberrancy, bundle-branch block, pacing, metabolic effects, or pre-excitation. Use the full rhythm features, history, tracing quality, pulse, and stability.

Summary — ECG Assessment at a Glance

ECG Grid: Small box = 0.04 sec horizontal / 0.1 mV vertical. Large box = 0.20 sec. 5 large boxes = 1 sec. 30 large boxes = 6-second strip.
5-Step Analysis: (1) Regularity (2) Rate (3) P:QRS Ratio (4) QRS Width (<0.12 sec = narrow) (5) PR Interval (0.12–0.20 sec = normal)
Chest Pain Sequence: Ischemia → inverted T waves. Injury (STEMI) → ST elevation ≥1 mm in ≥2 leads. Infarction → pathological Q waves (>0.04 sec and >25% of R height).
Artifact Rule: Assess the patient before the monitor. Never treat artifact. VFib patient is pulseless — if patient is alert, the monitor is wrong. Check pulse before defibrillating.
