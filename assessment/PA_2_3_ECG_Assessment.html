ECG Assessment — Equipment, Interpretation, Chest Pain & Artifact

The electrocardiogram is the most powerful non-invasive cardiac diagnostic tool available at the bedside. Respiratory therapists read ECGs daily — to assess cardiac rhythm during bronchoscopy, to recognize ischemia triggering a COPD exacerbation, or to identify why a patient's hemodynamics are deteriorating. This lesson builds systematic ECG assessment skills from equipment to interpretation to chest pain diagnosis.

2-Minute Overview: Reading the ECG as an RT

ECG Indications — When Does the Patient Need Monitoring?

The ECG is ordered when clinical findings suggest a cardiac cause for the patient's symptoms, or when cardiac monitoring is needed to guide treatment and detect deterioration. Respiratory therapists frequently encounter patients with these presentations.

Cardiac Symptoms
  • Chest pain / pressure / tightness — radiation to arm, jaw, or back suggests MI
  • Palpitations — awareness of own heartbeat, "fluttering," skipped beats
  • Syncope or pre-syncope — fainting or near-fainting; may indicate arrhythmia
  • Diaphoresis — unexplained sweating, especially with chest discomfort
Respiratory and Systemic Symptoms
  • Dyspnea at rest or minimal exertion — can indicate cardiac failure or PE
  • Lower extremity edema — may indicate right or left heart failure
  • Cyanosis — suggests severe desaturation requiring cardiac evaluation
  • Any hemodynamic instability — hypotension, tachycardia without obvious cause
RT-Specific Indications: ECG monitoring is required during flexible bronchoscopy, procedural sedation, and for all patients on mechanical ventilation. Cardiac arrhythmias are a documented complication of bronchoscopy — AARC guidelines mandate continuous cardiac monitoring during the procedure.

ECG Equipment — Monitors, Electrocardiographs & Oscilloscopes

Three categories of equipment are used for cardiac monitoring in respiratory care. Each serves a different clinical purpose, uses a different number of leads, and produces different information.

SystemLeadsElectrodesPrimary UseRT Application
Oscilloscope / Cardiac Monitor 1–3 leads displayed 3 electrodes (RA, LA, LL) Continuous rhythm monitoring ICU, bronchoscopy suite, transport monitoring
5-Lead Monitor System Up to 5 leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL + 1 chest) 5 electrodes (RA, LA, LL, RL + 1 precordial) Rhythm + limited ST segment monitoring Mechanically ventilated patients, monitored beds
12-Lead Electrocardiograph 12 simultaneous leads 10 electrodes (4 limb + 6 chest) Complete cardiac electrical evaluation Chest pain assessment, pre-procedure clearance, MI diagnosis
Key Distinction — Monitor vs. Electrocardiograph: A cardiac monitor/oscilloscope shows rhythm in real time but is not diagnostic for MI or ischemia alone. A 12-lead ECG captures a snapshot from 12 electrical perspectives simultaneously — required to diagnose STEMI, NSTEMI, ischemia, and infarction. The 12-lead is the gold standard for chest pain evaluation.

Lead Placement — Where Every Electrode Goes

Accurate lead placement is essential. Misplaced electrodes change the direction and morphology of waveforms — causing false-positive abnormalities or masking true findings. For the RT performing or assisting with ECG acquisition, correct electrode placement is a required competency.

Limb Leads (4 Electrodes)

RA — Right Arm

Right wrist or anywhere on right arm. Color code: White. "White is right."

LA — Left Arm

Left wrist or anywhere on left arm. Color code: Black.

LL — Left Leg

Left ankle or anywhere on left leg. Color code: Red. Forms positive pole of lead II (most common monitoring lead).

RL — Right Leg (Ground)

Right ankle or anywhere on right leg. Color code: Green. Reference/ground — does not contribute to any lead waveform.

The ECG Grid — Time, Voltage, and Wave Anatomy

Every ECG measurement — rate, interval, amplitude — depends on understanding the grid. The horizontal axis measures time; the vertical axis measures voltage (amplitude). These two axes define what every deflection on the strip means.

Time (Horizontal Axis)
  • 1 small box = 0.04 seconds
  • 5 small boxes = 1 large box = 0.20 seconds
  • 5 large boxes = 1 second
  • 30 large boxes = 6 seconds (rate counting strip)
Voltage/Amplitude (Vertical Axis)
  • 1 small box = 0.1 mV
  • 2 small boxes = 0.2 mV (standard QRS width threshold)
  • 10 small boxes = 1 mV (standard calibration)
  • 5 mm = 0.5 mV ST elevation is clinically significant

Wave Components — What Each Deflection Represents

P
P Wave — Atrial Depolarization
Small rounded hump before the QRS. Represents the electrical activation spreading across BOTH atria. Should be upright in Lead II, uniform in shape, one per QRS. Normal duration: <0.12 sec. Normal amplitude: <2.5 mm.
Q
Q Wave — Initial Septal Depolarization
Small downward deflection at the START of the QRS complex. May be absent in some leads normally. Significant (pathological) Q wave: >0.04 sec wide or >25% of R wave height — indicates prior myocardial infarction.
R
R Wave — Ventricular Depolarization (Upward)
The tall upward deflection — the most prominent feature of the QRS complex. Represents ventricular activation. R wave height and progression across the chest leads (R wave progression V1→V6) is diagnostically important.
S
S Wave — Terminal Ventricular Depolarization (Downward)
Downward deflection immediately after the R wave. Represents late ventricular depolarization. Completes the QRS complex. Normal QRS duration (Q through S): <0.12 seconds (<3 small boxes). Wide QRS (>0.12 sec) indicates ventricular origin or bundle branch block.
T
T Wave — Ventricular Repolarization
Broader rounded hump after the QRS. Represents ventricular electrical recovery. Should be upright and asymmetric (gradual rise, faster descent). Inverted T waves indicate ischemia. Peaked T waves suggest hyperkalemia.
ST
ST Segment — Isoelectric Baseline
From the end of the S wave to the beginning of the T wave. Should lie at the isoelectric (baseline) level. Elevation ≥1 mm in ≥2 contiguous leads = myocardial injury (STEMI pattern). ST depression = ischemia or NSTEMI.

The 5-Step Systematic ECG Analysis

Applying the same steps in the same order every time prevents missed findings and builds interpretive consistency. These five steps move from the most basic (is there a rhythm?) to the most specific (are the intervals normal?).

1
Regularity — Is the rhythm regular or irregular?
Compare all R-R intervals (or P-P intervals). Regular: all intervals within 2 small boxes of each other. Irregular: intervals vary more than 2 small boxes. If irregular → must use the 6-second method for rate calculation.
2
Rate — What is the heart rate?
Regular: 300 ÷ large boxes between R waves  |  Regular (precise): 1500 ÷ small boxes between R waves  |  Any rhythm: Count QRS complexes in 6 seconds × 10  |  Sequence method: 300–150–100–75–60–50 across dark lines
3
P to QRS Ratio — Is there one P wave before each QRS?
Normal: 1 P wave before each QRS (1:1 ratio). Missing P waves = junctional or ventricular origin. Multiple P waves per QRS = heart block. No P waves + irregular = atrial fibrillation. P waves without QRS = complete heart block.
4
QRS Width — Is the QRS narrow or wide?
Narrow (<0.12 sec / <3 small boxes): Supraventricular origin — impulse conducted normally through His-Purkinje system. Wide (≥0.12 sec / ≥3 small boxes): Ventricular origin OR bundle branch block — impulse traveling through slower ventricular muscle.
5
PR Interval — Is the conduction from atria to ventricles normal?
Measured from start of P wave to start of QRS complex. Normal: 0.12–0.20 seconds (3–5 small boxes). Short (<0.12) = pre-excitation (WPW). Prolonged (>0.20) = AV block (1st degree). Variable = 2nd or 3rd degree heart block.

Place the 5 Steps in the Correct Sequence

Are the R-R intervals consistent, or does the rhythm vary?

Normal Sinus Rhythm — The Baseline for All Comparison

Every rhythm is defined by comparing it to NSR. When all five steps of the analysis fall within normal limits, the rhythm is normal sinus rhythm. Any deviation from these criteria defines the specific arrhythmia.

StepNSR CriterionIf Abnormal → Suspect
1. RegularityRegular — R-R intervals consistent (±2 small boxes)Arrhythmia, AFib, PVCs, blocks
2. Rate60–100 beats per minute<60 = bradycardia; >100 = tachycardia
3. P:QRS RatioOne P wave before each QRS — upright, uniform in Lead IIMissing P = junctional; multiple P = block; no P = AFib/VFib
4. QRS Width<0.12 seconds (<3 small boxes) — narrow≥0.12 sec = BBB, ventricular rhythm, accessory pathway
5. PR Interval0.12–0.20 seconds (3–5 small boxes)>0.20 = 1st degree AV block; variable = 2nd/3rd degree block

Abnormal Rhythms — Recognition and Response

Abnormal rhythms are identified by comparing each of the 5 steps against NSR criteria. The step that deviates from normal often names the arrhythmia (too fast → tachycardia; no P wave → atrial or ventricular origin; wide QRS → ventricular rhythm).

Sinus Bradycardia
<60 bpm
Regular, P before each QRS, narrow QRS, normal PR. Rate <60. Causes: vagal stimulation, beta-blockers, hypothyroidism, athlete. Treat if symptomatic (atropine, pacing).
Sinus Tachycardia
>100 bpm
Regular, P before each QRS, narrow QRS, normal PR. Rate >100. Causes: pain, fever, hypoxia, hypovolemia, PE, anxiety. Treat the underlying cause, not the tachycardia itself.
Atrial Fibrillation
Irreg. irreg.
Irregularly irregular. No visible P waves — replaced by chaotic "f" waves (wavy baseline). Narrow QRS unless BBB. Ventricular rate varies 60–160. Risk: stroke from atrial thrombus. Rate control or rhythm control + anticoagulation.
Atrial Flutter
Sawtooth F waves
Regular "F" waves at 250–350 bpm (sawtooth baseline). Ventricular rate depends on AV block ratio (2:1, 3:1, 4:1). QRS narrow. Distinguish from AFib: flutter waves are organized and regular, AFib is chaotic.
SVT
150–250 bpm
Regular, narrow QRS, rate 150–250 bpm. P waves hidden in or after QRS. Sudden onset and offset ("on/off switch"). Vagal maneuver or adenosine (if stable). Narrow QRS distinguishes SVT from VT.
Ventricular Tachycardia
>100 bpm, wide
Regular, wide bizarre QRS (>0.12 sec), rate >100 bpm, no visible P waves. Wide QRS is the key differentiator from SVT. Pulseless VT = defibrillation. Stable VT = amiodarone. Unstable = synchronized cardioversion.
Ventricular Fibrillation
No QRS — chaos
Completely chaotic, disorganized waveforms — no recognizable QRS complexes. Patient pulseless. CPR + Defibrillation immediately. VFib is a shockable rhythm. Every second without defibrillation reduces survival.
Asystole
Flatline
Complete electrical silence — flat line on monitor. No P waves, no QRS, no electrical activity. Patient pulseless. CPR ONLY — defibrillation does NOT work on asystole. Check leads are connected. Consider 5 Hs and 5 Ts.

Diagnosing Chest Pain — Ischemia, Injury, and Infarction

The 12-lead ECG allows the RT and clinical team to localize cardiac pathology and differentiate the three stages of coronary artery disease presentation. These findings have a specific, progressive relationship — and each produces a distinct ECG signature.

1. Ischemia — Inverted T Waves

What is happening: Reduced blood flow to a region of myocardium — cells are alive but electrically abnormal due to oxygen deprivation.

ECG finding: T wave inversion in the affected leads. T waves that should be upright are now flipped downward.

Location clue: Inverted T waves in anterior leads (V1–V4) → anterior ischemia. Inferior leads (II, III, aVF) → inferior ischemia.

Reversible: With restoration of blood flow (unstable angina treatment), T waves can return to normal. No permanent cell death yet.

2. Myocardial Injury — ST Elevation

What is happening: Active, severe ischemia — myocardial cells are injured but not yet dead. This is the STEMI pattern — a cardiac emergency.

ECG finding: ST segment elevation ≥1 mm in ≥2 contiguous leads. The ST segment is lifted above the isoelectric baseline.

STEMI criteria: ≥1 mm in limb leads, ≥2 mm in precordial leads, in ≥2 anatomically contiguous leads.

Time-sensitive emergency: "Time is muscle." Goal: PCI within 90 minutes of first medical contact. Every minute of delay = myocardial cell death.

3. Myocardial Infarction — Pathological Q Waves

What is happening: Complete infarction — myocardial tissue has died. The dead zone cannot conduct electricity → an electrical "window" creates deep Q waves in overlying leads.

ECG finding: Pathological Q waves: >0.04 sec wide AND >25% of R wave height in ≥2 contiguous leads.

NSTEMI: Elevated troponin + ischemic symptoms, but NO ST elevation. ST depression or T wave changes. Treated urgently but without the same immediate PCI pressure as STEMI.

Permanent: Q waves from prior MI are typically permanent — they identify old infarction territory on future ECGs. "Tombstone" appearance indicates massive anterior STEMI.

ECG Artifact — Recognizing and Eliminating False Signals

Artifact is any electrical signal on the ECG that does not originate from the heart. It is one of the most common causes of false alarms, unnecessary interventions, and diagnostic confusion. Every RT should be able to identify the source of artifact and correct it before reporting or acting on an abnormal tracing.

Wandering Baseline

Appearance: The isoelectric baseline drifts slowly up and down, making the ECG appear to "walk" across the page.

Causes: Patient breathing (chest movement affects electrode-skin contact), loose or dry electrodes, diaphoresis (sweating under electrode)

Fix: Reapply electrodes to areas with less respiratory movement. Clean and dry skin thoroughly before electrode placement. Use fresh electrodes with adequate gel.
Muscle Tremor / Somatic Noise

Appearance: Irregular, high-frequency "fuzzy" noise on the baseline — can mimic ventricular fibrillation but is irregular in amplitude and frequency.

Causes: Patient shivering, anxiety, tremor (Parkinson's), voluntary movement, poor electrode placement over muscle

Fix: Ask patient to lie still and relax. Warm the patient if shivering. Place electrodes over bony prominences (less muscle movement). Check patient pulse before treating "VFib" — if patient has a pulse, it is artifact.
AC Interference (60 Hz Noise)

Appearance: Perfectly regular, fine, consistent buzzing noise overlying all leads — 60 cycles per second (in North America). Very regular, unlike muscle tremor.

Causes: Nearby electrical equipment (IV pumps, ventilators, lights), ungrounded equipment, poor skin-electrode contact

Fix: Unplug nearby electrical devices temporarily. Use the ECG machine's filter function. Ensure electrodes have good skin contact. Check for damaged lead wires.
Reversed or Misplaced Leads

Appearance: Lead I shows an inverted (negative) P wave and QRS — which should normally be upright in Lead I. V leads may show unexpected patterns.

Most common: RA and LA electrodes swapped. Lead I is negative when right arm and left arm are reversed. Precordial leads placed in wrong intercostal spaces.

Fix: Verify electrode placement against the patient's anatomy. If Lead I is globally negative/inverted and patient appears normal → suspect RA/LA reversal. Reapply correctly and repeat the tracing. NEVER report an ECG you suspect has reversed leads.
The Critical Safety Rule: Before responding to any alarming ECG finding — especially one that appears to be VFib or asystole — check the patient first. If the patient is alert and talking, the "rhythm" is artifact. Always assess the patient, then the monitor.

Case Summary — Four Situations, Key Lessons

Situation 1 (Artifact): Assess the patient before the monitor. An alert patient eating breakfast cannot be in VFib. Check pulse, check leads, check for muscle movement. Never shock artifact.
Situation 2 (STEMI): ST elevation ≥1 mm in ≥2 contiguous leads = STEMI. Leads II, III, aVF = inferior STEMI (RCA). Activate STEMI protocol immediately. Time is muscle — 90-minute PCI window.
Situation 3 (SVT during bronchoscopy): Regular narrow-complex tachycardia at 150–250 bpm = SVT. Narrow QRS rules out VT. Pause procedure, notify physician, vagal maneuver, prepare adenosine.
Situation 4 (Reversed leads): Globally inverted Lead I in a well patient = RA/LA reversal. Never report a 12-lead with suspected lead reversal. Reapply correctly and repeat.

FAQ

ECG Assessment — Common Questions

What is the difference between a 3-lead monitor, 5-lead monitor, and 12-lead ECG?

A 3-lead monitor uses only three electrodes (RA, LA, LL) and displays 1–2 leads simultaneously — primarily for continuous rhythm monitoring. It cannot diagnose MI or ischemia. A 5-lead system adds the RL ground and one precordial electrode, allowing up to 5 leads to be displayed including one chest lead for rudimentary ST monitoring. The 12-lead ECG uses 10 electrodes (4 limb + 6 precordial) to produce 12 simultaneous electrical views of the heart — required for MI diagnosis, ischemia localization, and conduction abnormality evaluation. The 12-lead is the standard for any chest pain assessment.

Why do we use Lead II as the standard monitoring lead?

Lead II records the electrical difference between the right arm (RA, negative) and left leg (LL, positive). This axis runs nearly parallel to the mean cardiac electrical vector — from upper right to lower left — which means P waves and QRS complexes appear tall and upright in Lead II. Tall, upright waveforms are easier to count, measure, and analyze. The P wave is particularly well-visualized in Lead II, making arrhythmia interpretation more reliable. For ST segment monitoring, V5 is often added because it is the most sensitive lead for detecting anterior and lateral ischemia.

How do I tell VT from SVT with aberrant conduction? Both can be rapid and wide.

This is clinically challenging. Key features favoring VT: (1) AV dissociation — P waves independent from QRS complexes (P waves march through at a different rate). (2) Capture beats — occasional narrow QRS when a normal impulse briefly gets through. (3) Fusion beats — QRS that is a blend of normal and ventricular conduction. (4) QRS extremely wide (>0.16 sec). (5) Concordance — all chest leads show QRS deflection in the same direction. If you cannot distinguish SVT-with-aberrancy from VT in a hemodynamically unstable patient, treat as VT. "When in doubt, treat as VT" is the safe clinical rule.

What are pathological Q waves, and how are they different from normal Q waves?

Small, narrow Q waves are normal in many leads — they represent initial septal depolarization moving away from the lead. Pathological (significant) Q waves are defined as: >0.04 seconds (1 small box) wide AND/OR >25% of the height of the R wave in the same lead. They indicate an electrical "window" — an area of dead myocardium that cannot conduct electricity, so the electrode looks "through" it to see distant electrical activity moving away. Pathological Q waves typically indicate prior MI and can persist permanently. New Q waves in a patient with chest pain suggest evolving MI.

What is the angle of Louis and why is it important for electrode placement?

The sternal angle (angle of Louis) is the horizontal ridge on the sternum where the manubrium (upper sternum) meets the body of the sternum — felt as a palpable bump. The 2nd rib articulates here. This is the most reliable bony landmark for counting down to the 4th intercostal space (between ribs 4 and 5) where V1 and V2 are placed. Starting from the angle of Louis and palpating down guarantees accurate lead placement regardless of patient body habitus. Never estimate — always count from the angle of Louis.
