Therapist-Driven Protocols — What They Are and Why They Matter

A Therapist-Driven Protocol (TDP) is a framework that allows the respiratory therapist to recommend, order, or modify approved therapies based on clinical assessment findings — without requiring a separate physician order for every change. TDPs enable the RT to function as an autonomous clinical practitioner within the scope of respiratory therapy.

What the RT Can Do Under a TDP
  • Initiate approved therapy when both the indication and protocol criteria are met
  • Modify therapy frequency (increase or decrease) based on patient response
  • Discontinue therapy when criteria for discontinuation are met
  • Escalate to physician-required orders when necessary (e.g., FiO₂ >44%)
The Severity-Based Approach

Treatment frequency and type are determined by patient acuity — not by a fixed schedule. A patient with a severity score of 3 (low acuity) receives PRN therapy. The same patient deteriorating to a score of 22 (high acuity) escalates to QID treatment. The RT scores the patient at each visit and adjusts accordingly.

Three Core TDP Competencies
  1. Make recommendations and order approved therapy using the TDP
  2. Complete SOAPIER documentation from verbal patient reports
  3. Score patients using the Respiratory Care Protocol Severity Assessment tool

Protocol Authority Is Bounded and Accountable

A TDP authorizes defined actions only when the therapist is competent, the patient meets approved criteria, contraindications have been assessed, and the required documentation and communication occur. It is not a substitute for judgment or provider involvement when the patient falls outside protocol.

Components of a Good Patient Report — Eight Elements

A complete patient report efficiently communicates everything the receiving clinician needs to understand the patient's current status, history, and treatment plan. More detail is better than too little — but keep it relevant. A report is not a biography; it is a clinical summary.

1 — Demographics

Patient's age, gender, race, and date of admission (DOA). Establishes who the patient is and how long they have been hospitalized.

2 — Chief Complaint

The primary reason for the current hospital admission. Example: "Dyspnea beginning 2 weeks ago following a respiratory infection."

3 — Relevant Medical History

Past medical conditions affecting current care. Medication history, smoking history, surgical history. Example: "History of asthma — uses MDI Albuterol PRN at home."

4 — Current Assessment Findings

Vital signs (HR, RR, Temp, SpO₂, BP). Physical exam findings: breath sounds (location, quality), accessory muscle use, I:E ratio, cough effectiveness, work of breathing.

5 — Diagnostic Results

CXR (infiltrates, atelectasis, effusions — note if current or old). ABG results. Lab values: WBC, glucose, electrolytes. Sputum: color, consistency, volume.

6 — Current Orders Under RT Protocol

Device name and type. Medication and dose. Frequency (PRN/TID/QID/Q4H). When last treatment was given. Example: "2.5 mg Albuterol via nebulizer QID, last given 08:00."

7 — Response to Therapy

How the patient is responding: improvement, deterioration, or no change. Adverse reactions. Patient tolerance of current therapies.

8 — Weaning/Modification Plan

Plan for titrating or discontinuing therapies. Criteria for discontinuation. Expected timeline for changes per protocol severity score.

SOAPIER Documentation — Complete Clinical Charting

SOAPIER is the structured charting format used in respiratory therapy documentation. Each letter represents a specific category of clinical information. Well-executed SOAPIER notes communicate the full clinical picture — what the patient said, what you measured, what it means, what you did, and whether it worked.

S — Subjective: What the PATIENT SAYS. Complaints, symptoms, feelings reported verbally. Example: "Patient states she is feeling so much better. Reports less difficulty breathing."
O — Objective: Measurable, observable data collected by the clinician. Vital signs, physical exam findings, lab values, test results. Example: "HR 90, RR 16, SpO₂ 96% on 1L nasal cannula. Breath sounds clear bilaterally."
A — Assessment: Your CLINICAL INTERPRETATION of the subjective and objective data. Diagnoses, disease process, anatomical alterations, and how therapies are addressing the problem. Example: "Patient showing significant improvement in respiratory status. Atelectasis resolving per CXR."
P — Planning: The TREATMENT PLAN per protocol indications. Which therapies are indicated based on the new severity score? Frequency and type of treatment. Example: "Per severity score drop to 4, continue HHN but decrease Albuterol to TID."
I — Implementing: Execute the plan. Administer medications and devices as planned. Document what was done. Example: "2.5 mg Albuterol nebulizer administered via HHN face mask. IPV therapy given × 20 minutes."
E — Evaluation: EVALUATE patient response to the therapy just administered. Did symptoms improve, stay the same, or worsen? Example: "Patient able to cough up large amounts of green sputum following IPV treatment. Better aeration noted post-treatment on auscultation."
R — Reassessing: Determine if the plan needs to change based on the response. Should frequency increase, decrease, or is discontinuation appropriate? Example: "Reassess in 12–24 hours. If continued improvement, consider discontinuing IPV and switching to PRN nebulizer."

Communicating Effectively with Physicians

Effective physician communication is a professional skill that requires preparation, clinical knowledge, and interpersonal awareness. The RT who communicates well is the one whose recommendations get heard, considered, and implemented — improving patient outcomes.

Four Keys to Effective Physician Communication

  1. Know the disease process — Understand anatomical alterations and pathophysiology before the conversation. You must be able to explain WHY you are recommending a therapy.
  2. Ensure therapies address the actual problem — Can you justify the therapy? Is it targeting the patient's specific pathophysiology? Example: Is Albuterol appropriate for a patient with pneumonia (consolidation, not bronchospasm)?
  3. Constructively question inappropriate orders — Use evidence-based rationale. Approach with curiosity, not confrontation. "Would Albuterol be appropriate here given the patient's findings?"
  4. Use a positive opening phrase — "Can I pick your brain for a moment?" This signals collaboration, respect, and willingness to learn rather than challenge.

Report Personalities — Know Your Audience

Different physicians have different communication preferences. Some want a complete 8-component report with all vital signs and CXR details. Others want only the critical finding and your recommendation. Adapt your reporting style based on who you are reporting to.

Always come fully prepared regardless of audience preference. If the physician wants only two sentences, you can provide that — but you must have all the supporting data ready if they probe further. Never give a brief report because you haven't done the full assessment.
Professional approach: Approach with willingness to learn. Present data, not opinions. Use evidence-based rationale. Respect hierarchy while advocating for the patient.

Respiratory Care Protocol Severity Assessment — The Scoring Tool

The severity scoring tool standardizes therapy frequency decisions. The RT assesses the patient across five clinical categories, assigns points based on findings, and the total score determines the appropriate therapy frequency. This removes subjectivity from frequency decisions and creates consistent, evidence-based care.

Severity Score → Therapy Frequency

Total ScoreFrequencyClinical Meaning
1 – 5PRN (As Needed)Low acuity — order therapy only when clinically indicated
6 – 15BID or TID (2–3×/day)Mild-moderate acuity — regular scheduled therapy needed
16 – 25QID (4×/day)Moderate-high acuity — frequent therapy required
> 26Q4H + PRNHigh acuity — every 4 hours plus additional as needed
Course Escalation Trigger — FiO₂ >44%: In this course protocol, a requirement above 44% FiO₂ triggers further ventilatory-support evaluation. It does not automatically require intubation. Reassess work of breathing, ventilation, mental status, gas exchange, hemodynamics, trajectory, and response to the current device.

The Five Scoring Categories

1. History & Condition: Points for respiratory symptoms (productive cough, wheezing), vital sign abnormalities (elevated RR, HR, Temp, low SpO₂), accessory muscle use, general condition changes, recent infection history, and weak/ineffective cough.
2. Oxygen Therapy: Points based on SpO₂ level and how much supplemental oxygen is required to maintain SpO₂ ≥90%. SpO₂ <90% on room air qualifies the patient for oxygen therapy. Higher oxygen requirements = higher score.
3. Airway Clearance: Points for thick/productive secretions, coarse crackles, weak or ineffective cough. Note: atelectasis prevention belongs to Lung Expansion — NOT airway clearance. Airway clearance is specifically about moving secretions.
4. Lung Expansion: Points for atelectasis (on CXR), decreased inspiratory capacity, bilateral infiltrates, consolidation, post-operative status, or difficulty taking deep breaths. Goal: alveolar recruitment and prevention of further collapse.
5. Aerosol Medication: Points for expiratory wheezing, bronchospasm, or breath sounds indicating need for bronchodilation. If no wheezing is present, aerosol medication may not be indicated regardless of other findings.

Total Severity Score = 3. Patient has mild symptoms and stable vital signs. Clear breath sounds.
Total Severity Score = 20. Patient has moderate hypoxemia, thick secretions, expiratory wheezing, and bilateral atelectasis on CXR.
Total Severity Score = 30. Critically ill patient requiring FiO₂ 60%, mechanically ventilated, with severe bilateral consolidation and ongoing bronchospasm.
Total Severity Score = 10. Patient recovering from pneumonia — mild hypoxemia on 2L NC, scattered crackles, productive cough, improving vital signs.

Airway Clearance Modalities — Selecting the Right Tool

Airway clearance therapy targets the mucociliary blanket — hydrating, mobilizing, and helping the patient expel secretions. The indications are specific: retained secretions, productive cough, coarse crackles, and weak cough. Note that preventing atelectasis is a lung expansion indication, not airway clearance.

IPV — Intrapulmonary Percussive Ventilation

Delivers high-frequency oscillating pulsatile airflow through a mouthpiece, breaking up thick mucus plugs and vibrating secretions loose. Requires patient cooperation and mouthpiece capability. Serves DUAL PURPOSE — airway clearance AND lung expansion. Particularly effective for thick, retained secretions.

Select when: Alert, cooperative patient. Thick secretions. Coexisting atelectasis (treats both problems with one modality).
Vibratory PEP

Patient exhales against resistance, creating oscillating positive expiratory pressure. The oscillations transmit through the airways, loosening secretions. Active, breathing-technique-based therapy. Effective for cooperative, alert patients.

Select when: Alert, cooperative patient with good respiratory effort. Can follow breathing technique instructions.
Flutter Valve

Handheld device held in the mouth during exhalation. A steel ball oscillates within the device, creating periodic resistance and vibration during exhalation. Portable and inexpensive — excellent for home care and discharge planning.

Select when: Discharge/home care planning. Portable therapy needed. Cooperative patient with chronic secretion management needs.
Vibrating Vest (HFCWO)

High-Frequency Chest Wall Oscillation — an inflatable vest worn by the patient, connected to an air pulse generator. The vest rapidly inflates and deflates, creating oscillations transmitted to the chest wall and airways. No mouthpiece or active cooperation required beyond sitting.

Select when: Patient cannot use mouthpiece or cooperate with active techniques. Chronic conditions (CF, bronchiectasis). Neuromuscular weakness.
Bland Aerosol: Humidification may support comfort and secretion hydration in selected patients, but routine bland aerosol does not replace hydration, mobilization, coughing, suction, or an indicated airway-clearance technique. Indicated to restore the mucociliary blanket. Not a bronchodilator — does not treat bronchospasm. Often combined with other airway clearance techniques.

CPT (Chest Physical Therapy): Percussion (cupped hand on chest) + vibration + postural drainage. Traditional technique — effective but labor-intensive. Requires patient cooperation and positioning.

20-year-old with cystic fibrosis and severe respiratory muscle weakness. Cannot use a mouthpiece or follow active breathing techniques. Needs daily secretion clearance.
Patient post-intubation has dry, crusted secretions in the upper airway. The goal is to hydrate the airway mucosa and restore the mucociliary blanket.

Lung Expansion Modalities — Recruiting Collapsed Alveoli

Lung expansion therapy targets atelectasis, decreased inspiratory capacity, and post-operative alveolar collapse. The goal is to recruit collapsed alveoli, increase functional residual capacity, and restore normal gas exchange. The indication is different from airway clearance — here the focus is on alveolar inflation, not secretion mobilization.

ModalityMechanismBest Patient/Indication
Incentive Spirometry (IS)Patient-directed maximal inspiration with visual feedback (ball rises in chamber)Cooperative, alert, post-operative patients. Directed self-care for deep breathing.
CPAPContinuous positive pressure throughout breathing cycle — splints airways and alveoli open continuouslySleep apnea, mild-moderate atelectasis, CHF-related airway closure. Maintains FRC.
PEEPPositive end-expiratory pressure at end of breath — prevents alveolar collapse between breathsVia ventilator or non-invasive devices. Critical care patients. ARDS management.
IPPBPositive pressure delivered during inspiration — assists or augments the patient's inhaled breathHyperinflation therapy for patients unable to take deep breaths independently.
IPVHigh-frequency oscillations + positive pressure components — mobilizes secretions AND recruits alveoliDUAL PURPOSE: both airway clearance AND lung expansion. Alert, cooperative patient with mouthpiece.
Key Distinction — Airway Clearance vs. Lung Expansion: A patient with retained secretions needs AIRWAY CLEARANCE therapy. A patient with atelectasis (collapsed alveoli) needs LUNG EXPANSION therapy. A patient with both (very common in clinical practice) benefits most from IPV — which serves dual purpose. The scoring tool has separate categories for each — score them separately and select the modality that addresses both if both are present.

Aerosol Medication Selection — Score-Based Dosing

Aerosol medication therapy is indicated when breath sounds demonstrate bronchospasm — specifically expiratory wheezing. The protocol links severity score to medication frequency directly. The primary agent under protocol is Albuterol 2.5 mg via nebulizer.

ScoreFrequencyMedication Order
1 – 5PRN2.5 mg Albuterol nebulizer — as needed only
6 – 15TID or QID2.5 mg Albuterol nebulizer TID or QID
16 – 25QID2.5 mg Albuterol nebulizer QID
> 26Q4H + PRN2.5 mg Albuterol nebulizer Q4H plus PRN as needed
Delivery Device Selection
  • Nebulizer via mask or mouthpiece: Standard delivery for most patients
  • MDI (Metered-Dose Inhaler): Preferred for home use and chronic management when available
  • Continuous nebulizer: Severe bronchospasm requiring sustained bronchodilation
  • HHN + IPV: When pharmacy lacks MDIs — combine high-flow nasal cannula with IPV nebulizer delivery
Key Points for Practice
  • Aerosol medication is indicated for WHEEZING — if no wheezing, question the order
  • If pharmacy lacks MDIs → order HHN in conjunction with IPV or continuous nebulizer via face mask
  • Albuterol is a bronchodilator — it relaxes bronchial smooth muscle. It does NOT treat secretions, atelectasis, or infection
  • MDI is preferred over nebulizer at equivalent doses when the patient can use it correctly

FAQ

TDP, SOAPIER & Severity Scoring — Common Questions

What is the difference between the Planning (P) and Implementation (I) sections of SOAPIER?

Planning (P) is what you INTEND to do — the proposed treatment plan based on the assessment and severity score. Implementation (I) is what you actually DID — documenting the execution of the plan. Example: P = "Per severity score 20, initiate 2.5 mg Albuterol QID and IPV QID." I = "2.5 mg Albuterol delivered via nebulizer × 15 minutes at 08:00. IPV administered via mouthpiece × 20 minutes." The plan is the intent; the implementation is the execution. Both are important — the plan shows your clinical reasoning, and the implementation proves the therapy was carried out.

Why can't Albuterol be used for pneumonia if the score still warrants aerosol medication?

Albuterol is a bronchodilator — it relaxes bronchial smooth muscle to open constricted airways. It treats bronchoconstriction (bronchospasm). Pneumonia causes consolidation — the alveoli fill with fluid, pus, and inflammatory debris. No amount of smooth muscle relaxation changes the fact that the alveolus is filled with fluid. Albuterol cannot drain the fluid, fight the bacteria, or reduce the consolidation. If the aerosol medication section of the scoring tool scores points ONLY because of the pneumonia (consolidation on CXR, crackles) but there is NO wheezing, NO bronchospasm, and NO obstructive history — the aerosol medication indication is not truly met. The RT should question whether Albuterol is appropriate and communicate that concern to the physician.

Can the RT de-escalate therapy without calling the physician first?

Under a properly established TDP, yes — the RT can adjust therapy frequency based on the severity score without first obtaining a separate physician order for every change. The TDP is the standing order that authorizes this. However, the RT must: (1) Document the scoring and clinical rationale in the chart using SOAPIER format. (2) Notify the physician of the change promptly (typically within the same shift). The TDP enables autonomous action within defined criteria — it does not eliminate the need for physician notification and collaboration. Think of it as: act first, document, then notify — rather than waiting for permission before acting.

What is the clinical difference between atelectasis prevention (lung expansion) and secretion clearance (airway clearance)?

Atelectasis is alveolar collapse — the alveoli themselves have deflated because they lacked sufficient pressure to stay open (surfactant depletion, mucus plug sealing off the segment, or post-operative splinting from pain). Lung expansion therapy (IS, CPAP, IPPB, IPV) addresses this by applying positive pressure to mechanically recruit and re-open the collapsed alveoli. Secretion clearance addresses the mucociliary blanket — moving thick mucus from the peripheral airways to the central airways where it can be coughed up or suctioned. The secretions themselves are not the atelectasis — but mucus plugging can CAUSE atelectasis by sealing off a segment. IPV addresses both because it both vibrates secretions loose (clearance) and provides positive pressure (expansion) simultaneously.

What does I:E ratio 1:2 tell you about this patient, and why is it significant?

A normal I:E ratio is approximately 1:2 — one unit of time for inspiration, two units for expiration. This is normal because expiration is a passive process relying on the elastic recoil of the lungs, which is slightly slower than the active process of inspiration. In obstructive lung disease (asthma, COPD exacerbation), the airways are narrowed — expiration becomes prolonged because air can't exit the constricted airways fast enough. A 1:2 ratio in this clinical context (combined with expiratory wheezing) indicates bronchial obstruction with air trapping. The longer the expiratory phase, the more significant the obstruction. Severe obstruction may show I:E ratios of 1:3, 1:4, or higher. This finding directly supports the aerosol medication indication (bronchodilation) and the lung expansion indication (air trapping causing dynamic hyperinflation).

Summary — TDP, SOAPIER & Severity-Based Care

Score → Frequency: 1-5 = PRN. 6-15 = BID/TID. 16-25 = QID. >26 = Q4H + PRN. FiO₂ >44% → recommend Ventilator Management Protocol.
SOAPIER: S = Patient says. O = You measure. A = You interpret (score + disease process). P = Your plan per score. I = What you did. E = How patient responded. R = Next steps.
Modality Selection: IPV = dual purpose (clearance + expansion). Flutter valve = home care. Vibrating vest = no cooperation needed. IS = cooperative post-op. CPAP/PEEP = sustained positive pressure.
