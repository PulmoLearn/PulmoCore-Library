Introduction to Respiratory Patient Assessment & Interviewing

This beginner lesson introduces how respiratory therapists begin understanding a patient: what to notice first, how to communicate professionally, how to gather history, how to separate subjective and objective data, and how to review a chart without assuming prior medical knowledge.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners should be able to explain the purpose of respiratory patient assessment, recognize basic signs of respiratory concern, gather beginner-level patient history, review common chart locations, and organize findings as subjective or objective data.

1. Describe the RT assessment mindset
Explain why RTs assess before selecting or changing respiratory care.
2. Recognize first-look concerns
Identify basic signs of respiratory distress such as abnormal breathing effort, color, posture, or mental status.
3. Gather patient history respectfully
Use professional communication to ask about symptoms, smoking/vaping, medications, and allergies.
4. Organize assessment data
Separate subjective reports from objective findings and identify where key respiratory information appears in the chart.

Welcome to Respiratory Patient Assessment

Assessment means collecting clues about how a patient is doing. A respiratory therapist uses those clues to decide whether the patient is stable, getting worse, responding to therapy, or needing urgent help.

Plain language:
Assessment means “What do I notice, measure, hear, and ask?”
Clinical term:
Respiratory assessment is the focused evaluation of breathing, oxygenation, ventilation, and response to therapy.
Why RTs care:
Good assessment prevents guessing. It helps the RT choose safe, appropriate care.

The RT Detective Framework

In early training, think of assessment as detective work. First, look at the patient. Then ask questions. Then review the chart. Then connect the information. Later in the program, you will use this same process to make more advanced decisions.

First Impression Assessment

Before touching equipment or asking a long list of questions, the RT first asks: “Does this patient look stable?” This first look often takes less than one minute.

What to NoticePlain MeaningWhy It Matters
Breathing rateIs breathing too fast, too slow, or paused?Abnormal rate can be an early sign of distress.
Work of breathingDoes breathing look hard?Hard breathing can lead to fatigue.
PostureIs the patient sitting upright, leaning forward, or unable to lie flat?Patients often position themselves to breathe easier.
Skin colorNormal, pale, blue/gray, flushed?Blue/gray color may suggest poor oxygenation.
Mental statusAwake and talking? Confused? Drowsy?Changes can signal poor oxygenation, poor ventilation, or severe illness.

Vital Signs & Oxygenation Basics

Vital signs are measurable clues about how the body is functioning. Early RT students do not need to know every advanced interpretation yet, but they should know what each vital sign means and when a value deserves attention.

RR: Respiratory Rate
How many breaths per minute. A common adult resting range is about 12–20/min.
SpO₂: Oxygen Saturation
A pulse oximeter estimate of how much hemoglobin is carrying oxygen. Many adults are commonly expected to be about 95–100%, though target ranges can vary by condition.
HR: Heart Rate
How many heart beats per minute. A common adult resting range is about 60–100/min.
BP: Blood Pressure
The pressure of blood against artery walls. Very low blood pressure can reduce oxygen delivery to tissues.

What does “2 L NC” mean?

2 L NC means the patient is receiving oxygen through a nasal cannula at 2 liters per minute. A nasal cannula is the small tubing that sits under the nose. In this lesson, focus on the basic idea: the patient needs some oxygen support.

Patient Communication & Interviewing

Interviewing is how the RT gathers the patient’s story. Good communication helps the patient feel respected and gives the RT better information.

Interview StepWhat to Say or AskWhy It Matters
Introduce yourself“Hi, I’m Melissa, a respiratory therapy student working with your care team.”Clarifies your role and builds trust.
Explain purpose“I’m going to ask a few breathing-related questions and check how you are doing.”Reduces anxiety and improves cooperation.
Ask symptoms“Are you feeling short of breath right now?”Identifies the patient’s current experience.
Ask smoking/vaping history“Do you currently smoke or vape, or have you in the past?”Helps estimate respiratory risk and exposures.
Ask meds/allergies“What breathing medications do you use at home?”Connects home baseline to current therapy.

Subjective vs Objective Data

Subjective data is what the patient reports. Objective data is what the RT observes, measures, or finds in the chart.

Subjective
“I feel short of breath.”
“My chest hurts.”
“I smoked for 20 years.”
Objective
SpO₂ 88%.
RR 30/min.
Wheezes heard bilaterally.
Chest x-ray report.
Why it matters
RTs use both types of data. A number without the patient’s story is incomplete; a story without measurement may miss severity.

Introduction to Chart Review

The chart, or electronic health record, helps the RT prepare before entering the room. At this beginner stage, your goal is not to interpret every advanced result. Your goal is to know where important respiratory information lives.

OrdersCurrent respiratory treatments, oxygen orders, frequency, and therapy instructions.
Medication Administration Record (MAR)When medications or breathing treatments were actually given.
History & Physical (H&P)Reason for admission, medical history, social history, and chief complaint.
Labs / Blood GasesMeasured blood values. Full ABG interpretation comes later.
ImagingChest x-ray or CT reports. Detailed imaging interpretation comes later.
RT Notes / FlowsheetPrevious RT assessments, treatments, oxygen device, and response to therapy.

FAQ

Common Beginner Questions

Do RTs diagnose diseases?

RTs assess, interpret respiratory information, provide ordered care, follow protocols, and communicate concerns. Diagnosis is provider-led, but RT findings strongly support clinical decision-making.

What if I do not know every abbreviation yet?

That is expected. Early lessons introduce abbreviations slowly. Always translate them into plain language first.

Why ask smoking or vaping history?

Smoking and vaping can affect airway inflammation, mucus production, gas exchange, disease risk, and response to therapy.

Why does the patient’s appearance matter if we have monitors?

Monitors give numbers, but appearance shows work of breathing, distress, fatigue, and whether the patient is compensating or worsening.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Respiratory assessment begins with a safe, organized approach: look at the patient, communicate respectfully, gather symptoms, review the chart, separate subjective from objective data, and recognize basic signs of concern.

Must know:
Assessment comes before intervention.
Clinical pearl:
A patient’s appearance can reveal distress before a full chart review is complete.
Board pearl:
NBRC-style questions often ask what the RT should assess, recognize, or do next.
Red flag:
Exhaustion, bluish color, inability to speak, or very low SpO₂ requires prompt attention.
