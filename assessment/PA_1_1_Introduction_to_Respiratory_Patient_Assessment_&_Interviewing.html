Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners should be able to explain the purpose of respiratory patient assessment, recognize basic signs of respiratory concern, gather beginner-level patient history, review common chart locations, and organize findings as subjective or objective data.

1. Describe the RT assessment mindset
Explain why RTs assess before selecting or changing respiratory care.
2. Recognize first-look concerns
Identify basic signs of respiratory distress such as abnormal breathing effort, color, posture, or mental status.
3. Gather patient history respectfully
Use professional communication to ask about symptoms, smoking/vaping, medications, and allergies.
4. Organize assessment data
Separate subjective reports from objective findings and identify where key respiratory information appears in the chart.

Week 1.1 Competency Outcomes

This lesson also prepares you to locate and verify respiratory information, distinguish the current problem from baseline, conduct a focused respiratory interview, recognize instability, identify unreliable or conflicting data, and communicate or escalate concerns clearly.

2-Minute Lesson Overview

Welcome to Respiratory Patient Assessment

Assessment means collecting clues about how a patient is doing. A respiratory therapist uses those clues to decide whether the patient is stable, getting worse, responding to therapy, or needing urgent help.

Plain language:
Assessment means “What do I notice, measure, hear, and ask?”
Clinical term:
Respiratory assessment is the focused evaluation of breathing, oxygenation, ventilation, and response to therapy.
Why RTs care:
Good assessment prevents guessing. It helps the RT choose safe, appropriate care.

The RT Detective Framework

In early training, think of assessment as detective work. First, look at the patient. Then ask questions. Then review the chart. Then connect the information. Later in the program, you will use this same process to make more advanced decisions.

First Impression Assessment

Before touching equipment or asking a long list of questions, the RT first asks: “Does this patient look stable?” This first look often takes less than one minute.

What to NoticePlain MeaningWhy It Matters
Breathing rateIs breathing too fast, too slow, or paused?Abnormal rate can be an early sign of distress.
Work of breathingDoes breathing look hard?Hard breathing can lead to fatigue.
PostureIs the patient sitting upright, leaning forward, or unable to lie flat?Patients often position themselves to breathe easier.
Skin colorNormal, pale, blue/gray, flushed?Blue/gray color may suggest poor oxygenation.
Mental statusAwake and talking? Confused? Drowsy?Changes can signal poor oxygenation, poor ventilation, or severe illness.

When the First Look Is Urgent

Interrupt the routine interview. Assess immediately, summon additional help or activate the institutional emergency response according to policy, remain with the patient, and continue monitoring and support within scope. Immediate patient safety comes before resolving assignment boundaries.

Vital Signs & Oxygenation Basics

Vital signs are measurable clues about how the body is functioning. Early RT students do not need to know every advanced interpretation yet, but they should know what each vital sign means and when a value deserves attention.

RR: Respiratory Rate
How many breaths per minute. A common adult resting range is about 12–20/min.
SpO₂: Oxygen Saturation
A pulse oximeter estimate of how much hemoglobin is carrying oxygen. Many adults are commonly expected to be about 95–100%, though target ranges can vary by condition.
HR: Heart Rate
How many heart beats per minute. A common adult resting range is about 60–100/min.
BP: Blood Pressure
The pressure of blood against artery walls. Very low blood pressure can reduce oxygen delivery to tissues.

What does “2 L NC” mean?

2 L NC means the patient is receiving oxygen through a nasal cannula at 2 liters per minute. A nasal cannula is the small tubing that sits under the nose. In this lesson, focus on the basic idea: the patient needs some oxygen support.

Patient Communication & Interviewing

Interviewing is how the RT gathers the patient’s story. Good communication helps the patient feel respected and gives the RT better information.

Interview StepWhat to Say or AskWhy It Matters
Introduce yourself“Hi, I’m Melissa, a respiratory therapy student working with your care team.”Clarifies your role and builds trust.
Explain purpose“I’m going to ask a few breathing-related questions and check how you are doing.”Reduces anxiety and improves cooperation.
Ask symptoms“Are you feeling short of breath right now?”Identifies the patient’s current experience.
Ask smoking/vaping history“Do you currently smoke or vape, or have you in the past?”Helps estimate respiratory risk and exposures.
Ask meds/allergies“What breathing medications do you use at home?”Connects home baseline to current therapy.

Expand the Focused Respiratory History

After the opening question, clarify onset and course; provoking or relieving factors; current versus usual dyspnea and function; cough and sputum; associated symptoms; home oxygen, inhalers, nebulizers, PAP, and airway-clearance devices; smoking, vaping, occupational, environmental, and infectious exposures; and any language, literacy, sensory, cognitive, or cultural needs that affect communication.

Interview DomainExample Follow-UpWhy It Matters
Onset and course“When did it begin? Sudden or gradual? Better, worse, or unchanged?”Establishes timing and possible urgency.
Baseline and function“What is your usual breathing and oxygen use? How far can you normally walk?”Distinguishes chronic findings from meaningful change.
Cough and sputum“Dry or productive? Has the amount, color, thickness, odor, blood, or ability to clear changed?”Clarifies secretion burden and infection or bleeding concerns.
Home therapies“What oxygen, inhalers, nebulizers, PAP, or airway-clearance equipment do you actually use?”Separates prescribed therapy from actual use and benefit.
Associated symptoms“Any chest pain, fever, chills, swelling, palpitations, fainting, or recent illness?”Identifies findings that may change urgency or the next assessment.
Learning and communication“What language do you prefer, and how would you like this explained?”Supports qualified interpretation, plain language, and teach-back.

Subjective vs Objective Data

Subjective data is what the patient reports. Objective data is what the RT observes, measures, or finds in the chart.

Subjective
“I feel short of breath.”
“My chest hurts.”
“I smoked for 20 years.”
Objective
SpO₂ 88%.
RR 30/min.
Wheezes heard bilaterally.
Chest x-ray report.
Why it matters
RTs use both types of data. A number without the patient’s story is incomplete; a story without measurement may miss severity.

Chief Complaint, HPI, Baseline & Source Attribution

Chief complaint
The patient’s main reason for seeking care, ideally in the patient’s own words.
History of present illness
How the current problem began and developed: onset, course, severity, triggers, relief, associated symptoms, and functional effect.
Baseline respiratory status
Usual breathing, function, support, cough/sputum pattern, and home therapy before the current change.
Observation versus interpretation
“Accessory-muscle use is present” is an observation; “the patient may be tiring” is an interpretation supported by several findings.

Source matters: A home SpO₂ value reported by the patient remains subjective until independently verified. A chart note may also contain copied or patient-reported information; identify whether the source is the patient, family, a prior note, a monitor, or your own assessment.

Introduction to Chart Review

The chart, or electronic health record, helps the RT prepare before entering the room. At this beginner stage, your goal is not to interpret every advanced result. Your goal is to know where important respiratory information lives.

OrdersCurrent respiratory treatments, oxygen orders, frequency, and therapy instructions.
Medication Administration Record (MAR)When medications or breathing treatments were actually given.
History & Physical (H&P)Reason for admission, medical history, social history, and chief complaint.
Labs / Blood GasesMeasured blood values. Full ABG interpretation comes later.
ImagingChest x-ray or CT reports. Detailed imaging interpretation comes later.
RT Notes / FlowsheetPrevious RT assessments, treatments, oxygen device, and response to therapy.

Choose the Source That Answers the Question

Orders show what was prescribed. The MAR or treatment documentation shows what was actually administered. Flowsheets show serial vital signs, oxygen device/settings, and monitoring trends. RT notes show assessment, intervention, response, education, communication, and reassessment. Always review timestamps and confirm that the record matches the bedside.

Advance Directives and DNR

A wristband, handoff statement, or prior note should prompt verification of the current active order and institutional policy. DNR limits resuscitative efforts as specified; it does not automatically prohibit routine respiratory assessment, oxygen, respiratory medication, or comfort-focused care.

Missing Data Are Unknown Data

A blank oxygen-device field does not mean room air. Verify the patient, device, setting, time, activity, position, intervention, and current condition before interpreting the result.

Data Reliability, Trend Validity & Resolving Contradictions

Clinical reasoning is only as reliable as the data used. Before comparing values or calling a change a trend, look for four common data problems.

Missing
SpO₂ without oxygen support, dyspnea without baseline, or a value without time or activity context.
Contradictory
Chart says room air while oxygen is connected; order differs from the device; note says improved while work of breathing is worse.
Outdated
Admission findings no longer represent the patient after treatment, activity, transfer, deterioration, or a support change.
Unreliable
Motion, poor perfusion, probe error, wrong patient, transcription/copying error, device malfunction, or a value that does not fit the patient.

Verify Before You Compare

  1. Assess the patient and determine urgency.
  2. Inspect the oxygen or respiratory device and verify the current setting.
  3. Repeat questionable measurements and check signal quality, perfusion, and probe placement.
  4. Review timestamps, orders, MAR, flowsheets, and notes; determine when a change occurred.
  5. Ask the patient or care team when appropriate and reconcile the record according to policy.

Do not remove needed oxygen merely to reproduce an earlier room-air value. Do not average conflicting values or silently choose the one you prefer.

Communication, Escalation & Reassessment

Communicate the respiratory problem—not just the number. A useful report identifies what is happening now, what has changed, how much support is being delivered, and what help is needed.

Identify and describe
Patient, current concern, appearance, mental status, speech, RR/pattern, work of breathing, and relevant symptoms.
State support and trajectory
SpO₂ with exact device and setting, change from baseline, and whether support needs are increasing.
State urgency and next need
Use specific language such as “less responsive than baseline” or “SpO₂ falling despite increased oxygen.” Request the appropriate immediate response.
Close the loop and reassess
Confirm the message was received, clarify who is taking the next action, remain with an unstable patient, and reassess after any change.

Patient Safety Before Workflow

If notified that a patient is suddenly struggling to breathe and becoming less responsive—even when the patient is not on your assignment—go assess immediately, mobilize help according to policy, and clarify ongoing assignment after urgent needs are addressed.

Case Wrap-Up

RT Takeaway: A beginner assessment does not require advanced interpretation. Start by recognizing appearance, breathing effort, basic vitals, patient symptoms, and where to find chart information.
Clinical thinking: The patient’s story plus measured data gives a clearer picture than either one alone.
Verify before comparing: Device, setting, time, activity, position, intervention, and signal quality determine whether values form a meaningful trend.
Escalate clearly: State the patient’s appearance, measured findings, current support, change from baseline, urgency, and the help needed.

FAQ

Common Beginner Questions

Do RTs diagnose diseases?

RTs assess, interpret respiratory information, provide ordered care, follow protocols, and communicate concerns. Diagnosis is provider-led, but RT findings strongly support clinical decision-making.

What if I do not know every abbreviation yet?

That is expected. Early lessons introduce abbreviations slowly. Always translate them into plain language first.

Why ask smoking or vaping history?

Smoking and vaping can affect airway inflammation, mucus production, gas exchange, disease risk, and response to therapy.

Why does the patient’s appearance matter if we have monitors?

Monitors give numbers, but appearance shows work of breathing, distress, fatigue, and whether the patient is compensating or worsening.

Does a blank oxygen-device field mean room air?

No. Missing data are unknown data. Inspect the patient and equipment, verify the current device and setting, identify when changes occurred, and reconcile the record.

Does DNR mean “do not treat”?

No. Verify the current active order and follow policy. DNR limits resuscitative efforts as specified; it does not automatically prohibit routine respiratory assessment, oxygen, medication, or comfort-focused treatment.

Why might the chart and bedside disagree?

Documentation can be delayed, copied, incomplete, mislabeled, or outdated. The patient may also have changed since the last entry. Current bedside findings and historical data must be placed into the correct timeline.

Why use a qualified interpreter and teach-back?

Communication barriers can make otherwise correct assessment data unreliable. Qualified interpretation supports accurate communication, and teach-back confirms understanding without relying only on “Do you understand?”

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Respiratory assessment begins with a safe, organized approach: look at the patient, communicate respectfully, gather symptoms, review the chart, separate subjective from objective data, and recognize basic signs of concern.

Must know:
Assessment comes before intervention.
Clinical pearl:
A patient’s appearance can reveal distress before a full chart review is complete.
Board pearl:
NBRC-style questions often ask what the RT should assess, recognize, or do next.
Red flag:
Exhaustion, bluish color, inability to speak, or very low SpO₂ requires prompt attention.

Competency Alignment Pearls

Verify before you compare. A trend requires known support, timing, activity/position, intervention, patient condition, and measurement quality. Communicate the patient—not only the number. Include the objective findings, exact support, change from baseline, urgency, and next needed action.
