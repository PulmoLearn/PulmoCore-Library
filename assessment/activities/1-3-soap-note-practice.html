Pulmonary Assessment • 1.3 Practice

SOAP Notes: Turn Assessment Data Into a Respiratory Story

Sort what the patient reports, what you measure, what the findings support, and what should happen next.

Start with the source of the information

SOAP is not four unrelated boxes

A good SOAP note tells one connected clinical story. The source of the information determines whether it is subjective or objective. Your interpretation must be supported by the data, and your plan must respond to that interpretation.

S

Subjective

What the patient or caregiver reports: symptoms, history, home therapy, perceived severity, and functional change.

Example: “My chest feels tight.”

O

Objective

What you measure, observe, or verify: vital signs, SpO₂ with oxygen setting, breath sounds, work of breathing, labs, and imaging results.

Example: RR 28/min; bilateral expiratory wheezes.

A

Assessment

Your supported respiratory interpretation. State what the findings support without inventing an unconfirmed medical diagnosis.

Example: Increased work of breathing with airflow-obstruction findings.

P

Plan

The next safe action: intervention, monitoring, education, communication, and when/how you will reassess.

Example: Give ordered bronchodilator; reassess symptoms, HR, RR, SpO₂, and breath sounds.

Key rule: Something does not become objective just because it is written in the chart. If the chart contains a patient-reported symptom or home history, the source is still subjective unless it has been independently measured or verified.
Another key rule: “Tolerated well” is not a complete evaluation. A respiratory note should show what was present before therapy, what was done, and what changed afterward.

Build the note from a real interaction

Conversation → respiratory SOAP note

Patient interview and bedside findings

RT: “Tell me what has changed with your breathing.”

Patient: “Since this morning I feel tighter in my chest and I get winded walking to the bathroom.”

RT: “Do you use an inhaler at home?”

Patient: “Albuterol, two puffs as needed. I usually need it maybe twice a month, but I used it three times yesterday.”

RT assessment: Alert, speaking full sentences. RR 26/min, HR 104/min, SpO₂ 91% on room air. Mild accessory-muscle use. Bilateral expiratory wheezes.

Order: Albuterol by metered-dose inhaler now.

Independent application

Find the SOAP information hidden in the paragraph

Scenario: Maria says, “My breathing has been worse since last night and my chest feels tight.” She reports using her rescue inhaler four times today compared with once or twice per week normally. She denies fever. On assessment she is alert and anxious, RR 30/min, HR 112/min, SpO₂ 89% on room air, with moderate accessory-muscle use and diffuse expiratory wheezes. The therapist determines that the findings support increased work of breathing with airflow obstruction and low oxygen saturation. Ordered oxygen and bronchodilator therapy are initiated. The therapist will reassess symptoms, HR, RR, SpO₂ and oxygen support, work of breathing, and breath sounds after treatment and notify the provider if the patient does not improve or worsens.

S
Subjective: Patient reports breathing worse since last night with chest tightness. Rescue inhaler used four times today compared with usual once or twice per week. Denies fever.
O
Objective: Alert and anxious. RR 30/min, HR 112/min, SpO₂ 89% on room air. Moderate accessory-muscle use and diffuse expiratory wheezes.
A
Assessment: Increased work of breathing with airflow-obstruction findings and low oxygen saturation.
P
Plan: Initiate ordered oxygen and bronchodilator therapy. Reassess symptoms, HR, RR, SpO₂ and oxygen support, work of breathing, and breath sounds after treatment. Notify provider/escalate if the patient fails to improve or worsens.
S vs O: Ask, “Who is the source?” Patient/caregiver report is subjective; clinician measurement or direct observation is objective.
A vs P: Assessment says what the evidence supports. Plan says what you will do, monitor, communicate, and reassess.
