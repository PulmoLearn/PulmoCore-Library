Protocol Decisions: Assess, Score, Treat & Reassess

Use therapist-driven protocols as structured clinical tools—not automatic order sets. Build a concise report, classify SOAPIER documentation, interpret the course severity ranges, identify the respiratory problem actually supported by the findings, choose a safe modality, and use reassessment to escalate, de-escalate, discontinue, or clarify therapy.

A protocol expands structured decision-making—not unlimited authority

Competency focus: Explain protocol purpose, scope, authorization, reassessment requirements, and the situations that require provider communication or emergency escalation.

Protocol can

Link approved assessment findings to defined initiation, modification, discontinuation, documentation, and reassessment actions when the therapist is qualified and criteria are met.

Protocol cannot

Authorize improvisation outside scope, competency, policy, approved criteria, or required prescriber/medical-staff authorization.

Instability overrides

Severe distress, exhaustion, apnea, altered mental status, inability to protect the airway, hemodynamic instability, rapidly increasing support needs, or impending arrest require stabilization and escalation—not routine scoring alone.

Build the eight-part respiratory report

Competency focus: Include enough relevant information that the next clinician can understand the current problem, treatment response, and next modification or weaning plan.
1. Who? Demographics and admission context
2. Why here? Chief complaint
3. What history matters? Relevant respiratory and safety history
4. What is happening now? Current assessment
5. What do diagnostics show? Imaging, blood gas, labs, sputum or other relevant data
6. What therapy is being delivered? Device, medication/dose, frequency, oxygen setting
7. Did it work safely? Response, no response, deterioration, tolerance, adverse effects
8. What happens next? Modification/weaning plan and reassessment interval

Which report element is missing?

Handoff: “68-year-old admitted with COPD exacerbation. RR 24, SpO₂ 91% on 2 L/min, mild expiratory wheeze. CXR without focal infiltrate. Albuterol 2.5 mg by nebulizer QID; last treatment at 0800. Plan is to continue QID.”

Find the respiratory problem before naming the device

Competency focus: Differentiate oxygenation, bronchospasm, retained secretions, lung-expansion need, and ventilatory-support failure from the assessment findings.

Oxygenation

Low saturation or PaO₂, rising oxygen requirement.

Bronchospasm

Expiratory wheeze, tightness, airflow obstruction, bronchodilator response.

Secretions

Thick sputum, coarse sounds, ineffective cough, retained mucus.

Lung expansion

Atelectasis, reduced inspiratory volume, postoperative expansion problem.

Ventilatory support

Rising PaCO₂/ETCO₂, fatigue, worsening work, altered mentation or airway-protection concern.

Choose the simplest safe modality that addresses the supported problem

Competency focus: Consider cooperation, interface tolerance, device limits, contraindications, and whether one modality safely addresses one or more independently supported indications.

Simple face mask

Requires adequate minimum flow to flush exhaled gas. Course reminder: do not operate below 5 L/min.

IPV

Can combine oscillatory secretion mobilization with positive-pressure lung expansion when both indications are present and the patient can safely tolerate the therapy.

MDI vs nebulizer

Select according to severity, coordination, inspiratory flow, interface, availability, and discharge needs—not the assumption that one device is always stronger.
