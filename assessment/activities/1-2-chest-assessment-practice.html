First understand what the assessment is detecting

Respiratory physical assessment is not a list of disease facts. Start by deciding where the change is, then what the tissue or air beneath your hands is doing, and finally whether all findings tell the same story.

Inspection — Where and how is breathing changing?

Look before touching.

  • Compare right with left for symmetry.
  • Notice whether the change is localized or widespread.
  • Describe work of breathing, posture, speech, respiratory pattern, color, and mental status.

Palpation — What moves or transmits?

Feel expansion, tracheal position, and vibration.

  • Reduced expansion on one side points to a localized problem.
  • A large pressure process can push the trachea away.
  • Major volume loss can pull the trachea toward the affected side.

Percussion — How much air or density is underneath?

Percussion estimates underlying air versus density.

  • More air tends to sound more hollow.
  • Fluid or poorly aerated dense tissue tends to sound duller.
  • Interpret the tone with location, symmetry, fremitus, and breath sounds.

Auscultation — How is air moving?

Listen for intensity, quality, and added sounds.

  • Narrowed lower airways can create wheezing and prolonged exhalation.
  • Markedly reduced airflow on one side is different from diffuse quiet breath sounds.
  • Coarse sounds that change after coughing suggest movable material in larger airways.

Disease context you already have

Use these broad ideas as starting clues. They intentionally do not give you the complete chest-assessment pattern.

Asthma
Episodes of lower-airway narrowing and bronchoconstriction usually involve both lungs.Think about how narrowed airways affect exhalation and air movement.
Chronic bronchitis
Chronic airway inflammation and mucus production create a widespread airway problem.Think about what material in larger airways might sound or feel like.
Emphysema
Loss of elastic recoil and chronic air trapping create a widespread excess-air pattern.Think about what extra retained air would do to percussion and sound transmission.
Atelectasis
A region of lung is not fully expanded, so there is less aerated lung volume in that area.Think about density, expansion, and what major volume loss could do to nearby structures.
Pneumothorax
Air enters the pleural space around one lung and can reduce expansion of that side.Think about how free air between lung and chest wall affects percussion and transmitted vibration.
Pleural effusion
Fluid collects in the pleural space and can compress nearby lung, usually in a dependent region.Think about how fluid changes density and separates the lung from the chest wall.
Pattern rule: One finding rarely proves the answer. A reliable pattern is stronger when distribution, percussion, fremitus, tracheal position, and breath sounds agree in the same region.

Step 2 of the pattern

Could the process move the trachea?

Think in terms of push, pull, or stay centered. Tracheal shift is usually a large or severe change, not an early finding in every patient.

Disease patterns

Sort by the typical tracheal effect.

Usually stays midline

No major one-sided push or volume-loss pull.

May pull toward

Major loss of volume can pull structures toward the affected side.

May push away

Enough pressure or volume on one side can push structures away.

Memory without memorizing: Volume loss pulls; pressure or a large space-occupying collection pushes. A shift away with severe distress and hypotension is a high-risk pattern.

Big idea: Hyperresonance tells you there is more air than expected; dullness tells you the region is denser or less aerated. Neither word alone names the disease.
Step 4 of the pattern

How well does spoken vibration reach the chest wall?

Tactile fremitus becomes useful when you combine it with percussion. Ask whether vibration is being transmitted normally or blocked/reduced.

Variable / not the best first clue

Use the other findings to define the pattern.

Combine findings: Dullness + decreased fremitus suggests a different process than hyperresonance + decreased fremitus. The combination is more useful than either finding alone.
