Do not memorize disease rows before you understand what the assessment is detecting

A strong respiratory examination combines inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation. Each method answers a different question. The pattern becomes meaningful when the findings agree in the same location and fit the patient's condition.

1. Inspection — What can I see?

Purpose: identify effort, symmetry, pattern, posture, speech, color, and mental status before touching the patient.

  • Reduced movement on one side suggests a focal problem and should trigger side-to-side comparison.
  • Prolonged exhalation supports airflow obstruction.
  • Diffuse increased work of breathing may accompany obstruction, edema, or other generalized respiratory problems.
  • Fatigue, altered mental status, severe accessory-muscle use, or gray/cyanotic appearance changes the urgency of the exam.

2. Palpation — What moves or transmits?

Purpose: assess chest expansion, tracheal position, tactile fremitus, tenderness, and crepitus.

  • Increased fremitus: vibration travels better through dense lung that remains connected to an open airway → think consolidation.
  • Decreased fremitus: pleural air or pleural fluid separates lung from chest wall → think pneumothorax or effusion when other findings match.
  • Shift toward: substantial volume loss can pull the trachea/mediastinum toward the affected side.
  • Shift away: a large pressure or space-occupying process can push structures away.

3. Percussion — What is under the chest wall?

Purpose: estimate whether the region is normally air filled, unusually dense/fluid filled, or contains excess air.

  • Resonant: expected over normally aerated lung.
  • Dull: increased density → fluid, consolidated lung, or volume loss may be present.
  • Hyperresonant: excess air → air trapping/hyperinflation or pleural air.
  • Always compare matched right-left locations and repeat a surprising finding before interpreting it.

4. Auscultation — How is air moving?

Purpose: evaluate air-movement intensity, sound quality, and added/adventitious sounds.

  • Wheeze: narrowed lower airways; severe obstruction may become very quiet when airflow is poor.
  • Rhonchi: coarse airway sounds often associated with secretions and may change after cough.
  • Crackles: discontinuous sounds associated with fluid or reopening of small airways/alveoli; distribution matters.
  • Bronchial sounds or increased voice transmission in a peripheral focal area: support dense consolidated lung.
  • Stridor: upper-airway red flag requiring prompt airway and stability assessment.
Describe before you diagnose. “Markedly diminished breath sounds at the right base” is more useful and defensible than “bad lungs.” Physical findings support a pattern; they do not prove a medical diagnosis by themselves.

Physiology before labels

Ask: what kind of problem would create this cluster?

Start with the dominant physical process, then ask which clinical patterns are consistent with it.

Airway narrowing

Why: narrowed airways slow expiratory flow.

Clues: prolonged exhalation, diffuse wheeze, reduced expiratory airflow; hyperresonance may appear with air trapping.

Consistent with: asthma or another obstructive-airway pattern.

Excess air / hyperinflation

Why: more trapped air lowers the density detected by percussion.

Clues: diffuse bilateral hyperresonance, diminished sounds, prolonged expiration, reduced expansion.

Consistent with: emphysema/hyperinflation.

Pleural air

Why: air in the pleural space separates lung from chest wall.

Clues: unilateral hyperresonance + decreased fremitus + markedly diminished/absent sounds.

Consistent with: pneumothorax. Shift away with instability raises concern for tension physiology.

Pleural fluid

Why: fluid adds density but also separates the aerated lung from the chest wall.

Clues: focal/dependent dullness + decreased fremitus + decreased/absent sounds.

Consistent with: pleural effusion.

Dense lung tissue

Why: fluid/cells replace alveolar air while an open airway still transmits sound.

Clues: focal dullness + increased fremitus + bronchial sounds/crackles + increased voice transmission.

Consistent with: consolidation.

Volume loss

Why: a region loses aerated volume and may pull surrounding structures toward it.

Clues: reduced expansion, dullness, diminished sounds; significant loss may shift trachea/mediastinum toward the affected side.

Consistent with: atelectasis.

Alveolar/interstitial fluid

Why: fluid within lung tissue and airspaces creates diffuse inspiratory discontinuous sounds and impaired oxygenation.

Clues: bilateral crackles, dyspnea, orthopnea, tachypnea, hypoxemia, increased work of breathing.

Consistent with: pulmonary edema.

Large-airway secretions

Why: secretions create coarse vibration as air moves through larger airways.

Clues: rhonchi/coarse sounds that may change after coughing; wheeze can coexist if airway narrowing is present.

Consistent with: secretion-heavy bronchitic/airway-clearance patterns.

Core distinction to know: Pneumothorax, pleural effusion, and consolidation can all reduce breath sounds, but percussion and fremitus separate the patterns: air = hyperresonant/decreased fremitus; pleural fluid = dull/decreased fremitus; dense consolidated lung = dull/increased fremitus.

Focal vs diffuse

Focal/asymmetric findings suggest a localized pleural, parenchymal, or obstructive problem. Diffuse/bilateral findings more often support generalized obstruction, edema, or chronic hyperinflation.

Internally consistent?

If one finding says “excess air” but another says “dense consolidated lung,” do not force a diagnosis. Reposition, verify landmarks/equipment, repeat, and compare sides.

Urgent pattern?

Acute unilateral absent breath sounds + hyperresonance + decreased fremitus + falling SpO₂/hypotension is an emergency pattern. Do not finish a routine chart before escalating.
