Respiratory Failure: Recognize, Support & Escalate

Classify what is failing, identify the dominant mechanism, recognize fatigue and airway-protection loss, select the level of respiratory support that matches the problem, reassess the response early, and act immediately when noninvasive support or mechanical ventilation becomes unsafe.

0 of 8 sections complete

Respiratory failure is a syndrome—not one abnormal number

Competency focus: Classify hypoxemic, hypercapnic, mixed, chronic, and acute-on-chronic failure using ABG values, current oxygen support, bedside findings, and baseline.

Type 1

Primarily an oxygenation problem. In the course framework, PaO₂ below about 60 mmHg with PaCO₂ normal or low supports hypoxemic failure.

Type 2

Primarily a ventilation problem. Elevated PaCO₂ with acidemia supports acute hypercapnic failure.

Mixed

Severe hypoxemia and hypercapnia occur together, so both oxygenation and carbon-dioxide elimination are failing.

Compare the patient with the patient. A PaCO₂ of 60 mmHg may be a compensated baseline for one patient and a major acute deterioration for another.

Identify the dominant mechanism

Competency focus: Differentiate shunt, dead space, hypoventilation, and expiratory obstruction using integrated bedside, ABG, ETCO₂, imaging, and perfusion clues.
Shunt

Perfusion without ventilation. Collapsed or fluid-filled alveoli remain perfused. Oxygen alone may not fully correct the problem; recruitment may be needed.

Dead space

Ventilation without perfusion. Pulmonary embolism is the classic acute example and may widen the PaCO₂–ETCO₂ gap.

Hypoventilation

Failure of drive or ventilatory pump. PaCO₂ rises as effective alveolar ventilation falls.

Obstruction

Expiratory flow limitation with prolonged exhalation, wheeze or quiet chest, air trapping, and a possible shark-fin capnogram.

A quieter patient can be getting worse

Competency focus: Recognize early compensation, respiratory-muscle fatigue, late pre-arrest findings, and the meaning of a falling respiratory rate when the rest of the patient is deteriorating.

Early compensation

Tachypnea, tachycardia, anxiety, restlessness, retractions, accessory-muscle use, inability to speak full sentences.

Fatigue

Respiratory rate falls, breaths become shallow, cough weakens, chest movement and air entry decrease, and the patient appears less vigorous.

Late / pre-arrest

Obtundation, bradycardia, apnea or irregular respirations, cyanosis despite support, secretion-handling failure, and loss of airway protection.

Maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP)

−40−20−10 cm H₂O

More negative = stronger. Less negative = weaker. In this course, a MIP less negative than approximately −20 cm H₂O is concerning; −15 cm H₂O is severely weak.

Before NIV, ask whether the mask can be used safely

Competency focus: Assess airway protection, cooperation, secretion management, emesis/aspiration risk, interface suitability, and hemodynamic stability before selecting noninvasive support.

Good candidate traits

Alert enough to cooperate, can protect the airway, can manage secretions, tolerates the interface, and remains sufficiently hemodynamically stable for close monitoring.

Major danger signs

Active vomiting, apnea, arrest, severe or rapidly worsening altered consciousness, unmanageable secretions, facial trauma preventing a seal, severe instability, inability to cooperate.

Independent airway indication

Airway-protection failure can require invasive support even before the ABG becomes profoundly abnormal.

Match the support to the failing function

Competency focus: Distinguish oxygenation failure, recruitment need, ventilatory failure, and airway-protection failure before choosing oxygen, CPAP, bilevel support, or invasive ventilation.
Supplemental oxygen

Raises inspired oxygen concentration when ventilation and airway protection are preserved.

CPAP

Continuous distending pressure for oxygenation/recruitment when spontaneous ventilation is adequate.

Bilevel support

Adds inspiratory assistance to improve ventilation while EPAP supports oxygenation.

Invasive ventilation

Secures the airway and supports or replaces breathing when NIV is unsafe or inadequate.

Ventilator alarms: assess the patient before changing the alarm

Competency focus: Distinguish common high- and low-pressure causes and recognize pressure-related emergencies.

High pressure

Think increased resistance or reduced compliance: secretions, kinking, biting, bronchospasm, water in tubing, tube obstruction, reduced compliance, mainstem position, or pneumothorax.

Low pressure / low volume

Think leak or disconnection: separated circuit, loose connection, cuff leak, open suction port, chest-tube-related air leak when relevant, or accidental extubation.

Patient first: chest rise, breath sounds, SpO₂, ETCO₂, color, mental status, hemodynamics, tube position, and circuit integrity come before simply silencing or raising an alarm limit.
