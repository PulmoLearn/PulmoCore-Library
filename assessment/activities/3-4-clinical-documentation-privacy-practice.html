Assessment form or treatment form?

Competency focus: Select the respiratory care assessment form for an initial comprehensive evaluation or significant status change, and the treatment form for each individual therapy session and response.
Respiratory Care Assessment Form

Whole-patient respiratory picture: history, baseline, current support, physical findings, diagnostics, orders, respiratory assessment, plan, and reassessment trigger.

Respiratory Care Treatment Form

One treatment encounter: pre-treatment baseline, therapy delivered, mid-treatment findings when indicated, post-treatment comparison, adverse response, action, notification, and follow-up.

Timing: the treatment record is completed after each session, before leaving the patient-care area when feasible. A significant change may require both a treatment note and a new comprehensive assessment.

Document what happened before, during, and after therapy

Competency focus: Build a treatment record that another clinician can use to judge benefit, no change, deterioration, or adverse response.

Pre-treatment

Indication, modality/dose, HR, RR, SpO₂ plus oxygen setting, breath sounds, cough/secretions, work of breathing, symptoms, position, mental status.

Mid-treatment

Added for prolonged therapy according to policy: HR, RR, SpO₂, symptoms, tolerance, early adverse effects. It never replaces post-treatment reassessment.

Post-treatment

Repeat the findings needed to judge response. State exactly what changed, what did not change, what adverse effect occurred, and what happens next.

“Tolerated well” is not enough. A treatment can improve wheezing and still cause tachycardia, tremor, anxiety, chest discomfort, or paradoxical bronchospasm.

Course bronchodilator tachycardia rule

Correct the record without erasing history

Competency focus: Use objective, timely, complete documentation and approved correction, addendum, and late-entry processes that preserve the original audit trail.
Original entry at 1015: “Albuterol given. Patient tolerated well.”

At 1400 the therapist realizes: the patient actually reported palpitations after the 1000 treatment, and HR increased from 92 to 124/min.
The approved correction should preserve the original entry, identify the current entry time, identify the actual event time when known, add only verified information, identify the author, and follow policy. Do not backdate or silently overwrite.

PHI is identifiable health information—not just a patient name

Competency focus: Identify PHI and distinguish the HIPAA Privacy Rule, Security Rule, and transaction/code-set standards.
Privacy Rule

Appropriate use/disclosure and patient rights, including access and request for amendment through approved processes.

Security Rule

Administrative, physical, and technical protections for electronic PHI confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

Transaction/code-set standards

Standardized electronic health-care transactions and coding formats—not privacy rights or password rules.

A medical record number linked to a diagnosis is PHI. Removing the name or face does not automatically de-identify a case if other details can identify the patient.

Safeguards turn confidentiality into repeatable behavior

Competency focus: Distinguish administrative, physical, and technical safeguards and protect electronic, verbal, and paper information.

Administrative

Policies, training, access authorization, risk analysis, incident response, sanctions, contingency planning.

Physical

Restricted rooms, badge-controlled areas, screen positioning, locked storage, printer retrieval, shredding.

Technical

Unique credentials, authentication, role-based access, encryption, audit logs, automatic screen lock, secure messaging.

Never share credentials. Audit trails tie chart activity to user credentials. Curiosity access can be discoverable even if nothing is later shared.
