Pulmonary Function Testing Basics

This lesson introduces pulmonary function testing as a patient-assessment process whose value depends on selecting the right procedure, preparing the patient and equipment, obtaining acceptable and repeatable maneuvers, verifying data inputs, and protecting patient safety. You will learn when to accept and interpret, coach and repeat, correct and regenerate, or stop and escalate.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners should be able to explain what forced vital capacity measures, prepare a patient and spirometer for testing, coach the maneuver using clear patient-centered language, recognize common quality problems, and connect FVC to basic lung volumes.

1. Define FVC
Explain forced vital capacity in plain language before using the abbreviation.
2. Prepare the test
Identify patient information and setup steps needed before spirometry.
3. Coach technique
Use clear instructions for maximal inhalation, tight seal, blast exhalation, and continued effort.
4. Recognize poor quality
Identify hesitation, cough, leak, early termination, incomplete inhalation, glottic closure, obstruction, and variable effort.
5. Select the right test
Match spirometry, lung volumes, DLCO, MIP/MEP, MVV, and CPET to the clinical question.
6. Verify valid data
Confirm patient inputs, calibration status, acceptability, and repeatability before interpreting raw or percent-predicted values.
7. Protect patient safety
Recognize stop criteria and prioritize assessment and escalation over completing the procedure.

Pulmonary function testing is a family of procedures, not one interchangeable test. Choose the procedure that measures the physiologic property in question.

What Is Forced Vital Capacity?

Forced vital capacity, or FVC, is the total amount of air a patient can forcibly exhale after taking the deepest breath possible. The word forced matters: this is not normal quiet breathing. It is a maximal-effort maneuver that requires good instruction and patient cooperation.

FVC is both a measurement and a coached maneuver. Technique matters.

MeasurementMeaningBeginner Interpretation Role
FVCTotal forcibly exhaled volume after maximal inhalationMay be reduced by true physiology or by incomplete inhalation, early termination, pain, weakness, misunderstanding, or poor effort
FEV₁Volume exhaled during the first secondStrongly influenced by airway caliber, rapid start, cough, hesitation, and expiratory flow limitation
FEV₁/FVCFraction of FVC exhaled in the first secondA reduced ratio supports obstruction; a preserved ratio with low FVC suggests possible restriction but requires lung-volume confirmation
Plain language
“Take the deepest breath you can, then blast the air out as hard and long as possible.”
What it measures
The amount of air exhaled after a maximal inhalation.
Why RTs care
FVC helps evaluate lung volume, obstruction/restriction patterns, and test effort quality.

FVC and Basic Lung Volumes

Before students can explain FVC, they need a beginner-friendly picture of what breath is being measured. During FVC, the patient starts at a maximal inhalation and exhales as much air as possible. Residual volume remains in the lungs and is not exhaled.

TermBeginner ExplanationFVC Connection
Tidal Volume (VT)Air moved during a normal relaxed breath.Part of the air exhaled during FVC.
Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV)Extra air that can be inhaled after a normal breath in.Included because the patient starts with the deepest breath possible.
Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV)Extra air that can be pushed out after normal exhalation.Included when the patient keeps blowing out until empty.
Residual Volume (RV)Air that remains in the lungs after maximal exhalation.Not exhaled during FVC.

Pretest Record Review, Demographics & Patient Readiness

A technically excellent maneuver can still produce a misleading or unsafe result when the wrong patient, wrong test, wrong demographic inputs, or an unstable condition is used.

Verify Before TestingWhy It Matters
Patient identity, order, indication, and clinical questionConfirms the correct procedure is being performed for the intended reason.
Baseline symptoms and respiratory statusEstablishes the starting condition and makes deterioration recognizable.
Precautions and contraindicationsRecent procedures, unstable cardiovascular symptoms, severe acute illness, hemoptysis, pneumothorax risk, or other concerns may make maximal pressure unsafe according to policy.
Medication and preparation statusBronchodilators, smoking, exercise, meals, oxygen, and other factors are managed according to the order and laboratory policy.
Age, standing height, sex, units, and reference settingsPredicted values are calculated from entered data; incorrect inputs can falsely normalize or abnormalize the report.
Understanding and physical abilityLanguage, hearing, cognition, pain, fatigue, coordination, and neuromuscular weakness may require adaptation or may limit performance.

Equipment Setup, Calibration Verification & Infection Prevention

Reliable data require two valid systems: the patient must perform an acceptable maneuver, and the equipment must measure it accurately.

Equipment CheckRequired Action
Calibration or calibration-verification statusConfirm required verification has passed and no unresolved out-of-range condition is present.
Quality-control recordReview required volume checks, leak tests, biologic controls, gas checks, or device-specific records.
Patient and reference settingsConfirm correct patient record, units, reference equation, test type, and software configuration.
Breathing circuitInstall approved filter, mouthpiece, tubing, and sensor correctly; verify the pathway is unobstructed and leak-free.
Infection preventionPerform hand hygiene and use required filters, PPE, cleaning, disinfection, and patient-specific components.
Failed quality controlDo not report patient data. Correct the equipment problem and repeat testing when appropriate.

How to Coach the FVC Maneuver

FVC testing is a team effort between the patient and the RT. The patient supplies effort; the RT supplies clear coaching. A good explanation reduces anxiety and improves test quality.

Patient-friendly script:
“I’m going to have you take the deepest breath you possibly can. Then seal your lips tightly around the mouthpiece and blast the air out as hard and fast as you can. Keep blowing until I tell you to stop. I’ll coach you the whole time.”
Good coaching
“Big breath in… seal tight… blast out, keep going, keep going…”
Clear, timed, and encouraging.
Needs improvement
“Just breathe into this.”
Too vague for a maximal effort test.
Avoid
“You’re doing it wrong.”
Discouraging feedback can increase frustration.

Recognizing Good and Poor FVC Quality

Pulmonary function results are only useful if the maneuver is acceptable. The RT must recognize when a result may be affected by hesitation, cough, leak, early stopping, or poor understanding.

Quality ErrorLikely EffectFocused Correction
Hesitation or slow startReduces early flow and can distort FEV₁Coach immediate hard, fast blast
Cough early in maneuverInterrupts first-second flow and may invalidate FEV₁Allow recovery and repeat when safe
Leak or poor sealLowers measured flow and volumeCorrect mouthpiece fit and seal
Incomplete inhalationCan falsely reduce both FEV₁ and FVCCoach maximal inspiration before blast
Early terminationFalsely lowers FVC and may mimic restrictionCoach sustained exhalation until appropriate endpoint
Glottic closure / breath holdingCreates sudden flow cessation and truncates the maneuverRe-explain continuous exhalation and reassess discomfort
Mouthpiece obstructionReduces flow because of tongue, teeth, biting, filter, or positionObserve and correct the airway pathway
Variable effortProduces inconsistent curves and poor repeatabilityAddress understanding, fatigue, pain, and technique

Acceptability Before Repeatability

Each maneuver must first be technically acceptable. Repeatability compares acceptable efforts with one another. Three similar invalid maneuvers are not valid simply because they agree.

Acceptability
Was the individual attempt performed with an adequate start, seal, inhalation, sustained exhalation, and no disqualifying artifact?
Repeatability
Do the best acceptable efforts agree within the current standard and laboratory policy?
Interpretability
Only after quality and consistency are established should raw and predicted values receive physiologic meaning.

Accept, Repeat, Correct, or Stop?

Three maneuvers end abruptly after two seconds and produce similar low FVC values.
Three acceptable maneuvers have good starts, complete exhalations, and closely agreeing values.
Maneuvers are acceptable, but height is entered as 150 cm instead of 180 cm.
During a maneuver, the patient develops chest pressure, pallor, and presyncope.

Raw Values, Percent Predicted & the Low-FVC Decision

The raw FVC is the volume the patient exhaled. Percent predicted compares that raw measurement with a reference value generated from the entered demographics and selected reference equation.

FindingInterpretationNext Step
Low FVC with abrupt early curve endingsMay be artificially low because of early terminationCorrect the quality problem, allow rest, and repeat when safe
Low FVC after acceptable repeatable testingMay reflect restriction, air trapping, weakness, pain, or another limitationUse the ratio and clinical context; obtain complete lung volumes to confirm restriction
Good raw efforts with wrong height or unitsRaw values may remain valid, but percent predicted is invalidCorrect inputs and regenerate the report before interpretation
Unexpected results despite good observed effortMay reflect setup, sensor, leak, calibration, or software errorInvestigate equipment and QC before blaming physiology

Patient Monitoring, Stop Criteria & Urgent Response

A complete curve has no value if obtaining it exposes the patient to harm. Observe the patient and the curve together throughout repeated maximal efforts.

Stop CriteriaImmediate Response
New chest pressure, pallor, presyncope, syncope, severe distress, significant desaturation, altered responsiveness, or neurologic changeStop the maneuver, remove or release the mouthpiece, protect from falling, assess, support airway/oxygenation, obtain appropriate vital signs, and activate notification or emergency response.
Dizziness or feeling faintDo not stand the patient. Maintain a supported position and reassess before deciding whether testing may resume.
Persistent cough, pain, fatigue, or inability to safely coordinatePause, assess, rest, modify, defer, or discontinue according to condition and policy.

Helping a Frustrated Patient Complete the Test

Patients may become embarrassed, tired, or frustrated during FVC testing. The RT should encourage without shaming, give short clear instructions, allow rest as appropriate, and demonstrate again if needed.

Tip 1: Demonstrate first
Showing the maneuver is often clearer than only describing it.
Tip 2: Use simple phrases
“Big breath in… blast out… keep going…” works better than long explanations during the maneuver.
Tip 3: Reset calmly
If the first attempt is poor, explain one fix and try again.
Tip 4: Watch safety
Stop and reassess if the patient becomes dizzy, distressed, or unstable.

FAQ

Common FVC Questions

Is FVC a normal breath?

No. FVC is a maximal effort test: deepest breath in, then hard and sustained exhalation.

Why does coaching matter so much?

Poor coaching can lead to shallow inhalation, slow start, leak, or early stopping, which can make results look worse than the patient’s true ability.

Does residual volume come out during FVC?

No. Residual volume is the air that remains in the lungs after maximal exhalation.

What should the RT do if the patient is frustrated?

Stay calm, encourage the patient, explain one correction at a time, demonstrate again, allow rest if appropriate, and repeat when safe.

Do three similar attempts automatically make spirometry valid?

No. Each maneuver must first be acceptable. Three similar early-terminated or otherwise invalid attempts are not valid repeatable data.

Does low FVC prove restriction?

No. Low FVC may reflect restriction, air trapping, incomplete inhalation, early termination, pain, weakness, misunderstanding, or poor effort. Acceptable testing and complete lung volumes are needed to confirm restriction.

What happens if height is entered incorrectly?

The raw exhaled volume may remain valid, but the predicted comparison and percent predicted are invalid. Correct the height and regenerate the report before interpretation.

When must testing stop?

Stop and assess when new chest pressure, pallor, presyncope, syncope, severe distress, significant desaturation, altered responsiveness, neurologic change, or another concerning response occurs.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

FVC is a maximal forced exhalation after a full inhalation. The measurement depends on both lung function and technique. RTs must prepare the device, explain the test clearly, coach the maneuver, recognize quality problems, and repeat the test when needed and safe.

Must know:
FVC is not a normal breath; it is a maximal forced maneuver.
Board pearl:
Poor technique can falsely reduce results.
RT pearl:
Simple, confident coaching improves test quality.
Common mistake:
Accepting early termination as a valid full FVC.
Decision rule:
Accept and interpret, coach and repeat, correct and regenerate, or stop and escalate.
Validity pearl:
Acceptability comes before repeatability; quality comes before physiology.
Safety pearl:
Do not finish the breath at the expense of patient stability.
