ABG: pH 7.28, PaCO₂ 58 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 25 mEq/L. Which classification is correct at the weekly recall level?

Important: A PaCO₂ of 40 mmHg may be normal in a healthy adult but dangerously high relative to expected compensation in severe metabolic acidosis. Compare measured values with what the physiology should be doing.

Mixed Acid-Base Disorders

A mixed disorder occurs when two primary acid-base problems are present simultaneously — not one primary plus its compensation. The key clue: values that are impossible to explain by a single disorder with expected compensation. Often the pH is more extreme than either disorder alone would produce, or the secondary parameter moves in the wrong direction.

Preanalytic Blood-Gas Errors: Before the Sample Reaches the Analyzer

Many dangerous ABG errors occur during patient identification, collection, transport, or handling. A valid analyzer cannot repair an invalid sample.

Recognizing Analytical Errors in ABG Results

Beyond pre-analytical collection errors, analytical errors occur when the result itself is internally inconsistent or the analyzer, cartridge, calibration, quality control, units, interface, or transcription cannot be trusted. Blood-gas analyzers directly measure pH, PaCO₂, and PaO₂; bicarbonate and several derived values are calculated from the measured data.

The Henderson-Hasselbalch Consistency Check

pH, PaCO₂, and HCO₃⁻ are mathematically related by the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation. If the reported pH does not match what the equation predicts from the PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ values, an analytical error has occurred.

Practical rule: For every 10 mmHg rise in PaCO₂ above 40, pH should fall by ~0.08. For every 10 mEq/L drop in HCO₃⁻ below 24, pH should fall by ~0.15. If the reported pH does not match this pattern — reject the result.

Analytically impossible example: pH 7.12, PaCO₂ 43, HCO₃⁻ 27
pH is severely acidemic. PaCO₂ is normal. HCO₃⁻ is elevated (alkalotic direction). Neither parameter explains the acidemia. This combination is physically impossible — metabolic alkalosis does not cause severe acidemia. Result should be rejected and resampled.
pH Moving in the Wrong Direction

The pH should always move in the direction predicted by the primary abnormal parameter:

  • High PaCO₂ (hypoventilation) → pH should be low (acidemic)
  • Low PaCO₂ (hyperventilation) → pH should be high (alkalemic)
  • Low HCO₃⁻ → pH should be low (acidemic)
  • High HCO₃⁻ → pH should be high (alkalemic)

If the pH moves in the opposite direction from what the primary abnormal parameter predicts, an analytical or transcription error is highly likely.

Equipment-Related Errors to Know
  • Electrode contamination: Protein deposits or contaminating substances alter electrode response — particularly affects pH electrode. Results: pH drift, inconsistent readings.
  • Calibration failure: ABG analyzers calibrate automatically with reference gases. If calibration fails, results across all parameters may be systematically shifted. Quality control material should be run after any calibration failure.
  • Clotted sample entering analyzer: Fibrin clots partially or completely block sample pathways — values may be dramatically altered or the analyzer may reject the sample.
  • Temperature correction errors: Analyzers measure at 37°C. Hypothermic patients require temperature correction — uncorrected results may show falsely high PaO₂ and falsely low PaCO₂.

pH 7.28, PaCO₂ 60, HCO₃⁻ 28 — COPD with acute exacerbation.
pH 7.55, PaCO₂ 52, HCO₃⁻ 18 — no clinical context, sent from an unknown ward.

The Complete ABG Decision Tree

This 7-step framework integrates everything from prior ABG interpretation lessons into a single clinical workflow. Apply it in order to every ABG — simple or complex.

1
pH: <7.35 = Acidemia · 7.35–7.45 = Normal · >7.45 = Alkalemia
2
Primary problem: Which parameter matches pH direction? Elevated PaCO₂ + acidemia = Resp. Acidosis. Low HCO₃⁻ + acidemia = Met. Acidosis. And so on.
3
Apply compensation formula: Is the secondary parameter within the expected range? (Winter's for met. acidosis; ±3–4 mEq/L per 10 mmHg for resp. acidosis, etc.)
4
Mixed disorder check: If secondary is far OUTSIDE expected range — a second primary disorder is present. Name both.
5
Analytical error check: If pH direction contradicts the primary parameter (e.g., high HCO₃⁻ with acidemia) — the result is internally inconsistent. Reject and recollect.
6
Oxygenation: PaO₂ 80–100 = Normal · 60–79 = Mild · 40–59 = Moderate · <40 = Severe hypoxemia. Note FiO₂.
7
Clinical integration: Does the interpretation match the patient's presentation, history, medications, and recent interventions? If not — reconsider or recollect.

Apply the Decision Tree

ABG: pH 7.48, PaCO₂ 28, HCO₃⁻ 20. The patient was admitted for anxiety and panic attacks. RR 30 and labored. No metabolic history.

Applying compensation rules for resp. alkalosis: Expected HCO₃⁻ drop = -2–3 per 10 mmHg ↓ PaCO₂ from 40. PaCO₂ dropped 12 mmHg → expected HCO₃⁻ ≈ 24 – 2.4–3.6 = 20.4–21.6. Measured HCO₃⁻ = 20. What is the interpretation?

Serial ABG Trending — Reading the Direction of Change

A single ABG is a snapshot. Serial ABGs taken over time reveal whether the patient is improving, deteriorating, or not responding to treatment. The direction and magnitude of change is often more clinically actionable than any single value.

Electrolyte Shifts During Acid-Base Changes

Acid-base disorders cause predictable electrolyte redistribution — particularly potassium. These shifts affect treatment decisions and can create secondary complications that require separate management. The RT must anticipate these changes when interpreting combined ABG and electrolyte data.

Acidosis → Hyperkalemia

In acidemia, excess H⁺ ions move into cells to be buffered. To maintain electrical balance, K⁺ moves out of cells into the bloodstream.

Rule of thumb: For every 0.1 drop in pH → potassium rises ~0.6 mEq/L.

Clinical risk: ECG changes (tall peaked T waves, widened QRS). Severe hyperkalemia can cause lethal arrhythmias even during treatment of acidosis.

Alkalosis → Hypokalemia

In alkalemia, H⁺ ions move out of cells to help buffer the alkalosis. To maintain electrical balance, K⁺ moves into cells — dropping serum levels.

Rule of thumb: For every 0.1 rise in pH → potassium falls ~0.6 mEq/L.

Clinical risk: Muscle weakness, cardiac dysrhythmias. Correcting alkalosis without addressing hypokalemia can worsen both conditions.

Sodium — Indirect Relationships

Sodium itself does not shift significantly with pH changes, but its relationship to chloride and bicarbonate is central to the anion gap and NAGMA (non-anion gap metabolic acidosis) calculation covered in Foundations 3.3.

Key point: Hypernatremia causes osmotic shifts affecting pH buffering capacity. Always evaluate sodium alongside the ABG in critically ill patients.

Calcium & Ionized Calcium

pH affects calcium binding to albumin. In alkalosis: more calcium binds to albumin → ionized (free) calcium falls → symptomatic hypocalcemia (tetany, Chvostek's sign, carpopedal spasm).

In acidosis: less calcium bound → more free ionized calcium (clinically beneficial effect during acidosis).

Key point: Hyperventilating patients can develop tetany from acute respiratory alkalosis reducing ionized calcium. Correct the alkalosis — not just the calcium.

Point-of-Care ABG Technology

Point-of-care (POC) blood gas analyzers bring ABG testing to the bedside, the emergency department, and the operating room — dramatically reducing the time from sample collection to result. The respiratory therapist must understand each device's capabilities, limitations, and proper operation.

iStat (Abbott)

Handheld, cartridge-based POC analyzer. CLIA-waived for many cartridge types. Highly portable — used at bedside, transport, OR, and emergency settings.

Cartridges measure: pH, PaCO₂, PaO₂, HCO₃⁻, Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, ionized Ca²⁺, glucose, lactate, creatinine, hematocrit, troponin (cartridge-dependent).

Sample: 65–95 µL whole blood (arterial or venous). Cartridge must be at room temperature before use.

QC: Electronic simulators check device function. Liquid QC run per facility protocol. Cartridges have temperature and expiration limits.

RT role: Collect arterial sample, fill cartridge immediately (avoid air), insert within 5 minutes, document FiO₂ and patient temp.
ABL90 FLEX (Radiometer)

High-throughput floor-standing or benchtop analyzer. Common in ICUs, respiratory therapy departments, and labs requiring rapid high-volume testing.

Measures: Full blood gas panel + electrolytes + metabolites + co-oximetry (oxyHb, COHb, MetHb, tHb) + lactate + glucose + bilirubin.

Co-oximetry advantage: Detects carbon monoxide poisoning (COHb) and methemoglobinemia (MetHb) — conditions where SpO₂ gives false reassurance.

QC: Automated QC runs every 4 hours with manufacturer-supplied ampoules. Auto-calibration with reference gases.

RT role: Load sample via syringe or capillary, enter patient data (FiO₂, temp, mode), interpret printed co-oximetry report alongside standard ABG.
GEM Premier (IL)

Instrumentation Laboratory GEM series — self-contained, touch-screen interface with integrated QC system. Common in larger hospital labs and critical care areas.

Measures: pH, PaCO₂, PaO₂, electrolytes, glucose, lactate, hematocrit, hemoglobin, co-oximetry (full panel).

iQM (Intelligent Quality Management): Continuous, automated internal QC — flags results that fall outside quality limits without requiring manual QC intervention for every sample.

QC: iQM runs automatically. External liquid QC verification required per facility CLIA requirements.

RT role: Enter patient FiO₂ and temp before analysis. Review iQM flags on the result report. Never report a result flagged as QC failure.
Critical Point Across All Devices: Always enter the correct FiO₂ and patient temperature into the analyzer before or with each sample. Without these, the analyzer cannot calculate derived parameters correctly (A-a gradient, P/F ratio, temperature-corrected values). A result without accurate FiO₂ is incomplete.

Pulse Oximetry — Reliability and Limitations

Pulse oximetry (SpO₂) is the most widely used monitoring tool in respiratory care. It is non-invasive, continuous, and easy to use. But it has specific and serious limitations that every RT must know — because when SpO₂ lies, it is dangerous.

Capnography — End-Tidal CO₂ as an Assessment Tool

Capnography measures the CO₂ concentration in exhaled gas — providing real-time, continuous, non-invasive ventilation monitoring. End-tidal CO₂ (ETCO₂) is the peak CO₂ value at the end of expiration. It is a critical assessment tool because it reflects both ventilation and perfusion — and its waveform shape carries diagnostic information.

Normal ETCO₂ and Its Clinical Uses

Normal ETCO₂: 35–45 mmHg
Correlates with PaCO₂ within ~2–5 mmHg in patients with normal lung function. Rising ETCO₂ = hypoventilation. Falling ETCO₂ = hyperventilation, PE, or decreased cardiac output.
ETCO₂ vs. PaCO₂ Gradient
The ETCO₂-PaCO₂ gradient (normally 2–5 mmHg) widens with V/Q mismatch, PE, or decreased perfusion — areas of the lung ventilating but not perfusing contribute CO₂-free gas that dilutes ETCO₂.

Clinical Applications of Capnography

Clinical UseWhat to Look For
Endotracheal tube confirmationSustained ETCO₂ waveform across 6 breaths = tube in trachea. No waveform = esophageal intubation. Most reliable confirmation method available at bedside.
CPR quality monitoringETCO₂ <10 mmHg during CPR = poor chest compressions or no perfusion. Sudden rise in ETCO₂ during CPR = return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC).
Ventilation monitoring (sedated patients)Continuous ETCO₂ detects apnea, hypoventilation, and hyperventilation without repeated ABG draws.
Bronchospasm detection"Shark fin" waveform — loss of alveolar plateau with a rounded upslope and slow return to baseline. Indicates uneven gas emptying from obstructed airways.
Procedural sedation monitoringETCO₂ detects hypoventilation before SpO₂ drops — provides an earlier warning window for intervention.

Special Populations — Adjusted ABG Interpretation

Generic adult ranges are a starting point, not a substitute for the patient's documented baseline, altitude, oxygen setting, temperature, symptoms, and trend. Population-specific expectations can coexist with an acute superimposed disorder; “expected” does not automatically mean benign.

Pregnancy

Progesterone directly stimulates the respiratory center → chronic mild hyperventilation throughout pregnancy.

Expected pregnant baseline:
PaCO₂: 27–32 mmHg (normal for them — not respiratory alkalosis)
HCO₃⁻: 18–21 mEq/L (renal compensation for the chronic low PaCO₂)
pH: 7.40–7.45 (fully compensated)
PaO₂: slightly elevated (increased ventilation)
Key error: A pregnant patient with PaCO₂ 32 and HCO₃⁻ 20 appears to have "partially compensated respiratory alkalosis" by standard interpretation — but this is their normal baseline. Treat only if acutely worse than their baseline.

Chronic Lung Disease (COPD)

Chronic CO₂ retention with renal compensation creates a new "normal" for each COPD patient — different from population norms.

Example COPD baseline (patient-specific):
PaCO₂: 55–65 mmHg (their normal — fully compensated)
HCO₃⁻: 34–40 mEq/L (renal compensation)
pH: 7.35–7.42 (normalized)
PaO₂: 55–70 mmHg (their acceptable baseline on home O₂)
Key rule: Always know the patient's baseline ABG before treating. A COPD patient with PaCO₂ 65 and pH 7.38 may be at their compensated baseline — not in acute respiratory failure. Compare current values to prior ABGs.

Elderly Patients

Age-related lung changes (reduced elastic recoil, increased closing volume) cause a predictable decline in PaO₂ with age — even with healthy lungs.

Age-adjusted PaO₂ formula:
Expected PaO₂ ≈ 100 − (0.33 × age)
Example: 75-year-old → 100 − (0.33 × 75) = 100 − 24.75 = ~75 mmHg
This is their "normal" — not mild hypoxemia requiring intervention.
Key error: Treating a single PaO₂ cutoff without considering age, altitude, FiO₂, symptoms, perfusion, and trend. Age-estimation formulas are teaching approximations, not stand-alone treatment rules.

Endurance Athletes

High VO₂max, increased metabolic efficiency, and enhanced respiratory muscle strength create subtly different ABG baselines in elite athletes.

Typical athlete baseline (at rest):
PaCO₂: 36–40 mmHg (lower end of normal — efficient CO₂ clearance)
PaO₂: 95–105 mmHg (sometimes slightly above standard normal)
pH: 7.40–7.45
HCO₃⁻: 23–26 mEq/L (normal)
Note: Exercise-induced arterial hypoxemia (EIAHs) can occur in elite athletes at maximal exertion — PaO₂ may drop to 70–85 mmHg despite normal resting values. Not pathological during performance, but documented on exercise ABG testing.

FAQ

Advanced ABG Questions

How do I tell a mixed disorder from a fully compensated single disorder?

Apply the expected compensation formula. In a fully compensated single disorder, the secondary parameter falls within the formula's predicted range — and the pH returns to 7.35–7.45. In a mixed disorder, the secondary parameter is far outside the predicted compensation range. The formulas are the objective test: if the measured secondary value is outside the predicted range, a second primary disorder is present. If it falls within the range and pH is normalized — it is fully compensated, not a mixed disorder.

When would I see a normal pH in a mixed disorder?

When two primary disorders are opposing — one acidotic and one alkalotic. Metabolic acidosis + respiratory alkalosis, or metabolic alkalosis + respiratory acidosis, can partially or fully cancel each other's effect on pH. The pH appears normal but both individual parameters are abnormal and cannot be explained by single-disorder compensation. Clinical example: a COPD patient on furosemide may have pH 7.40, PaCO₂ 65, HCO₃⁻ 42 — pH looks normal but two active disorders are present.

Why does the iStat not replace the central lab ABL90 for all patients?

The iStat provides excellent portability and reasonable accuracy for routine ABG parameters. However, it uses cartridge-based electrochemical sensors that have more limited analyte panels than full-featured benchtop analyzers. For patients requiring co-oximetry (COHb, MetHb for CO poisoning or methemoglobinemia), a full electrolyte panel, lactate plus ABG in the same run, or bilirubin measurement — the ABL90 or GEM is superior. The iStat fills a specific niche: fast, portable, bedside testing when the full panel is not needed.

Why does alkalosis cause tetany but not other acid-base disorders?

Albumin (the major protein in blood) binds calcium. In alkalosis, albumin becomes more negatively charged, increasing its affinity for calcium — more calcium binds to albumin, leaving less as free ionized calcium (the physiologically active form). Low ionized calcium causes increased neuromuscular excitability: muscle cramps, carpopedal spasm (trousseau's sign), facial twitching (Chvostek's sign), and in severe cases laryngospasm and seizures. The fix is correcting the alkalosis, which releases calcium back to its ionized form — not just giving supplemental calcium.

How much earlier does capnography detect hypoventilation compared to pulse oximetry?

In clinical studies, capnography detects hypoventilation an average of 30–60 seconds to several minutes before SpO₂ falls below 90%. The delay exists because: (1) there is an oxygen reserve in the lungs and blood that must be depleted first, and (2) SpO₂ only drops steeply once PaO₂ falls below 60 mmHg on the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve. ETCO₂ rises immediately when minute ventilation decreases — no reserve to exhaust. For procedural sedation monitoring, capnography is now considered the standard of care in many settings because of this early warning advantage.

What is the difference between a preanalytic and analytical ABG error?

Preanalytic errors occur before analysis, such as wrong identity, air contamination, delay, venous admixture, line contamination, clotting, excess heparin, or missing oxygen context. Analytical errors involve analyzer, cartridge, calibration, quality control, units, interface, transcription, or physiologically impossible values.

Why can a PaCO₂ of 40 mmHg be dangerous in severe metabolic acidosis?

Winter's formula may predict a much lower compensatory PaCO₂. A measured value of 40 can therefore represent added respiratory acidosis from fatigue, CNS depression, or inadequate ventilation even though 40 is within a generic adult range.

When is transcutaneous monitoring useful?

It can provide a continuous noninvasive CO₂ or O₂ trend when repeated invasive sampling is undesirable, especially in selected neonatal, pediatric, sleep, or chronic-monitoring contexts. Sensor heat, placement, calibration, skin integrity, perfusion, edema, vasoconstriction, and lag must be considered.

Should an improving COPD patient be intubated because PaCO₂ is still above 45 mmHg?

Not from that number alone. Evaluate pH, direction of PaCO₂ change, respiratory rate, work of breathing, mental status, oxygenation, hemodynamics, airway protection, device tolerance, and documented baseline.

Summary — Advanced Assessment Integration

Mixed Disorders:
Secondary parameter outside expected compensation range = second primary disorder. Opposing disorders may normalize pH — both parameters still abnormal. Apply Winter's and compensation formulas to differentiate.
Analytical Errors:
pH moving opposite to primary parameter = reject and resample. Henderson-Hasselbalch must hold. Severe acidemia cannot coexist with normal PaCO₂ AND normal/elevated HCO₃⁻.
Technology:
iStat = portable, cartridge-based, limited co-ox. ABL90 = full co-ox + electrolytes + lactate. GEM = iQM continuous QC. Always enter FiO₂ and patient temperature. SpO₂ fails with CO, MetHb, dark skin, poor perfusion.
Special Populations:
Pregnancy and chronic hypercapnia require baseline-aware interpretation. Expected population values may still have a superimposed acute abnormality.
Preanalytic Validation:
Verify patient, source, time, oxygen/support conditions, air, delay, heparin, clot, line contamination, transport, and temperature before trusting the result.
Whole-Patient Trend:
Use pH, PaCO₂, SpO₂, ETCO₂, RR, work of breathing, mental status, hemodynamics, support settings, and tolerance to judge response.
Electrolytes + Acid-Base: Acidosis → K⁺ shifts out of cells → hyperkalemia. Alkalosis → K⁺ shifts in → hypokalemia. Alkalosis → ionized calcium falls → tetany risk. Every 0.1 pH change → ~0.6 mEq/L potassium change in the opposite direction.
