Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to build an ABG interpretation from the beginning and then use the result safely in clinical context.

  • Explain what pH, PaCO₂, HCO₃⁻, PaO₂, and SaO₂ tell you and recall the course reference ranges.
  • Use the direction rules — rising PaCO₂ lowers pH; rising HCO₃⁻ raises pH — to identify the four primary acid-base disorders.
  • Classify no/uncompensated, partially compensated, and fully compensated patterns using a consistent step-by-step method.
  • Recognize chronic compensated respiratory acidosis and acute-on-chronic respiratory acidosis by comparing pH, PaCO₂, HCO₃⁻, baseline, and clinical status.
  • Use Winter's formula in metabolic acidosis to decide whether respiratory compensation is appropriate.
  • Connect common ABG patterns with clinical situations that can produce them without diagnosing a disease from the ABG alone.
  • Recognize preanalytic and analytic blood-gas errors; select appropriate monitoring technology; and interpret serial trends with the whole patient.

Lesson Overview: Build the Skill One Step at a Time

This is your first formal ABG interpretation lesson. Begin with what each value means, learn the direction rules, identify the four basic disorders, and then add compensation. Only after that foundation is secure will you use Winter's formula, sample validation, monitoring technology, and clinical trends.

Start Here: What an ABG Tells You

An arterial blood gas (ABG) gives several pieces of information at the same time. Do not try to name the entire ABG at once. Read it in a fixed order.

ValueCourse ReferenceWhat it tells youDirection rule
pH7.35–7.45Overall acid-base direction<7.35 = acidemia; >7.45 = alkalemia
PaCO₂35–45 mmHgRespiratory component / ventilationCO₂ goes opposite pH: ↑CO₂ → ↓pH; ↓CO₂ → ↑pH
HCO₃⁻22–26 mEq/LMetabolic/renal componentHCO₃⁻ goes with pH: ↑HCO₃⁻ → ↑pH; ↓HCO₃⁻ → ↓pH
PaO₂ / SaO₂Context dependentOxygenationInterpret separately from acid-base status and include FiO₂/device.
Memory anchor: CO₂ is an acid, so it moves opposite the pH. Bicarbonate is a base, so it moves with the pH.

The Four Basic Disorders

PatternWhat matches the pH?Clinical situations where you may see it
Respiratory acidosispH ↓ and PaCO₂ ↑Hypoventilation from COPD exacerbation, sedatives/opioids, neuromuscular weakness, severe fatigue, or airway obstruction.
Respiratory alkalosispH ↑ and PaCO₂ ↓Hyperventilation from pain/anxiety, hypoxemia, fever/sepsis, pregnancy, pulmonary embolic disease, or excessive ventilatory support.
Metabolic acidosispH ↓ and HCO₃⁻ ↓Diabetic ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis/shock, renal failure, diarrhea/bicarbonate loss, or selected toxic ingestions.
Metabolic alkalosispH ↑ and HCO₃⁻ ↑Vomiting/gastric acid loss, nasogastric suction, diuretic/volume-contraction states, or excess alkali.
Important: An ABG pattern does not diagnose the cause. It tells you the acid-base pattern. History, examination, medications, laboratory data, and trends tell you why it is happening.

Worked Example: Do Not Skip Steps

ABG: pH 7.28, PaCO₂ 58 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 24 mEq/L

  1. pH 7.28 is acidic.
  2. PaCO₂ 58 is high. Rising CO₂ lowers pH, so this value explains the acidemia.
  3. HCO₃⁻ 24 is still normal.
  4. The primary disorder is respiratory acidosis.
  5. Because HCO₃⁻ has not moved outside its normal range, this is uncompensated respiratory acidosis.

Compensation: How the Body Tries to Bring pH Back Toward Normal

Once you can identify the primary disorder, ask whether the other body system is trying to move the pH back toward normal. The lungs change PaCO₂ quickly. The kidneys change HCO₃⁻ more slowly.

Primary problemWhat is abnormal first?Compensating systemCompensatory direction
Respiratory acidosisPaCO₂ ↑ → pH ↓Kidneys retain HCO₃⁻HCO₃⁻ ↑ to push pH upward
Respiratory alkalosisPaCO₂ ↓ → pH ↑Kidneys excrete HCO₃⁻HCO₃⁻ ↓ to push pH downward
Metabolic acidosisHCO₃⁻ ↓ → pH ↓Lungs increase ventilationPaCO₂ ↓ to push pH upward
Metabolic alkalosisHCO₃⁻ ↑ → pH ↑Lungs reduce ventilationPaCO₂ ↑ to push pH downward

A Beginner-Friendly Compensation Count

Uncompensated = pH + 1

The pH and one primary value are outside range. The other value has not yet moved outside its range.

Example: 7.28 / 58 / 24 → uncompensated respiratory acidosis. pH + 1 = two values out of range.

Partially compensated = pH + 2

The pH is still abnormal and both PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ are now outside range. The compensating system has responded, but pH is not normal yet.

Example: 7.30 / 60 / 29 → partially compensated respiratory acidosis. pH + 2 = all three values out of range.

Fully compensated = normal pH + 2

The pH has returned to 7.35–7.45 while PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ remain abnormal.

Example: 7.38 / 55 / 32 → fully compensated respiratory acidosis. Normal pH + 2 = pH is back in range while both other values remain abnormal.

When pH is normal, use 7.40 as your guide. A compensated simple disorder generally approaches 7.40 without crossing past it. A pH of 7.35–7.39 remains on the acid side; 7.41–7.45 remains on the alkaline side. Later, expected-compensation calculations help verify that the pattern truly fits simple compensation.

Why Chronic Respiratory Acidosis Looks Different

Rising PaCO₂ lowers pH. If the CO₂ stays elevated, the kidneys retain HCO₃⁻ to push the pH back upward. Renal compensation develops over hours to days and becomes more established over several days. A stable chronic CO₂ retainer may therefore have a high PaCO₂, high HCO₃⁻, and a pH back in the normal range.

SituationpHPaCO₂HCO₃⁻Interpretation
Stable chronic baseline7.376034Chronic / fully compensated respiratory acidosis
New acute illness on chronic baseline7.277835Acute-on-chronic respiratory acidosis

In the acute-on-chronic example, PaCO₂ has climbed above the patient's usual level and the pH has fallen again. The kidneys cannot immediately add enough new bicarbonate to correct the new CO₂ rise. Clinically, the patient may need help increasing ventilation — for example bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) or invasive ventilation when appropriate — so PaCO₂ falls and pH rises while the underlying cause is treated.

Is the Compensation Appropriate?

After you identify the primary disorder and decide whether compensation is present, ask one more question: Did the compensating system change by about the amount physiology predicts? For practice calculations, begin at the center of normal: pH 7.40 · PaCO₂ 40 mmHg · HCO₃⁻ 24 mEq/L.

Primary disorderExpected response
Acute respiratory acidosisFor every PaCO₂ ↑ 10 mmHg: pH ↓ about 0.08 and HCO₃⁻ ↑ about 1 mEq/L
Chronic respiratory acidosisFor every PaCO₂ ↑ 10 mmHg: pH ↓ about 0.03 and HCO₃⁻ ↑ about 4 mEq/L
Acute respiratory alkalosisFor every PaCO₂ ↓ 10 mmHg: pH ↑ about 0.08 and HCO₃⁻ ↓ about 2 mEq/L
Chronic respiratory alkalosisFor every PaCO₂ ↓ 10 mmHg: pH ↑ about 0.03 and HCO₃⁻ ↓ about 5 mEq/L
Metabolic acidosisExpected PaCO₂ = 1.5 × HCO₃⁻ + 8, ±2
Metabolic alkalosisExpected PaCO₂ ≈ 40 + 0.7(HCO₃⁻ − 24), ± about 2 mmHg
Example: PaCO₂ 60 is 20 mmHg above 40, or two 10-mmHg steps. Acute respiratory acidosis would predict a larger pH fall and only a small HCO₃⁻ rise; chronic respiratory acidosis would predict a smaller pH fall and a much larger HCO₃⁻ rise.

Preanalytic Blood-Gas Errors: Before the Sample Reaches the Analyzer

Many dangerous ABG errors occur during patient identification, collection, transport, or handling. A valid analyzer cannot repair an invalid sample.

ProblemTypical Effect or ConcernSafe Response
Wrong patient, wrong time, or missing support settingsThe number may be technically accurate but clinically assigned to the wrong context.Verify two identifiers, source, time, FiO₂/device/settings, recent intervention, and baseline.
Air bubblePaCO₂ tends to fall and pH tends to rise; PaO₂ moves toward room-air equilibrium and may rise or fall depending on the starting value.Withhold interpretation and recollect when contamination is clinically meaningful.
Delayed analysisOngoing cell metabolism lowers PaO₂, raises PaCO₂, and lowers pH.Follow approved timing and transport policy; recollect when delay invalidates the sample.
Venous admixture or venous samplePaO₂ is generally lower, with possible higher PaCO₂ and lower pH than arterial blood.Verify source and collection characteristics before oxygenation decisions.
Excess liquid heparin or underfilled syringeDilution may lower gases and electrolytes and alter calculated bicarbonate.Use approved balanced-heparin device and correct fill volume.
Line contaminationFlush solution, medication, or venous blood may distort results.Follow discard-volume and line-draw policy; recollect if integrity is uncertain.
Clot or inadequate mixingMay obstruct the analyzer and produce unreliable values.Do not force a clotted sample through the analyzer.
Temperature or transport mismatchMeasured-at-37°C and temperature-corrected values answer different clinical questions.Know which values are reported and follow policy for the clinical context.

Recognizing Results That Do Not Make Physiologic Sense

Before interpreting or treating an ABG, ask whether pH, PaCO₂, and HCO₃⁻ can logically coexist. A suspicious result may reflect analyzer, cartridge, calibration, transcription, patient-identification, or specimen problems. Do not force an acid-base label onto values that cannot be trusted.

Simple consistency check: If pH is severely acidemic, at least PaCO₂ should be high, HCO₃⁻ should be low, or both. If neither can explain the pH, verify the result.

Valid Pattern or Verify the Result?

pH 7.12, PaCO₂ 43, HCO₃⁻ 27; patient unchanged.
pH 7.28, PaCO₂ 58, HCO₃⁻ 24; patient is hypoventilating after opioids.
pH 7.38, PaCO₂ 55, HCO₃⁻ 32; stable chronic CO₂ retainer.

The ABG Interpretation Sequence

Use the same sequence every time. The goal is not speed at first; the goal is a reproducible method.

1
Verify context: patient, sample source, time, oxygen device/FiO₂, recent intervention, and baseline.
2
Read pH: acidemia, alkalemia, or normal range.
3
Find the primary value: CO₂ goes opposite pH; HCO₃⁻ goes with pH.
4
Classify compensation: uncompensated, partial, or full. If pH is normal, use 7.40 to identify the original side.
5
Check whether compensation is appropriate: use the expected acute/chronic respiratory response or the metabolic compensation formula that matches the primary disorder.
6
Assess oxygenation separately: PaO₂/SaO₂ in relation to FiO₂, device, age, perfusion, and hemoglobin.
7
Return to the patient: compare with baseline, work of breathing, mental status, monitoring, and trend; then choose the next safe action.

Serial ABG Trending — Reading the Direction of Change

A single ABG is a snapshot. Serial ABGs taken over time reveal whether the patient is improving, deteriorating, or not responding to treatment. The direction and magnitude of change is often more clinically actionable than any single value.

Electrolyte Shifts During Acid-Base Changes

Acid-base disorders cause predictable electrolyte redistribution — particularly potassium. These shifts affect treatment decisions and can create secondary complications that require separate management. The RT must anticipate these changes when interpreting combined ABG and electrolyte data.

Acidosis → Hyperkalemia

In acidemia, excess H⁺ ions move into cells to be buffered. To maintain electrical balance, K⁺ moves out of cells into the bloodstream.

Rule of thumb: For every 0.1 drop in pH → potassium rises ~0.6 mEq/L.

Clinical risk: ECG changes (tall peaked T waves, widened QRS). Severe hyperkalemia can cause lethal arrhythmias even during treatment of acidosis.

Alkalosis → Hypokalemia

In alkalemia, H⁺ ions move out of cells to help buffer the alkalosis. To maintain electrical balance, K⁺ moves into cells — dropping serum levels.

Rule of thumb: For every 0.1 rise in pH → potassium falls ~0.6 mEq/L.

Clinical risk: Muscle weakness, cardiac dysrhythmias. Correcting alkalosis without addressing hypokalemia can worsen both conditions.

Sodium — Indirect Relationships

Sodium itself does not shift significantly with pH changes, but its relationship to chloride and bicarbonate is central to the anion gap and NAGMA (non-anion gap metabolic acidosis) calculation covered in Foundations 3.3.

Key point: Hypernatremia causes osmotic shifts affecting pH buffering capacity. Always evaluate sodium alongside the ABG in critically ill patients.

Calcium & Ionized Calcium

pH affects calcium binding to albumin. In alkalosis: more calcium binds to albumin → ionized (free) calcium falls → symptomatic hypocalcemia (tetany, Chvostek's sign, carpopedal spasm).

In acidosis: less calcium bound → more free ionized calcium (clinically beneficial effect during acidosis).

Key point: Hyperventilating patients can develop tetany from acute respiratory alkalosis reducing ionized calcium. Correct the alkalosis — not just the calcium.

Point-of-Care ABG Technology

Point-of-care (POC) blood gas analyzers bring ABG testing to the bedside, the emergency department, and the operating room — dramatically reducing the time from sample collection to result. The respiratory therapist must understand each device's capabilities, limitations, and proper operation.

iStat (Abbott)

Handheld, cartridge-based POC analyzer. CLIA-waived for many cartridge types. Highly portable — used at bedside, transport, OR, and emergency settings.

Cartridges measure: pH, PaCO₂, PaO₂, HCO₃⁻, Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, ionized Ca²⁺, glucose, lactate, creatinine, hematocrit, troponin (cartridge-dependent).

Sample: 65–95 µL whole blood (arterial or venous). Cartridge must be at room temperature before use.

QC: Electronic simulators check device function. Liquid QC run per facility protocol. Cartridges have temperature and expiration limits.

RT role: Collect arterial sample, fill cartridge immediately (avoid air), insert within 5 minutes, document FiO₂ and patient temp.
ABL90 FLEX (Radiometer)

High-throughput floor-standing or benchtop analyzer. Common in ICUs, respiratory therapy departments, and labs requiring rapid high-volume testing.

Measures: Full blood gas panel + electrolytes + metabolites + co-oximetry (oxyHb, COHb, MetHb, tHb) + lactate + glucose + bilirubin.

Co-oximetry advantage: Detects carbon monoxide poisoning (COHb) and methemoglobinemia (MetHb) — conditions where SpO₂ gives false reassurance.

QC: Automated QC runs every 4 hours with manufacturer-supplied ampoules. Auto-calibration with reference gases.

RT role: Load sample via syringe or capillary, enter patient data (FiO₂, temp, mode), interpret printed co-oximetry report alongside standard ABG.
GEM Premier (IL)

Instrumentation Laboratory GEM series — self-contained, touch-screen interface with integrated QC system. Common in larger hospital labs and critical care areas.

Measures: pH, PaCO₂, PaO₂, electrolytes, glucose, lactate, hematocrit, hemoglobin, co-oximetry (full panel).

iQM (Intelligent Quality Management): Continuous, automated internal QC — flags results that fall outside quality limits without requiring manual QC intervention for every sample.

QC: iQM runs automatically. External liquid QC verification required per facility CLIA requirements.

RT role: Enter patient FiO₂ and temp before analysis. Review iQM flags on the result report. Never report a result flagged as QC failure.
Critical Point Across All Devices: Always enter the correct FiO₂ and patient temperature into the analyzer before or with each sample. Without these, the analyzer cannot calculate derived parameters correctly (A-a gradient, P/F ratio, temperature-corrected values). A result without accurate FiO₂ is incomplete.

Pulse Oximetry — Reliability and Limitations

Pulse oximetry (SpO₂) is the most widely used monitoring tool in respiratory care. It is non-invasive, continuous, and easy to use. But it has specific and serious limitations that every RT must know — because when SpO₂ lies, it is dangerous.

LimitationMechanismClinical RiskSolution
Carbon monoxide poisoningCOHb absorbs light at the same wavelength as OxyHb — standard pulse ox cannot differentiate themSpO₂ reads normal (95–100%) while patient is severely hypoxicCo-oximetry (ABL90 / GEM) — directly measures COHb%
MethemoglobinemiaMetHb absorbs at both wavelengths — drives SpO₂ reading toward 85% regardless of true valueTrue hypoxemia may be masked or true oxygenation may appear worse than it isCo-oximetry measures MetHb% directly
Dark skin pigmentationMelanin absorbs some of the spectrophotometric signalSpO₂ systematically overestimates true SaO₂ in patients with darker skin — by an average of 1–2% but up to 4–5% in severe casesArterial ABG co-oximetry when accuracy is critical

Nail polish / artificial nailsPigments (especially dark blue, black, green) absorb spectrophotometric signalFalsely low SpO₂ or loss of signalRemove polish or rotate probe 90° to read from side of finger
Motion artifactMovement creates false pulsatile signal or signal disruptionRandom inaccurate readings — may show sudden drops or spikesSecure probe; use forehead or ear probe; verify clinically

Capnography — End-Tidal CO₂ as an Assessment Tool

Capnography measures the CO₂ concentration in exhaled gas — providing real-time, continuous, non-invasive ventilation monitoring. End-tidal CO₂ (ETCO₂) is the peak CO₂ value at the end of expiration. It is a critical assessment tool because it reflects both ventilation and perfusion — and its waveform shape carries diagnostic information.

Normal ETCO₂ and Its Clinical Uses

Normal ETCO₂: 35–45 mmHg
Correlates with PaCO₂ within ~2–5 mmHg in patients with normal lung function. Rising ETCO₂ = hypoventilation. Falling ETCO₂ = hyperventilation, PE, or decreased cardiac output.
ETCO₂ vs. PaCO₂ Gradient
The ETCO₂-PaCO₂ gradient (normally 2–5 mmHg) widens with V/Q mismatch, PE, or decreased perfusion — areas of the lung ventilating but not perfusing contribute CO₂-free gas that dilutes ETCO₂.

Clinical Applications of Capnography

Clinical UseWhat to Look For
Endotracheal tube confirmationSustained ETCO₂ waveform across 6 breaths = tube in trachea. No waveform = esophageal intubation. Most reliable confirmation method available at bedside.
CPR quality monitoringETCO₂ <10 mmHg during CPR = poor chest compressions or no perfusion. Sudden rise in ETCO₂ during CPR = return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC).
Ventilation monitoring (sedated patients)Continuous ETCO₂ detects apnea, hypoventilation, and hyperventilation without repeated ABG draws.
Bronchospasm detection"Shark fin" waveform — loss of alveolar plateau with a rounded upslope and slow return to baseline. Indicates uneven gas emptying from obstructed airways.
Procedural sedation monitoringETCO₂ detects hypoventilation before SpO₂ drops — provides an earlier warning window for intervention.

Special Populations — Adjusted ABG Interpretation

Generic adult ranges are a starting point, not a substitute for the patient's documented baseline, altitude, oxygen setting, temperature, symptoms, and trend. Population-specific expectations can coexist with an acute superimposed disorder; “expected” does not automatically mean benign.

Pregnancy

Progesterone directly stimulates the respiratory center → chronic mild hyperventilation throughout pregnancy.

Expected pregnant baseline:
PaCO₂: 27–32 mmHg (normal for them — not respiratory alkalosis)
HCO₃⁻: 18–21 mEq/L (renal compensation for the chronic low PaCO₂)
pH: 7.40–7.45 (fully compensated)
PaO₂: slightly elevated (increased ventilation)
Key error: A pregnant patient with PaCO₂ 32 and HCO₃⁻ 20 appears to have "partially compensated respiratory alkalosis" by standard interpretation — but this is their normal baseline. Treat only if acutely worse than their baseline.

Chronic Lung Disease (COPD)

Chronic CO₂ retention with renal compensation creates a new "normal" for each COPD patient — different from population norms.

Example COPD baseline (patient-specific):
PaCO₂: 55–65 mmHg (their normal — fully compensated)
HCO₃⁻: 34–40 mEq/L (renal compensation)
pH: 7.35–7.42 (normalized)
PaO₂: 55–70 mmHg (their acceptable baseline on home O₂)
Key rule: Always know the patient's baseline ABG before treating. A COPD patient with PaCO₂ 65 and pH 7.38 may be at their compensated baseline — not in acute respiratory failure. Compare current values to prior ABGs.

Elderly Patients

Age-related lung changes (reduced elastic recoil, increased closing volume) cause a predictable decline in PaO₂ with age — even with healthy lungs.

Age-adjusted PaO₂ formula:
Expected PaO₂ ≈ 100 − (0.33 × age)
Example: 75-year-old → 100 − (0.33 × 75) = 100 − 24.75 = ~75 mmHg
This is their "normal" — not mild hypoxemia requiring intervention.
Key error: Treating a single PaO₂ cutoff without considering age, altitude, FiO₂, symptoms, perfusion, and trend. Age-estimation formulas are teaching approximations, not stand-alone treatment rules.

Endurance Athletes

High VO₂max, increased metabolic efficiency, and enhanced respiratory muscle strength create subtly different ABG baselines in elite athletes.

Typical athlete baseline (at rest):
PaCO₂: 36–40 mmHg (lower end of normal — efficient CO₂ clearance)
PaO₂: 95–105 mmHg (sometimes slightly above standard normal)
pH: 7.40–7.45
HCO₃⁻: 23–26 mEq/L (normal)
Note: Exercise-induced arterial hypoxemia (EIAHs) can occur in elite athletes at maximal exertion — PaO₂ may drop to 70–85 mmHg despite normal resting values. Not pathological during performance, but documented on exercise ABG testing.

32-week pregnant patient: pH 7.42, PaCO₂ 30, HCO₃⁻ 19. No shortness of breath or distress.
78-year-old healthy non-smoker post-cardiac cath: pH 7.40, PaCO₂ 40, PaO₂ 74 on room air. No symptoms.

ABG Questions Students Commonly Ask

Why can pH be normal when PaCO₂ is still high?

In chronic respiratory acidosis, the kidneys retain bicarbonate over time. The elevated HCO₃⁻ pushes pH upward even though PaCO₂ remains elevated. That is why a normal pH does not automatically mean a normal ABG.

How do I know whether a normal pH is on the acid or alkaline side?

Use 7.40 as the midpoint. A normal pH of 7.35–7.39 is on the acid side; 7.41–7.45 is on the alkaline side. This helps identify the primary process in a fully compensated simple disorder.

Does an ABG tell me the patient's diagnosis?

No. It identifies acid-base and oxygenation patterns. The cause comes from the history, examination, medications, laboratory data, and clinical trend.

Summary — Build the Pattern, Then Use It Clinically

Analytical Errors:
pH moving opposite to primary parameter = reject and resample. Henderson-Hasselbalch must hold. Severe acidemia cannot coexist with normal PaCO₂ AND normal/elevated HCO₃⁻.
Technology:
iStat = portable, cartridge-based, limited co-ox. ABL90 = full co-ox + electrolytes + lactate. GEM = iQM continuous QC. Always enter FiO₂ and patient temperature. SpO₂ fails with CO, MetHb, dark skin, poor perfusion.
Special Populations:
Pregnancy and chronic hypercapnia require baseline-aware interpretation. Expected population values may still have a superimposed acute abnormality.
Preanalytic Validation:
Verify patient, source, time, oxygen/support conditions, air, delay, heparin, clot, line contamination, transport, and temperature before trusting the result.
Whole-Patient Trend:
Use pH, PaCO₂, SpO₂, ETCO₂, RR, work of breathing, mental status, hemodynamics, support settings, and tolerance to judge response.
Electrolytes + Acid-Base: Acidosis → K⁺ shifts out of cells → hyperkalemia. Alkalosis → K⁺ shifts in → hypokalemia. Alkalosis → ionized calcium falls → tetany risk. Every 0.1 pH change → ~0.6 mEq/L potassium change in the opposite direction.
