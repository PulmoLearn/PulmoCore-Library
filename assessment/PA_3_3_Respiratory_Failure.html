2-Minute Overview: Respiratory Failure — From ABG to Action

Types of Respiratory Failure — The Fundamental Distinction

Respiratory failure occurs when the respiratory system can no longer maintain adequate gas exchange — either getting enough oxygen into the blood, removing enough CO₂, or both. The ABG immediately reveals which type is present and guides every subsequent clinical decision.

Type 1 — Hypoxemic Respiratory Failure

Definition: Failure of GAS EXCHANGE — oxygenation problem

PaO₂ <60 mmHg ← PRIMARY PROBLEM
PaCO₂ Normal or LOW (≤45)
pH Normal or HIGH (hyperventilating to compensate)
HCO₃⁻ Normal

Key feature: Patient CAN ventilate (CO₂ normal or low) but CANNOT oxygenate. Lungs are ventilating but gas exchange is failing.

Examples: Pneumonia, pulmonary edema, ARDS, atelectasis, intrapulmonary shunting

Type 2 — Hypercapnic Respiratory Failure

Definition: Failure of VENTILATION — CO₂ removal problem

PaCO₂ >50–60 mmHg ← PRIMARY PROBLEM
pH <7.30 (respiratory acidosis)
PaO₂ Low or normal
HCO₃⁻ May elevate (metabolic compensation)

Key feature: CO₂ is accumulating. Patient cannot move air effectively — alveolar ventilation is impaired. Most common reason for mechanical ventilation.

Examples: Hypoventilation, respiratory muscle weakness, COPD, airway obstruction

Mixed Respiratory Failure

Definition: BOTH oxygenation AND ventilation are failing simultaneously

PaO₂ <60 mmHg ← hypoxemia
PaCO₂ >50–60 mmHg ← hypercapnia
pH <7.30 (severe respiratory acidosis)
HCO₃⁻ Variable

Key feature: Most severe form. Multiple simultaneous respiratory failures. High mortality risk. Requires aggressive intervention.

Examples: Advanced ARDS, end-stage COPD with pneumonia, severe aspiration

Classify Each ABG as Type 1, Type 2, or Mixed Respiratory Failure

pH 7.29, PaCO₂ 68, PaO₂ 55, HCO₃⁻ 24. Patient using accessory muscles, lethargic.
pH 7.22, PaCO₂ 75, PaO₂ 48, HCO₃⁻ 24. Patient obtunded, barely responding.
pH 7.42, PaCO₂ 38, PaO₂ 62, HCO₃⁻ 25. Patient on 5L nasal cannula. SpO₂ 91%.
pH 7.37, PaCO₂ 60, PaO₂ 60, HCO₃⁻ 37. COPD patient at baseline — fully compensated.

Causes of Respiratory Failure — Four Mechanisms

Respiratory failure arises from four distinct pathological mechanisms. Identifying the correct mechanism determines the treatment — and several of these look similar clinically but require fundamentally different interventions.

1 — Impaired Gas Exchange

Problem at the alveolar-capillary membrane. Fluid, pus, or inflammatory debris fills the alveolus — oxygen cannot cross from air to blood despite adequate ventilation.

ABG: Low PaO₂. Normal or low PaCO₂ (patient hyperventilates). → Type 1 RF

CXR: Infiltrates (white opacities in lung fields)

Causes: Pneumonia, pulmonary edema (cardiogenic/ARDS), aspiration, inhalation injury, sepsis

2 — V/Q Mismatch

Imbalance between ventilation (V) and perfusion (Q) within the lung. Two patterns: SHUNT (blood perfuses non-ventilated alveoli → hypoxemia) and DEAD SPACE (ventilated alveoli not perfused → wasted ventilation).

ABG: Low PaO₂, normal CO₂ (CO₂ elimination less affected than O₂ uptake). → Type 1 RF

Causes: PE (dead space), pneumonia, atelectasis, shock, sepsis

3 — Hypoventilation

Inadequate movement of air in and out of the lungs. The problem is the "bellows" — the respiratory muscles and drive cannot generate sufficient airflow. CO₂ accumulates.

ABG: High PaCO₂, low pH, low PaO₂. → Type 2 RF

MIP (Maximum Inspiratory Pressure): Normal = −80 to −120 cmH₂O. If MIP >−20 cmH₂O (e.g., −15) → severe respiratory muscle weakness → ventilatory support needed

Causes: Opioids, sedatives, CNS depression, spinal cord injury, myasthenia gravis, kyphoscoliosis, obesity hypoventilation

4 — Airway Obstruction

Physical or functional obstruction resists airflow through the airways. Air traps in alveoli — expiration is prolonged and incomplete. CO₂ cannot be cleared efficiently.

ABG: Eventually high PaCO₂ and low pH as obstruction worsens. → Type 2 or Mixed RF

Capnography: Shark fin waveform — baseline doesn't return to zero. Sloped expiration. Elevated ETCO₂.

Causes: COPD, asthma, foreign body, thick secretions, bronchospasm, tumor compression

V/Q Mismatch in Detail — Shunt vs. Dead Space

V/Q mismatch is the most common cause of hypoxemia in clinical practice. Understanding the direction of the mismatch explains which treatment helps and why.

Shunt — V/Q = 0 (No Ventilation)

Blood PERFUSES a non-ventilated alveolus. The alveolus is collapsed, flooded with fluid, or blocked — no gas exchange occurs. Blood passes through the lungs without gaining oxygen.

Hallmark: Does NOT respond well to supplemental oxygen alone — the problem is the alveolus isn't open, not the inspired oxygen.

Causes: Atelectasis (collapsed alveolus), pneumonia (flooded alveolus), ARDS

Treatment: Positive pressure (PEEP/CPAP) to recruit the collapsed alveolus, not just more O₂.

Dead Space — V/Q = ∞ (No Perfusion)

A ventilated alveolus receives NO blood flow. Air reaches the alveolus but there is no capillary blood to exchange gas with. The ventilation is wasted — "physiologic dead space."

Hallmark: ETCO₂ falls (normal ETCO₂ requires perfusion to deliver CO₂ to the alveolus). Wide arterial-ETCO₂ gradient.

Causes: Pulmonary embolism (clot blocks pulmonary artery), shock (no cardiac output), severe pulmonary hypertension

Treatment: Treat the perfusion problem (anticoagulation for PE, restore cardiac output for shock).

Shunt or Dead Space?

A patient's PaO₂ is 52 mmHg. Increasing FiO₂ from 0.40 to 0.80 does NOT improve PaO₂ significantly. The CXR shows bilateral consolidation. What type of V/Q mismatch is this, and why doesn't extra oxygen help?

Hypoventilation — Causes and the MIP Measurement

Hypoventilation is the failure of the "bellows" — the respiratory muscles and neurological drive that move air in and out. CO₂ accumulates, pH falls, and Type 2 respiratory failure develops. The Maximum Inspiratory Pressure (MIP) measures how well the respiratory muscles can generate force.

Neurological Depression
  • Opioid overdose
  • Sedatives (benzodiazepines)
  • CNS depressants (alcohol)
  • Stroke, TBI, increased ICP
  • Sleep apnea (obstructive or central)
Neuromuscular Problems
  • Spinal cord injury (C3-C5 especially)
  • Myasthenia gravis
  • Guillain-Barré syndrome
  • Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)
  • Respiratory muscle fatigue
Musculoskeletal Problems
  • Flail chest (multiple rib fractures)
  • Severe obesity (restrictive)
  • Kyphoscoliosis (spinal deformity)
  • Chest wall injury
  • Post-operative splinting from pain

MIP — Maximum Inspiratory Pressure

What MIP Measures

MIP measures the maximum negative pressure (suction force) the patient can generate during a forced maximal inhalation against a closed airway. It directly assesses respiratory muscle strength — the ability to "suck" air into the lungs.

Normal MIP: −80 to −120 cmH₂O (large negative pressure). More negative = stronger.

Interpreting MIP Values
  • −80 to −120 cmH₂O → Normal strength
  • −20 to −30 cmH₂O → Weak — monitor closely
  • >−20 cmH₂O (e.g., −15) → Severe weakness — ventilatory support needed
Scenario: Patient with Guillain-Barré has MIP of −12 cmH₂O. This is far weaker than the −20 threshold → intubation and mechanical ventilation indicated before ABG worsens.

Interpreting MIP in Context

A patient with myasthenia gravis presents with increasing dyspnea. Serial MIP measurements: 6 AM = −38 cmH₂O. 10 AM = −22 cmH₂O. 2 PM = −14 cmH₂O. ABG at 2 PM: pH 7.34, PaCO₂ 52. What is the clinical significance and what should happen?

ABG Patterns for Respiratory Failure — Applying the Data

ABG is the most pertinent diagnostic test for a dyspneic patient. It reveals the type of failure, the severity, and drives the intervention decision. These worked ABG examples represent the major clinical presentations you will encounter.

Pattern 1 — Type 1 (Hypoxemic RF): Supplemental Oxygen Indicated

pH 7.48 | PaCO₂ 32 | PaO₂ 55 | HCO₃⁻ 24
Interpretation: Patient hyperventilating (↓CO₂) trying to compensate for low O₂ — alkalotic. GAS EXCHANGE problem. → Supplemental Oxygen

Pattern 2 — Type 2 Mild (BiPAP Candidate): pH 7.25–7.35

pH 7.29 | PaCO₂ 68 | PaO₂ 55 | HCO₃⁻ 24
Interpretation: Respiratory acidosis. CO₂ elevated. pH in 7.25–7.35 range = MILD-MODERATE ventilatory failure. → BiPAP 15/5 Trial

Pattern 3 — Type 2 Severe (Intubation): pH <7.25

pH 7.22 | PaCO₂ 75 | PaO₂ 55 | HCO₃⁻ 24
Interpretation: Severe respiratory acidosis. pH <7.25 = SEVERE. → INTUBATE — Mechanical Ventilation

Pattern 4 — Compensated Chronic COPD (Patient Baseline)

pH 7.37 | PaCO₂ 60 | PaO₂ 60 | HCO₃⁻ 37
Interpretation: Fully compensated. High HCO₃⁻ normalized pH. PaCO₂ 60 is THIS patient's baseline — do NOT treat as acute failure without knowing prior ABGs.

Pattern 5 — Acute Deterioration of COPD Patient (pH Math)

COPD baseline: pH 7.37, PaCO₂ 50. Now PaCO₂ suddenly 65 (+15 mmHg acute rise)
pH change = 15 ÷ 10 × 0.08 = −0.12. New pH = 7.37 − 0.12 = 7.25
→ Even from a "normal" COPD baseline, acute CO₂ rise rapidly creates severe acidosis. Intervene early.
The pH-CO₂ Relationship Rule: For every 10 mmHg acute rise in PaCO₂ above normal, the pH falls by approximately 0.08. This formula helps predict how quickly a patient is deteriorating and when the BiPAP → intubation threshold will be crossed.

pH 7.28, PaCO₂ 62, PaO₂ 52. COPD patient, alert, following commands, able to protect airway.
pH 7.20, PaCO₂ 80, PaO₂ 48. Patient obtunded, not following commands, respiratory rate 6.

Clinical Signs of Respiratory Failure — What to Look For

The physical exam reveals the severity and trajectory of respiratory failure before the ABG returns. Recognizing early signs allows intervention before the patient deteriorates to a level requiring emergency intubation.

Early Warning Signs

  • Tachypnea (RR >20/min): First compensatory response
  • Tachycardia: Sympathetic activation from hypoxia
  • Restlessness and anxiety: Brain sensing inadequate O₂
  • Diaphoresis: Sympathetic activation, increased work of breathing
  • Accessory muscle use: Scalene, sternocleidomastoid, intercostals
  • Patient speaks in sentences but struggles

Late / Ominous Signs

  • Cyanosis: Late sign — already severely hypoxemic
  • Altered mental status / confusion: Brain hypoxia
  • Cannot speak full sentences: Severe WOB
  • Tripod positioning: Patient leaning forward on arms
  • Bradycardia (late): Myocardial hypoxia — pre-arrest
  • Obtunded / unresponsive: Requires immediate airway control
Most Pertinent Test for the Dyspneic Patient: Always answer ABG (Arterial Blood Gas). ECG rules out cardiac cause but doesn't guide respiratory intervention. Sputum culture takes days. CBC and electrolytes are useful but not primary diagnostics. Only the ABG provides immediate, exact acid-base status and type of respiratory failure.

Capnography Waveforms — Reading the Pattern to Identify the Mechanism

Capnography provides real-time, continuous information about CO₂ elimination — and the SHAPE of the waveform tells you the MECHANISM of failure. Three distinct patterns correspond to three different respiratory problems.

Normal / V/Q Mismatch

ETCO₂: 35–45 mmHg (normal)

Baseline: Returns to zero between breaths

Key insight: Normal ETCO₂ + low PaO₂ = V/Q mismatch. Ventilation works (CO₂ cleared) but oxygenation fails (gas exchange problem). Do NOT increase ventilation rate.

Airway Obstruction — Shark Fin

ETCO₂: May be elevated (CO₂ trapped)

Baseline: Does NOT return to zero — residual CO₂ remains

Key insight: Prolonged sloped expiration ("shark fin"). Patient struggling to exhale. Air trapping = COPD, asthma, bronchoconstriction. → Bronchodilators, airway clearance.

Hypoventilation

ETCO₂: Elevated and rising (CO₂ accumulating)

Pattern: Normal shape but fewer breaths (slow rate). Rising ETCO₂ over time.

Key insight: Respiratory depression (opioids, sedation, neuromuscular disease). CO₂ rising because patient not breathing often enough. → Increase ventilation, reverse sedation, or support ventilation.

Intervention Hierarchy — ABG-Driven Decision Making

Treatment for respiratory failure progresses through three escalating levels. The ABG drives every decision. The goal at each level is to correct the gas exchange abnormality and prevent escalation to the next level if possible.

1
Lowest
intensity
Supplemental Oxygen Only

Indications: Normal pH (7.35–7.45) + Normal PaCO₂ (35–45) + Low PaO₂. No ventilation problem — ONLY an oxygenation problem (Type 1 RF).

Devices: NC (1–6L, 24–44%) → Simple mask (5–8L, 40–60%) → Non-rebreather (10–15L, 60–95%) → HHN (30–100%)

Escalation trigger: If patient requires >44% FiO₂ to maintain SpO₂ ≥90% → move to Level 2 (non-invasive ventilation).
2
Moderate
intensity
Non-Invasive Ventilation (BiPAP or CPAP)

Indications: Mild-moderate respiratory acidosis — pH 7.25–7.35 AND/OR PaCO₂ 50–60 (or >15 above baseline for chronic CO₂ retainer). Patient MUST be alert, cooperative, and able to protect the airway.

BiPAP (bilevel): For CO₂ retention and hypercapnic failure — delivers higher pressure on inspiration (assists breathing) and lower on expiration. Typical: 15/5 cmH₂O, 30–50% FiO₂.

CPAP (continuous): For hypoxemia WITHOUT CO₂ retention — single constant pressure recruits collapsed alveoli throughout the breathing cycle.

Escalation trigger: pH falls below 7.25 during BiPAP trial, patient cannot cooperate, apnea, or aspiration risk develops → move to Level 3.
3
Highest
intensity
Mechanical Ventilation (Intubation)

Indications: Any ONE of: pH <7.25, PaCO₂ >60 and rapidly rising, cannot protect airway (no gag reflex, cough), altered mental status/unresponsive, apnea, failed BiPAP trial, respiratory muscle fatigue/exhaustion.

Goal: Take over breathing completely. Control airway definitively. Correct severe acid-base disturbance. Buy time for underlying cause to improve.

Key principle: We delay intubation as long as it is safe to do so — not because intubation is bad, but because mechanical ventilation carries serious risks (barotrauma, VAP, sedation effects). Non-invasive support is preferred when feasible.

BiPAP vs. CPAP — Selecting the Right Non-Invasive Device

Both BiPAP and CPAP deliver positive pressure without intubation, but they work differently and are indicated for different presentations. The ABG — specifically the PaCO₂ and pH — determines which device to choose.

BiPAP — Bilevel Positive Airway Pressure

Delivers TWO pressure levels: higher pressure (IPAP) during inspiration to assist the breath, and lower pressure (EPAP) during expiration to maintain airway patency. Effectively assists ventilation — reduces the patient's work of breathing and improves CO₂ elimination.

Indicate when: pH 7.25–7.35 AND PaCO₂ >50–60 mmHg. The patient has a CO₂ problem — BiPAP assists each breath to help blow off CO₂.

Classic use: COPD exacerbation, hypercapnic heart failure, obesity hypoventilation, neuromuscular disease (if patient cooperative).

Typical settings: IPAP 15 / EPAP 5 cmH₂O with 30–50% FiO₂.
BiPAP Example: pH 7.29 | PaCO₂ 68 | PaO₂ 55 | HCO₃⁻ 24

CPAP — Continuous Positive Airway Pressure

Delivers ONE constant pressure level throughout the entire breathing cycle — both inspiration and expiration. Splints the airways and alveoli open, increasing functional residual capacity and recruiting collapsed alveoli. Does NOT assist ventilation — the patient must breathe spontaneously at normal rate and depth.

Indicate when: pH and PaCO₂ are NORMAL but PaO₂ <60 mmHg. The patient is oxygenating poorly from V/Q mismatch or atelectasis but is ventilating adequately.

Classic use: Hypoxemic respiratory failure without CO₂ retention (pneumonia, CHF, atelectasis, sleep apnea).

Key limitation: Cannot help if patient has a CO₂ problem — CPAP maintains pressure but does not assist individual breaths. Use BiPAP if CO₂ is also elevated.
CPAP Example: pH 7.38 | PaCO₂ 43 | PaO₂ 58 | HCO₃⁻ 24

Mechanical Ventilation Basics — What the Machine Is Actually Doing

Mechanical ventilation is the highest level of respiratory support in this intervention hierarchy. Before learning specific ventilator modes, settings, and waveforms in later courses, students need one clear mental model: a ventilator supports or replaces the patient’s ability to move air, exchange gases, and protect the airway when less invasive support is no longer safe or effective.

Beginner Mental Model: A ventilator is not “just oxygen.” Oxygen therapy changes how much oxygen the patient breathes in. Mechanical ventilation changes the mechanics of breathing by pushing gas into the lungs with positive pressure and controlling or assisting parts of the breath.

Normal Spontaneous Breathing

The diaphragm contracts and creates negative pressure inside the chest. Air flows inward because the pressure inside the lungs becomes lower than atmospheric pressure.

FeatureNormal Breathing
Pressure patternNegative pressure pulls air in.
Main driverDiaphragm and chest wall movement.
ControlPatient controls rate, depth, and timing.
ExhalationUsually passive recoil.

Positive-Pressure Ventilation

A ventilator creates positive pressure at the airway opening. Gas flows into the lungs because the machine pushes gas down the pressure gradient.

FeatureMechanical Ventilation
Pressure patternPositive pressure pushes air in.
Main driverVentilator-generated pressure or flow.
ControlMachine may assist, partially support, or fully control breaths.
ExhalationUsually passive recoil through the ventilator circuit.

Why a Patient May Need Mechanical Ventilation

Oxygenation failure
PaO₂ remains low despite oxygen and non-invasive support. Positive pressure and PEEP may be needed to recruit alveoli.
Ventilation failure
PaCO₂ rises and pH falls because the patient cannot move enough air to clear CO₂.
Airway protection failure
Obtundation, absent gag/cough, vomiting risk, or inability to protect the airway makes BiPAP unsafe.
Muscle fatigue
The patient is working so hard to breathe that respiratory muscles are failing or MIP is dangerously weak.

The Basic Breath Cycle

A ventilator breath can be simplified into three beginner steps. Later, students will learn trigger, limit, and cycle variables in more depth. For now, focus on what starts the breath, what the machine delivers, and how exhalation occurs.

1

Trigger

The breath starts. The patient may trigger it by making an inspiratory effort, or the ventilator may trigger it based on the set respiratory rate.

2

Breath Delivery

The ventilator delivers gas using a set pressure or volume target. This is where tidal volume, inspiratory pressure, flow, and FiO₂ matter.

3

Exhalation

The machine stops pushing gas in and the patient exhales passively. PEEP remains at the end of exhalation to keep alveoli from collapsing.

Four Core “Knobs” Students Should Recognize First

FiO₂

How much oxygen is in the gas delivered to the patient. Mainly supports oxygenation.

Tidal Volume

How much gas is delivered with each breath in volume-targeted ventilation. Too much volume can cause volutrauma.

Respiratory Rate

How many breaths the ventilator delivers or guarantees per minute. Rate affects minute ventilation and CO₂ removal.

PEEP

Positive pressure left at end-exhalation. Helps keep alveoli open, improves oxygenation, and reduces collapse.

PEEP in plain language: PEEP is like keeping a tiny amount of air in a balloon so it does not completely collapse between breaths. It can improve oxygenation by keeping alveoli open, but too much pressure can also overdistend lung tissue or reduce venous return.

RT Safety Mindset: Always Assess the Patient First

High-pressure alarm
Think obstruction or resistance: secretions, kinked tubing, biting the tube, bronchospasm, reduced compliance, or pneumothorax. Assess the patient and the circuit.
Low-pressure / low-volume alarm
Think leak or disconnection: circuit disconnected, cuff leak, loose connection, or extubation. Assess the patient immediately.
Ventilator numbers are not enough
Look at chest rise, breath sounds, SpO₂, ETCO₂, mental status, work of breathing, and hemodynamics.
Manual ventilation backup
A bag-valve-mask must be immediately available for ventilated patients. If the machine fails, the RT must be ready to manually ventilate.

Ventilator Hazards — Why We Delay Intubation When Safe

Mechanical ventilation is life-saving when necessary — but it carries significant risks that accumulate with time. The RT must know these hazards to monitor for them, prevent them, and communicate their importance when advocating for non-invasive alternatives.

1 — Upper Airway Injury During Intubation

Trauma from ET tube insertion can damage teeth, lips, vocal cords, trachea, and esophagus. Risk includes: dental damage, vocal cord paralysis (hoarseness, aphonia), tracheal stenosis, laryngeal edema, and esophageal intubation (immediately life-threatening if not recognized).

2 — Long-Term Neurological Effects from Sedation

Prolonged use of propofol, benzodiazepines, and opioids to maintain ventilator tolerance causes: delayed awakening, respiratory muscle weakness (difficulty weaning), cognitive impairment, delirium (ICU delirium), and dependency risk. Longer intubation = greater cumulative sedation burden.

3 — VAP — Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia

Bacteria aspirates around the ET tube cuff into the lower airways. Risk factors: poor oral hygiene, supine positioning, ET tube biofilm, cuff pressure too low. Prevention: oral CHG care, head-of-bed elevation, cuff pressure monitoring (20–30 cmH₂O), subglottic suctioning.

4 — Barotrauma (Pressure Injury)

Excessive airway pressure ruptures alveolar walls → pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, pulmonary interstitial emphysema. Prevention: limit peak inspiratory pressure. Use lung-protective ventilation strategy. Monitor plateau pressure ≤30 cmH₂O.

5 — Volutrauma (Volume/Over-Expansion Injury)

Excessive tidal volumes overstretch alveoli beyond their capacity → alveolar rupture, inflammatory cascade, pulmonary edema, and multi-organ dysfunction. Prevention: lung-protective tidal volumes of 6–8 mL/kg of ideal body weight — especially critical in ARDS patients.

6 — Ventilator Malfunction

Rare but catastrophic. Prevention checklist: (1) Calibrate before every use. (2) Plug into red emergency outlet (backup power during outages). (3) Monitor battery during transport. (4) Verify all alarms are active before connecting. (5) Always have manual bagging capability immediately available.

Oxygen Delivery (DO₂) — The Complete Picture

PaO₂ alone does not determine whether tissues are receiving adequate oxygen. DO₂ integrates three factors: hemoglobin carrying capacity, saturation, and cardiac output. This is why a patient can have a normal PaO₂ and still be tissue-hypoxic (covered in PA 2.2).

DO₂ = Cardiac Output × CaO₂ × 10
CaO₂ = (Hb × SaO₂ × 1.34) + (PaO₂ × 0.003)
Normal DO₂: 600–1000 mL/min/m²
Worked Example — Type 1 RF
Hb = 14 g/dL, SaO₂ = 0.88, PaO₂ = 55, Cardiac Output = 5 L/min
CaO₂ = (14 × 0.88 × 1.34) + (55 × 0.003) = 16.5 + 0.17 = 16.7 mL/100mL
DO₂ = 5 × 16.7 × 10 = 835 mL/min (low-normal)
Despite low PaO₂, cardiac compensation maintains DO₂ — patient not yet in tissue hypoxia.
Worked Example — Anemic + Low O₂
Hb = 7 g/dL, SaO₂ = 0.88, PaO₂ = 55, Cardiac Output = 4 L/min
CaO₂ = (7 × 0.88 × 1.34) + (55 × 0.003) = 8.3 + 0.17 = 8.5 mL/100mL
DO₂ = 4 × 8.5 × 10 = 340 mL/min (critically low)
Same PaO₂ but anemia and reduced cardiac output → critical tissue hypoxia.

Why Can Two Patients Have the Same PaO₂ but Different Oxygen Delivery?

Patient A: Hb 14, PaO₂ 60, SaO₂ 91%, Cardiac output 5 L/min. Patient B: Hb 7, PaO₂ 60, SaO₂ 91%, Cardiac output 3 L/min. Both have identical PaO₂ and SaO₂. Why is Patient B much more likely to have tissue hypoxia?

A 72-Year-Old COPD Patient — Escalating Over 12 Hours

The patient is intubated and on the ventilator. Day 3: tidal volume was set at 600 mL (which is 9 mL/kg for this patient's ideal body weight). New bilateral infiltrates appeared on CXR that were not present on admission. Oxygenation is worsening. The RT recognizes this pattern.

Case Summary — Key Takeaways

Hour 0: Acute-on-chronic Type 2 RF. pH 7.31 (in BiPAP range). Alert and cooperative. → BiPAP 15/5 with 40% FiO₂. Compare always to patient's documented baseline.
Hour 4: BiPAP working — pH improving, CO₂ falling, less distress. → Continue, reassess in 2-4 hours. Never remove non-invasive support prematurely.
Hour 10: pH 7.19, obtunded, BiPAP failed. Three simultaneous intubation criteria met. → Immediate intubation. When pH falls below 7.25 AND patient cannot protect airway — there is no more BiPAP trial.
Day 3: Tidal volumes at 9 mL/kg caused volutrauma and bilateral inflammatory infiltrates. → Reduce to 6 mL/kg immediately. Lung-protective ventilation is not optional in ventilated patients.

FAQ

Respiratory Failure — Common Questions

If a patient is hyperventilating (low PaCO₂), why are they still hypoxemic?

Hyperventilation removes CO₂ very efficiently — CO₂ is small and diffuses easily. But oxygen transfer depends on the INTEGRITY of the alveolar-capillary membrane and the MATCHING of ventilation to perfusion. In Type 1 respiratory failure, the problem is that fluid, pus, or collapse prevents oxygen from crossing from the alveolus into the blood — no matter how fast the patient breathes, the affected alveoli cannot transfer oxygen. CO₂ diffuses 20× more easily than O₂, so CO₂ clears normally (or supranormally from hyperventilation) while O₂ cannot cross. The hyperventilation (low PaCO₂) is actually a compensatory response to the hypoxemia — the patient is breathing faster trying to get more oxygen, but the lung disease is preventing it.

What is the most important single diagnostic test for a patient with dyspnea? Why not a chest X-ray?

The ABG (Arterial Blood Gas) is the most pertinent test. It immediately reveals: (1) The type of respiratory failure (Type 1 vs Type 2 vs Mixed). (2) The severity (how acidotic is the pH?). (3) Which intervention is needed (O₂ vs BiPAP vs intubation). A chest X-ray shows anatomical abnormalities (infiltrates, effusion, pneumothorax) but does not directly tell you the acid-base status or type of failure. An ECG rules out cardiac cause but does not guide respiratory intervention. The ABG is the direct physiological measurement that drives the treatment decision — it is the first test ordered for a dyspneic patient in most clinical settings.

Why does pH matter more than PaCO₂ when deciding on intubation?

pH represents the NET effect of CO₂ on the acid-base balance, accounting for whether the kidneys have compensated. A COPD patient with a baseline PaCO₂ of 55 has kidneys that have compensated over weeks with elevated HCO₃⁻ — their pH is normal at 7.37 despite the high CO₂. If their PaCO₂ acutely rises to 70, the pH drops to 7.25 — that's an acute emergency even though 70 "doesn't sound that much higher." Conversely, a patient who chronically has PaCO₂ of 70 with compensated HCO₃⁻ of 40 might have a normal pH — that's their baseline, not acute failure. The pH integrates the degree of compensation and tells you how the body is handling the CO₂ right now, which is why pH drives the intervention threshold (7.25-7.35 = BiPAP, <7.25 = intubation).

Why can't I just give more oxygen to treat any type of respiratory failure?

Oxygen only helps if the problem is inadequate oxygen delivery from low FiO₂ or a partially functional gas exchange mechanism. In pure shunt (V/Q = 0 — alveolus completely blocked by fluid or collapse), no supplemental oxygen can reach the alveolus — the oxygen never contacts the capillary blood, so increasing FiO₂ has minimal impact on PaO₂. For Type 2 failure (CO₂ retention), the problem is inadequate VENTILATION — even with 100% FiO₂, if the patient cannot blow off CO₂, the pH continues to fall. You also risk suppressing hypoxic respiratory drive in chronic CO₂ retainers (COPD). Treatment must match the mechanism: oxygen for Type 1 without shunt, positive pressure for shunt/atelectasis, ventilatory support for Type 2.

What is the lung-protective tidal volume and why does it prevent volutrauma?

Lung-protective tidal volume = 6 mL/kg of IDEAL body weight (IBW). In ARDS, the disease does not uniformly affect all lung regions — some areas remain recruitible (baby lung) while others are consolidated. If you set a tidal volume appropriate for a normal lung (10-12 mL/kg), it all goes to the few remaining open areas — over-distending those alveoli far beyond their capacity. This mechanical stretch triggers an inflammatory cascade (mechanotransduction), releases cytokines, causes alveolar rupture, and extends the injury to previously unaffected lung. Setting 6 mL/kg keeps the tidal volume proportional to the "baby lung" size, preventing over-distension while still providing adequate minute ventilation (by using higher respiratory rates if needed). The ARDSNet trial showed mortality reduction with this strategy.

Summary — Respiratory Failure Decision Framework

Type 1 (Hypoxemic): PaO₂ <60. Normal/low PaCO₂. Normal or alkalotic pH. Problem = gas exchange. Mechanism: impaired gas exchange or V/Q mismatch. Treatment = O₂ supplementation or CPAP.
Type 2 (Hypercapnic): PaCO₂ >50-60. pH <7.30. Problem = ventilation. Mechanism: hypoventilation or airway obstruction. Treatment = BiPAP (mild-mod) or intubation (severe).
Intervention hierarchy: O₂ (pH normal) → BiPAP (pH 7.25-7.35) → Intubate (pH <7.25 OR cannot protect airway). BiPAP for CO₂ problems. CPAP for O₂ problems without CO₂ rise.
Mechanical ventilation basics: A ventilator uses positive pressure to support or replace breathing. Core beginner settings include FiO₂, tidal volume, respiratory rate, and PEEP. Always assess the patient first when alarms occur.
Ventilator hazards: Upper airway injury, sedation effects, VAP, Barotrauma (excessive pressure → PTX), Volutrauma (excessive volume → bilateral injury — use 6 mL/kg IBW), Malfunction (plug into red outlet, verify battery).
