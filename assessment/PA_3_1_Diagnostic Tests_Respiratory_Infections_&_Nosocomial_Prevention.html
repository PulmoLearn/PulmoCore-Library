Diagnostic Tests, Respiratory Infections & Nosocomial Prevention

Clinical assessment begins with selecting the right diagnostic test, recognizing the signs of infection, and interpreting the results to guide treatment. This lesson covers the eight key diagnostic procedures, signs of infection, antibiotic selection principles, pneumonia classification, and evidence-based strategies to prevent hospital-acquired and ventilator-associated pneumonia.

Sputum Collection — Steps and Standards

A properly collected sputum sample is the foundation of accurate respiratory infection diagnosis. The most common error is collecting saliva instead of actual sputum from the lower airways. Every step of the collection process has a specific clinical reason.

1
Patient Positioning — Semi-Fowler's
Elevate the head of bed 30–45°. Upright positioning uses gravity to help mobilize secretions toward the central airways for expectoration. Lying flat traps secretions in the lower airways.
2
Patient Preparation
Verify patient identification with two identifiers. Instruct the patient on what is needed — sputum, not saliva. Perform hand hygiene. Have the patient rinse their mouth with water (removes surface contamination but does not affect lower airway organisms).
3
Breathing Technique — 3–4 Slow, Deep Breaths
Instruct the patient to take 3–4 slow, deep breaths before coughing. Deep breathing recruits lower airways and helps loosen secretions. Hyperventilation-induced respiratory alkalosis can cause dizziness — instruct slow, controlled breathing.
4
Coughing — Productive, Not Saliva
Instruct the patient to take a deep breath, hold briefly, and cough forcefully from deep in the chest — directing sputum (not saliva) into the sterile container. If patient coughs saliva only, have them attempt again after additional deep breathing. The specimen must come from the lower airways.
5
Volume Collected — 2–10 mL of Sputum
The laboratory requires a minimum of 2 mL of actual sputum for gram stain, culture, and sensitivity testing. Larger volumes (>10 mL) are unnecessary. If the patient cannot produce an adequate sample by expectoration, nasotracheal suctioning may be used to collect the specimen.
6
Labeling and Biohazard Disposal
Immediately cap the sterile container. Label with two patient identifiers, date, time, and collection method. Place in biohazard bag. Complete the laboratory requisition form. Sputum is an infectious specimen — handle with appropriate PPE.
7
Transport — Immediate or Refrigerate
Transport to the laboratory immediately. Organisms in the sample continue to die or multiply at room temperature, altering culture results. If immediate transport is not possible, refrigerate (4°C) — but not for more than 2 hours. Do NOT freeze.

Place the Sputum Collection Steps in the Correct Order

Assign each step a number from 1 to 6. Step 7 (transport) is fixed.

Instruct patient to take 3–4 slow, deep breaths to mobilize lower airway secretions.
Position patient in semi-Fowler's position (head of bed elevated 30–45°).
Confirm 2–10 mL of actual sputum (not saliva) has been collected into the sterile container.
Verify patient identification, perform hand hygiene, and instruct patient to rinse mouth with water.
Label container with two identifiers, date, time, and collection method. Place in biohazard bag.
Have patient take a deep breath, hold briefly, and cough forcefully from deep in the chest into the sterile container.

Thoracentesis — Pleural Fluid Drainage

Thoracentesis is both a diagnostic procedure (to analyze pleural fluid) and a therapeutic procedure (to relieve dyspnea from large effusions). The most common cause of pleural effusion in the RT setting is congestive heart failure. Understanding the procedure helps the RT prepare the patient, assist the physician, and identify complications.

Procedure Steps (Simplified)
  1. Patient seated upright, leaning forward (widens ICS)
  2. Skin anesthetized — between the ribs
  3. Small 2–3 mm incision made
  4. Further anesthetic infiltration into pleural space
  5. Needle and catheter inserted above the rib (avoids neurovascular bundle on inferior rib margin)
  6. Fluid drained — typically 1–1.5 liters
  7. Post-procedure CXR to rule out pneumothorax
Common Causes of Pleural Effusion
  • Congestive Heart Failure — most common; bilateral transudative effusions
  • Kidney disease — low oncotic pressure; protein loss causes fluid accumulation
  • Infection (pneumonia) — parapneumonic effusion; may become empyema
  • Cancer — malignant effusion from pleural metastases; often recurrent
Pleurodesis is indicated when effusion recurs multiple times. A sclerosing agent (talc, doxycycline) is instilled into the pleural space to fuse the pleural layers and prevent re-accumulation.

Laboratory Tests — Blood Chemistry, CBC & Electrolytes

Three categories of blood tests guide clinical decision-making alongside the ABG. The RT must understand what each measures, why it is ordered, and how abnormal values link to respiratory care decisions.

Blood Chemistry Panel

Ordered for heart, liver, or kidney dysfunction

  • Glucose: Blood sugar — elevated in diabetes (affects WOB when ketoacidosis occurs)
  • BUN (Blood Urea Nitrogen): Kidney filtration efficiency — elevated in renal failure
  • Bilirubin: Liver function — elevated with liver disease/hemolysis
  • Creatinine: Kidney clearance — elevated indicates renal dysfunction; affects drug dosing

Hematology — CBC

Ordered for infection or anemia

  • WBC: Normal 4,500–11,000/μL. >11,000 = elevated → suggests infection or inflammation
  • Hemoglobin/Hematocrit: Low = anemia → reduced O₂ carrying capacity (relevant to PA 2.2)
  • Platelets: Clotting function — relevant before invasive procedures (thoracentesis)
  • Differential: Neutrophils ↑ = bacterial; Lymphocytes ↑ = viral infection

Electrolytes

Ordered for weakness, diarrhea, vomiting, or kidney signs

  • Na⁺ (Sodium): 135–145 mEq/L — fluid balance; hyponatremia causes confusion
  • K⁺ (Potassium): 3.5–5.0 mEq/L — critical for cardiac and muscle function; low from vomiting; high in renal failure
  • Cl⁻ (Chloride): 95–105 mEq/L — lost with vomiting; guides metabolic alkalosis interpretation
  • Ca²⁺ (Calcium): 8.5–10.5 mg/dL — muscle/nerve function; low in alkalosis causes tetany

Which Lab Panel Provides This Information?

A respiratory patient's hemoglobin is critically low — the team wants to know if anemia is contributing to hypoxia.
A patient who has been vomiting for days is weak and having muscle cramps. The team suspects low potassium and chloride.
Patient with ascites, jaundice, and elevated creatinine. The team wants to assess both liver and kidney function simultaneously.
Patient with fever and productive cough. The team wants to know if the white blood cell count supports a diagnosis of bacterial infection.

Recognizing Infection — Clinical Signs and Special Populations

Infection produces a constellation of systemic signs triggered by the body's immune response. However, these signs are NOT universally present — elderly and immunocompromised patients may present atypically, making clinical vigilance critical for the respiratory therapist.

Atypical Presentations

Elderly Patients

The classic signs of infection — fever and elevated WBC — may be ABSENT in elderly patients. The most common atypical presentation is new onset confusion or disorientation. A previously oriented elderly patient who suddenly becomes confused should be evaluated for infection (UTI, pneumonia, sepsis) before attributing it to cognitive decline. Monitor mental status closely.

Clinical Pearl: An 80-year-old who is "not acting like herself" at 2 AM — start the infection workup. Do not wait for fever.
Immunocompromised Patients

Patients on corticosteroids, chemotherapy, or with HIV/AIDS may have severely blunted immune responses. They may have minimal or no fever, normal or even low WBC (neutropenia), and minimal local inflammation — yet have life-threatening infections. Maintain high clinical suspicion in these patients.

Gram Stain & Antibiotic Selection

The gram stain is the fastest microbiological tool available — results in minutes, before culture and sensitivity data (which takes 24–72 hours). Knowing the gram reaction and morphology of the organism immediately narrows the antibiotic selection and guides empirical treatment.

PURPLE

Gram-Positive

Thick peptidoglycan cell wall retains the crystal violet dye — stains purple/blue. More susceptible to penicillin-based antibiotics because penicillin targets peptidoglycan synthesis.

Examples:

  • Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA if resistant)
  • Streptococcus pneumoniae (CAP)
  • Streptococcus pyogenes (strep throat)
PINK/RED

Gram-Negative

Thin peptidoglycan wall plus outer lipopolysaccharide membrane — loses crystal violet during decolorization step, picks up safranin counterstain — stains pink/red. Outer membrane makes them more resistant to many antibiotics.

Examples:

  • Pseudomonas aeruginosa (HAP/VAP)
  • Escherichia coli (hospital infections)
  • Moraxella catarrhalis (COPD exacerbations)

Antibiotic Selection — Broad vs. Narrow Spectrum

Broad-Spectrum Antibiotics

Used when: Culture/sensitivity results not yet available. Suspected mixed infection. Critically ill patient where delay risks deterioration.

Advantage: Covers a wide range of gram-positive AND gram-negative organisms simultaneously.

Disadvantage: Can promote antibiotic resistance and eliminate beneficial microbiome organisms.

Example: Amoxicillin-clavulanate (Augmentin) — covers strep, staph, H. influenzae, Moraxella, E. coli. Used empirically for CAP and exacerbations when culture not yet available.

Narrow-Spectrum Antibiotics

Used when: Culture and sensitivity results are back. Specific organism and location identified. Targeted therapy possible.

Advantage: Targeted — more effective against the specific organism, fewer side effects, reduced resistance development.

Disadvantage: Cannot be used empirically — must know the organism first.

Example: Penicillin G — narrow coverage targeting gram-positive streptococcal infections specifically. Not appropriate until organism is identified by culture.
Selective Toxicity — Why Antibiotics Work (and Why Chemotherapy Is Different): Antibiotics exploit structural differences between bacterial cells and human cells. For example, bacteria have peptidoglycan cell walls — human cells do not. Penicillin attacks peptidoglycan synthesis specifically, killing bacteria without harming human cells. This is called selective toxicity. Chemotherapy agents, by contrast, attack cellular processes that both cancer cells AND normal cells use — which is why chemotherapy has far more side effects than most antibiotics.

Pneumonia Classification — CAP, HAP & VAP

Pneumonia is classified by when and where the infection is acquired — not just by organism. This distinction determines the likely causative pathogen, the expected antibiotic resistance patterns, and the treatment approach. Timing relative to hospital admission is the key criterion.

CAP — Community-Acquired Pneumonia

Timing: Symptoms present before admission OR within <48 hours of hospital admission

Common organism: Streptococcus pneumoniae

Presentation: Wet cough, fever, lobar consolidation on CXR, productive sputum

Treatment: Azithromycin + Ceftriaxone (covers both atypicals and typical bacteria)

Key Identifier: Symptoms begin in the community — before hospital exposure.
HAP — Hospital-Acquired Pneumonia

Timing: Symptoms appear ≥48 hours AFTER hospital admission (not intubated)

Common organism: MRSA (Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus) and gram-negative rods

Risk: Higher severity — hospital organisms are more antibiotic-resistant than community strains

Treatment: Broad-spectrum antibiotics targeting resistant organisms (vancomycin for MRSA + coverage for gram-negatives)

Key Identifier: New pneumonia symptoms at 48+ hours after hospital admission.
VAP — Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia

Timing: Occurs in mechanically ventilated patients (typically ≥48 hours of intubation)

Common organisms: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, MRSA, Acinetobacter baumanii

Mechanism: Bacteria enter via aspiration around the ET tube cuff, contamination of the ventilator circuit, or oral secretions dripping below the cuff

Treatment: Broad-spectrum targeting resistant gram-negatives + MRSA coverage. Prevention is the priority.

Key Identifier: Mechanically ventilated patient develops new pneumonia. Bundle prevention is essential.

Prevention of HAP & VAP — The RT's Evidence-Based Bundle

Hospital-acquired pneumonia and ventilator-associated pneumonia are largely preventable. The respiratory therapist's daily practice directly influences infection rates in critically ill patients. These five strategies form the RT's core infection prevention bundle.

🤲
Hand Hygiene — Most Important Measure

Perform before AND after: entering the patient's room, all patient contact, touching any item in the room. Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) is acceptable for most situations. Soap and water required for C. difficile (ABHR is ineffective against C. diff spores).

Evidence: Hand hygiene compliance is the single most effective measure to prevent healthcare-associated infections. It prevents cross-contamination from patient to patient via the RT's hands and equipment.
⚕️
Equipment Management

Per AARC guidelines: use a new sterile nebulizer for each treatment. If reusable equipment is used, clean and dry completely between uses — moisture supports bacterial growth. Do not share masks, nasal cannulas, or other respiratory equipment between patients.

Common error: Leaving a nebulizer soaking in saline between treatments. Wet, warm nebulizers are ideal culture media for Pseudomonas and other gram-negative organisms.
🫁
Ventilator Circuit Management

Minimize unnecessary disconnections of the ventilator circuit. Each disconnection exposes the inside of the circuit to ambient air and surfaces — a route for bacterial entry. Condensate (water pooling in the tubing) should be drained away from the patient — do not drain toward the patient's airway.

Circuit changes: Guidelines no longer recommend routine scheduled circuit changes. Change only when clinically indicated (visible contamination). Frequent changes increase VAP risk by increasing circuit disruption.
🦷
Oral Care — VAP Prevention

Perform systematic oral care on ALL ventilated patients at a minimum every 2–4 hours with chlorhexidine gluconate (CHG) oral rinse, per institutional protocol. The rationale: oral bacteria (especially gram-negative organisms that colonize the mouth in ICU patients) pool in oropharyngeal secretions and drip around the ET tube cuff into the lower airways.

For non-ventilated patients: Encourage and assist with tooth brushing. Oral hygiene reduces the bacterial load available for aspiration.
🥼
Isolation Precautions & PPE

Follow institution-specific isolation protocols for patients with confirmed or suspected transmissible organisms (MRSA, VRE, C. diff, respiratory viruses). Use appropriate PPE — but critically — do NOT keep PPE on between patients. PPE worn for one patient and then walked into another patient's room transfers organisms between patients.

Donning and doffing: Proper PPE removal technique (doffing) is as important as wearing it. The contaminated outer surface of gloves and gowns must not contact the wearer's skin or clothing during removal.

Case Summary

Decision 1 — HAP: Pneumonia developing ≥48 hours after admission = HAP. Not VAP (not intubated). Target MRSA and resistant gram-negatives with broad-spectrum antibiotics.
Decision 2 — Start Broad-Spectrum: Gram stain shows gram-negative bacilli but culture is 48 hours away. Deteriorating patient cannot wait. Start broad-spectrum empirical antibiotics covering gram-negatives NOW. De-escalate after culture and sensitivity returns.
Decision 3 — Inadequate Specimen: 0.5 mL of clear watery saliva is not a valid sputum specimen. Minimum 2 mL of actual lower-airway sputum required. Coach proper technique (3-4 deep breaths, productive deep cough) or use nasotracheal suction.
Decision 4 — Atypical Elderly Presentation: New confusion without fever or elevated WBC in an elderly patient = infection until proven otherwise. Complete infection workup (UA, blood cultures, CBC, CXR). Never dismiss elderly confusion as "sundowning" without ruling out infection first.

FAQ

Diagnostics, Infections & Prevention — Common Questions

Why does the sputum need to be at least 2 mL, and what happens if the sample is saliva?

The laboratory requires a minimum of 2 mL of actual sputum to perform a gram stain AND culture and sensitivity reliably. If the sample volume is inadequate, the results may be inconclusive. More importantly, a saliva sample contains the normal oral flora (Streptococcus viridans, Veillonella, Bacteroides) — organisms that are NOT the ones causing the lower respiratory infection. A saliva culture will grow these oral contaminants and lead to inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions. The lab can actually screen samples microscopically — a sample with >25 squamous epithelial cells per low-power field is classified as "saliva" and rejected. Properly collected sputum has <10 squamous epithelial cells and >25 neutrophils per low-power field.

What is the difference between HAP and VAP — aren't they the same?

VAP is actually a subset of HAP. HAP is any pneumonia acquired in the hospital ≥48 hours after admission. VAP is specifically HAP that occurs in patients who are mechanically ventilated. VAP has its own unique risk factors (ET tube bypassing upper airway defenses, pooling of secretions above the cuff), its own organism profile (more resistant gram-negative organisms like Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter), and its own prevention bundle (oral care, circuit management, cuff pressure management, head of bed elevation). HAP in non-ventilated patients is also serious but has somewhat different prevention strategies (ambulation, incentive spirometry, oral hygiene).

When should the RT start broad-spectrum antibiotics vs. waiting for culture results?

The general principle: the sicker the patient, the less you can afford to wait. For a stable outpatient with mild CAP, you may have time to collect cultures before starting antibiotics (though even this is increasingly challenged by evidence). For a critically ill ICU patient with worsening hemodynamics, increasing O₂ requirements, and signs of septic shock — every hour of antibiotic delay increases mortality. Current sepsis guidelines recommend antibiotics within 1 hour of septic shock recognition. The gram stain (available in 30-60 minutes) guides the initial choice. Culture and sensitivity (48-72 hours) then guide de-escalation to a narrower, targeted antibiotic.

Why is the thoracentesis needle inserted above the rib rather than below it?

The intercostal neurovascular bundle — the intercostal nerve, artery, and vein — runs along the inferior (lower) margin of each rib, protected in a groove. If the thoracentesis needle is placed below the rib (in the neurovascular groove), it risks lacerating the intercostal artery, causing potentially significant bleeding into the pleural space (hemothorax) — making the situation worse. By inserting the needle along the SUPERIOR margin of the lower rib (just above the rib in the intercostal space), the neurovascular bundle is avoided. This is one of the most clinically important anatomical principles in chest procedures.

Can an elderly patient with an infection have a completely normal temperature and WBC?

Yes — this is one of the most clinically important pearls in geriatric medicine. The elderly immune system has diminished reserve — the febrile response requires intact interleukin and tumor necrosis factor signaling, which is often blunted with age. Similarly, the bone marrow's ability to rapidly produce white blood cells may be reduced. An elderly patient with pneumonia or a UTI may present with temperature 37.2°C (technically "normal") and WBC 8,400 — both appearing unremarkable — yet have life-threatening sepsis. The most common atypical presentation is acute confusion (delirium). Any sudden change in mental status, behavior, or functional ability in an elderly patient should trigger an infection workup even in the absence of classic signs.

Summary — Diagnostic Tests, Infections & Prevention

8 Diagnostic Tests: Sputum = productive cough. Skin test = TB screening. Bronchoscopy = mucus plug. Thoracentesis = pleural effusion. Pleurodesis = recurrent effusion/PTX. Blood chemistry = heart/liver/kidney. CBC = infection/anemia. Electrolytes = weakness/vomiting/kidney.
Gram Stain: Purple = gram-positive (thick peptidoglycan). Pink = gram-negative (outer lipopolysaccharide membrane). Broad-spectrum = before culture (empirical). Narrow-spectrum = after culture and sensitivity (targeted).
Pneumonia Classification: CAP = symptoms before or within <48 hrs of admission (S. pneumoniae). HAP = symptoms ≥48 hrs after admission (MRSA, gram-negatives). VAP = mechanically ventilated patient (Pseudomonas, MRSA, Acinetobacter).
Infection Signs: Classic = fever, chills, sweating, pain, fatigue, elevated WBC, wound redness, weight loss. Atypical in elderly = new confusion WITHOUT fever or elevated WBC. Treat as infection until proven otherwise.
VAP Prevention Bundle: 🤲 Hand hygiene (before/after all contact) | ⚕️ New sterile nebulizer each treatment | 🫁 Minimize circuit disconnections | 🦷 Oral care with CHG every 2-4 hours | 🥼 Correct PPE use — doff between patients, never wear between rooms.
