SOAP Notes & Clinical Documentation

Documentation is a clinical assessment and communication skill. In this lesson, you will organize respiratory information into SOAP format, use objective and legally defensible wording, document comparable treatment response, make reassessment and escalation visible, and record refusal, adverse response, provider notification, and corrections transparently.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners should be able to explain the purpose of RT documentation, sort assessment data into SOAP categories, identify respiratory information from a chart, and write clear beginner-level respiratory documentation.

1. Define SOAP
Describe what belongs in Subjective, Objective, Assessment, and Plan sections.
2. Sort data correctly
Differentiate what the patient reports from what the RT measures or observes.
3. Extract chart data
Identify important respiratory information from a simple chart review.
4. Document clearly
Write concise RT notes that communicate findings, actions, response, and reassessment.
5. Close the treatment loop
Compare relevant pre- and post-treatment data and distinguish tolerance from therapeutic response.
6. Document exceptional events
Record refusal, adverse response, deterioration, notification, correction, late entry, and addendum transparently.
7. Critique the record
Identify missing evidence, unclear chronology, inconsistent data, unsupported conclusions, and an absent next safe step.

Why RT Documentation Matters

Respiratory therapists document so the care team can understand what the patient reported, what the RT observed, what was done, how the patient responded, and what should happen next. Good documentation is part of safe patient care.

Communication
Your note helps nurses, providers, and other RTs understand the respiratory picture.
Continuity
The next clinician can see trends and continue care appropriately.
Legal record
The medical record should accurately reflect assessment, treatment, response, and patient education.

The SOAP Framework

SOAP is a simple way to organize clinical information. For beginners, think of it as a four-part story: what the patient says, what you find, what you think it means, and what you plan to do next.

SOAP PartPlain LanguageRespiratory Example
SubjectiveWhat the patient reports."I feel short of breath." Smoking history. Home inhaler use.
ObjectiveWhat you measure, observe, or hear.RR 28/min, SpO₂ 89%, wheezing, oxygen device.
AssessmentYour clinical interpretation of the findings.Increased work of breathing with wheezing and low oxygen saturation.
PlanWhat will happen next.Give ordered therapy, educate patient, reassess response, notify provider if worsening.

Internal Logic and Chronology

The Objective findings must support the Assessment, and the Plan must respond to the Assessment. The note should also show the sequence of assessment, intervention, response, communication, and follow-up. When the patient is unstable, assessment and escalation come before completing a polished SOAP entry.

Relevant and Concise

Include information that affects respiratory status, safety, continuity, or the next decision. A concise complete note is stronger than copied detail that hides the clinically important change.

Subjective vs Objective Data

Before writing a full SOAP note, learners must be able to separate subjective and objective information. Subjective data comes from the patient or caregiver. Objective data comes from measurement, observation, testing, or assessment.

Writing the Assessment and Plan

The Assessment and Plan are often harder for beginners because they require interpretation. Start simple: state the respiratory concern you see, then state what should happen next and what must be reassessed.

Assessment asks:
What does the information suggest?
Plan asks:
What should be done next?
Reassessment asks:
Did the patient improve, worsen, or stay the same?

Quality Documentation: Clear, Objective, Useful

Good documentation does not need to be fancy. It needs to be accurate, objective, complete enough to support care, and clear enough that another clinician can understand what happened.

Documentation PrincipleWhat It Requires
Describe, do not labelReplace “difficult,” “noncompliant,” or “looked bad” with exact behavior, patient statements, measurable findings, timing, and response.
Accuracy before speedVerify patient, time, device, oxygen setting, medication, dose, route, response, and critical values before signing.
TimelinessDocument close enough to the event that chronology is reliable. Use the approved late-entry process when delayed.
Approved terminologyUse approved abbreviations, units, device details, location, laterality, and timing.
Copy-forward safetyReview and update copied text so old oxygen settings, breath sounds, or assessments do not remain after the condition changes.
Truthfulness and attributionDocument only what occurred and identify the source when recording information received from someone else.

Respiratory Treatment Documentation & Comparable Reassessment

Giving a treatment does not prove that it worked. The therapist must document a relevant baseline, the intervention, comparable post-treatment findings, patient-reported response, adverse effects, and the next step.

Before TreatmentInterventionAfter Treatment
Indication; HR, RR, SpO₂; oxygen device/setting; breath sounds; work of breathing; dyspnea; cough or secretions when relevantMedication or modality, dose/setting, delivery method, duration, oxygen support, and any deviationRepeat the same relevant variables, record timing and oxygen context, ask the patient, note adverse effects, classify improved/unchanged/worsened, and state the next action

Tolerance Is Not the Same as Therapeutic Response

“Tolerated well” means the patient completed the intervention without an obvious problem. It does not show that wheezing decreased, respiratory rate changed, oxygenation improved, dyspnea lessened, or the treatment achieved its purpose.

Treatment Refusal, Education, Autonomy & Adverse Response

A patient with decision-making capacity may refuse care. The therapist should assess current status, clarify the reason, address remediable barriers, explain expected benefit and material risk in understandable language, respect the informed decision, notify according to policy, and arrange reassessment.

Refusal Note ElementWhat to Document
Therapy declinedExact treatment, modality, or support the patient declined or stopped.
Current respiratory statusRelevant symptoms, RR, SpO₂ with device/setting, breath sounds, work of breathing, and immediate risk.
Patient reasonThe patient's own words when clinically useful, such as nausea, pain, fear, misunderstanding, or prior adverse effect.
Education and problem-solvingExpected benefit, material risk, alternatives or troubleshooting offered, and how understanding was assessed.
Final decision and communicationContinued refusal, person notified, time, response/order, and reassessment or escalation plan.

Adverse Response Sequence

When significant tachycardia, chest pain, worsening dyspnea, desaturation, nausea, dizziness, or altered mental status occurs: pause or stop as appropriate, reassess, support the patient, notify, document the chronology, and reassess again.

Provider Notification, Closed-Loop Communication & Handoff

“Provider aware” does not show what was communicated, whether the message was received, what response occurred, or what happens next.

Communicate
Time, person notified, current findings/trend, oxygen or device context, interventions attempted, concern, and recommendation.
Close the loop
Record the provider response or order and use read-back or repeat-back according to policy.
Follow through
Continue support and monitoring; document implementation, response, reassessment, or further escalation.

Corrections, Late Entries, Addenda & Record Integrity

Never delete, overwrite, backdate, change the time, or conceal an original entry. Follow institutional policy so the original record and audit trail remain visible.

MethodPurpose
CorrectionIdentifies inaccurate information and provides the correct information while preserving the original entry.
Late entryDocuments an event after the fact while showing both the current entry time and the actual event time.
AddendumAdds clinically important information after the original note with clear authorship and timestamp.
EscalationSuspected falsification or intentional concealment is reported through the designated supervisory or compliance process.

Documentation Critique: Is the Note Safe and Actionable?

A polished sentence is not automatically a strong clinical note. Critique whether the evidence supports the Assessment, the chronology is clear, the data are consistent, reassessment is complete, and the next safe action is visible.

Critique QuestionCommon Problem
Is the evidence complete?Missing oxygen setting, pre/post values, breath sounds, work of breathing, timing, response, or notification details.
Is the conclusion supported?Unverified diagnosis, severity claim, motive, or treatment success not supported by the recorded data.
Can the chronology be reconstructed?Deterioration, intervention, communication, and response are combined without times.
Are the conditions comparable?SpO₂ improved, but oxygen support changed and was omitted.
Can another clinician act safely?The current problem, urgency, completed action, response, and next responsibility are unclear.

Identify the Primary Documentation Problem

“Albuterol given. Patient better.”
O: RR 22/min, SpO₂ 95% on room air, clear breath sounds. A: Severe bacterial pneumonia.
SpO₂ improved from 88% to 95%, but the note omits that oxygen increased from room air to 4 L/min.
Pre-albuterol: HR 104, RR 26, SpO₂ 91% RA, bilateral wheezes, dyspnea 6/10. Fifteen minutes post: HR 112, RR 22, SpO₂ 93% RA, wheezing decreased, dyspnea 3/10, mild tremor. Continue monitoring and notify if HR or symptoms worsen.

FAQ

Common Documentation Questions

Do I need to write perfect SOAP notes right away?

No. At this stage, the goal is to correctly organize data and write clearly. Your notes will become more detailed as your assessment skills grow.

Can the same information appear in more than one section?

The raw data should usually appear once in the best category. You may refer to findings again when interpreting them in the Assessment section.

Should RTs diagnose diseases in SOAP notes?

RTs document findings, interpretation, response, and respiratory care. Avoid over-diagnosing beyond your role and available information.

What makes documentation useful?

Useful documentation is accurate, specific, objective when possible, and includes patient response and reassessment needs.

Is “tolerated well” enough after a treatment?

No. Tolerance and therapeutic response are different. Document relevant comparable pre- and post-treatment findings, patient report, adverse effects, and the next step.

What should replace “provider aware”?

Record the time, person notified, relevant findings and trend, oxygen or device context, concern or recommendation, response or order, and follow-up plan.

Can I delete a documentation error?

No. Use the approved correction, addendum, or late-entry process so the original record, authorship, timing, and audit trail remain transparent.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

SOAP notes help RTs organize assessment findings and communicate respiratory care clearly. Strong documentation separates patient reports from measured findings, interprets respiratory concerns appropriately, and includes a plan for therapy and reassessment.

Must know:
Subjective = what the patient reports.
Must know:
Objective = what you measure, observe, hear, or test.
Documentation pearl:
Patient response after therapy matters.
Common mistake:
Avoid vague notes like "patient looked bad." Use findings.
Treatment pearl:
Repeat relevant baseline variables before claiming improvement or failure.
Communication pearl:
“Provider aware” is not closed-loop documentation.
Integrity pearl:
Correct transparently; never delete, backdate, or conceal the original entry.
Safety pearl:
In an unstable patient, assess and escalate before completing routine charting.
