Clinical Documentation — Purpose and the Two Primary Forms

Clinical documentation is the permanent written record of patient assessment, treatment, and response to therapy. A common legal and quality principle is: care that is not documented may be difficult to verify. Thorough documentation protects the patient, protects the RT legally, enables continuity of care, and supports insurance reimbursement.

Respiratory Care Assessment Form

When completed: Once per patient encounter or new admission. Completed at initial evaluation and when significant status changes occur.

Purpose: Comprehensive patient evaluation — provides the full clinical picture of the patient's respiratory status, history, current condition, and plan.

  • Demographics and identification
  • Chief complaint and history
  • Complete physical exam findings
  • All diagnostic test results (CXR, ABG, labs)
  • Current oxygen therapy and orders
  • Clinical assessment and plan

Respiratory Care Treatment Form

When completed: After EACH treatment session — multiple forms per day (one per treatment). Document before leaving the patient area.

Purpose: Documents each individual therapy session — what was done, vital signs before and after, how the patient responded, and any adverse reactions.

  • Treatment identification (date, time, therapist)
  • Pre-treatment vitals and breath sounds
  • Mid-treatment data (only if treatment >30 minutes)
  • Post-treatment vitals and breath sounds
  • Patient response and adverse reactions
Six Functions of Clinical Documentation: Patient care continuity across shifts · Legal record of treatments provided · Communication between the healthcare team · Quality assurance and review · Billing and insurance reimbursement · Research and quality improvement data.

Respiratory Care Assessment Form — Ten Required Components

Every component of the Assessment Form serves a clinical purpose. Missing sections leave the clinical picture incomplete and create legal vulnerabilities. Follow the form and facility policy for unavailable information; use an approved notation such as “not available,” “not assessed,” or “N/A” when appropriate rather than inventing data.

1 — Patient Demographics: Full name, Medical Record Number (MRN), Date of Admission (DOA), attending physician name, current room number. These verify identity and continuity.
2 — Chief Complaint: Primary respiratory reason for admission. Example: "Dyspnea × 3 days," "Post-operative monitoring," "Acute COPD exacerbation."
3 — History of Present Illness (HPI): How long has the problem existed? Progression (improving, stable, worsening)? Any recent changes? Relevant timeline of current episode.
4 — Past Medical History (PMH): Chronic conditions (asthma, COPD, heart disease), surgical history, smoking history (current/former/never, pack-years), current medications, and allergies.
5 — Current Vital Signs: Most recent HR, RR, BP, Temperature, and SpO₂ on current oxygen delivery. Must be current — not from 6 hours ago.
6 — Physical Exam Findings: Breath sounds (location AND quality: clear, crackles, wheezing, diminished, bilateral/unilateral), accessory muscle use, cough (productive/nonproductive; color, consistency, volume if productive), general appearance (A&O level, distress, ability to speak).
7 — Diagnostic Test Results: Most recent CXR findings (infiltrates, consolidation, atelectasis, effusion — bilateral/unilateral, location). ABG if available (pH, PaCO₂, PaO₂, HCO₃⁻, time drawn, FiO₂ at time of draw). Other labs: CBC, electrolytes, sputum culture if available.
8 — Current Oxygen Therapy: Current device, flow rate (L/min), SpO₂ at this setting, estimated FiO₂. Example: "Simple mask, 6L/min, SpO₂ 92%, FiO₂ ~44%."
9 — Physician Orders: All respiratory care orders in the chart — oxygen therapy protocol, airway clearance type and frequency, lung expansion type and frequency, aerosol medications (drug, dose, frequency), monitoring orders.
10 — Clinical Assessment/Plan: RT's clinical interpretation. Which protocols are indicated based on findings? What should be started, continued, modified, or discontinued? Any physician recommendations?

"Coarse crackles in right lower lobe. Expiratory wheezing bilaterally. Diminished at left base. Accessory muscle use: minimal. Productive cough, yellow-green sputum, moderate amount."

Respiratory Care Treatment Form — Pre, Mid & Post Documentation

The Treatment Form documents each individual therapy session. Pre-treatment data is required for every session — you cannot evaluate a response without a baseline. Post-treatment data is always required. Mid-treatment monitoring and documentation follow the course form and current facility policy; longer treatments and higher-risk patients require interval reassessment.

Pre-Treatment (Always Required)

  • Treatment modality and medication
  • Breath sounds — location and quality
  • Cough effectiveness and sputum characteristics
  • Patient position
  • Full vital signs (HR, RR, BP, SpO₂)
  • Patient status — alert/oriented, work of breathing
  • Presence of bronchospasm or wheezing

Mid-Treatment (If Treatment >15-30 min)

  • Vital signs (HR, RR, SpO₂)
  • Any breath sound changes
  • Patient tolerance and comfort
  • Early signs of adverse reaction

Required for oxygen treatments >30 min and aerosol treatments >15 min. Skip for short treatments.

Post-Treatment (Always Required)

  • Breath sounds — compare to pre-treatment
  • Sputum expectorated (amount, color, consistency)
  • Full vital signs post-treatment
  • Patient response ("states breathing easier")
  • Adverse reaction check — HR threshold
  • Overall assessment of treatment effectiveness
  • Continue, modify, or discontinue plan?
Key Documentation Rule — Be Specific: "Breath sounds improved" is not adequate documentation. Write: "Breath sounds improved from bilateral expiratory wheezing to scattered end-expiratory wheezes in upper lobes following treatment." Specific, objective, comparative documentation is legally defensible. Vague documentation is not.

HR Adverse Reaction Threshold — The 30% Rule

Bronchodilators (especially Albuterol) can cause tachycardia as a side effect. The RT must recognize when a heart rate increase following treatment constitutes an adverse reaction requiring documentation and physician notification. This course uses a 30% increase from baseline and an absolute heart-rate trigger for learning. Apply it with symptoms, rhythm, medication guidance, and current facility policy.

Adverse Reaction Threshold Calculation:
Pre-treatment HR × 1.3 = 30% threshold
If post-treatment HR > threshold, OR post-treatment HR > 125 bpm under the course rule → document the response, assess symptoms and rhythm, take the indicated action, notify, and reassess

Example: Pre-treatment HR = 90 bpm
Threshold = 90 × 1.3 = 117 bpm
Post-treatment HR = 122 bpm → ADVERSE REACTION (122 > 117)

Example 2: Pre-treatment HR = 80 bpm
Threshold = 80 × 1.3 = 104 bpm
Post-treatment HR = 98 bpm → Within normal response (98 < 104)

Additional trigger: Post-treatment HR > 125 bpm regardless of baseline = adverse reaction
Other Adverse Reactions to Document: Tremor or muscle shaking (adrenergic effect of Albuterol), anxiety or restlessness, palpitations, nausea, paradoxical bronchospasm (wheezing worsening post-treatment), and any other negative response. Document: specific reaction, vital sign changes, action taken, patient's response to intervention.

Adverse Reaction or Normal Response?

Pre-treatment HR: 88 bpm. Post-treatment HR after Albuterol nebulizer: 118 bpm. (Threshold = 88 × 1.3 = 114.4 bpm)
Pre-treatment HR: 72 bpm. Post-treatment HR: 88 bpm. (Threshold = 72 × 1.3 = 93.6 bpm)
Pre-treatment HR: 100 bpm. Post-treatment HR: 128 bpm. HR is above 125 bpm regardless of threshold calculation.
Pre-treatment HR: 95 bpm. Post-treatment HR: 110 bpm. (Threshold = 95 × 1.3 = 123.5 bpm)

HIPAA — What It Is and Why It Applies to You

HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996) is federal law. As a respiratory therapist, you are legally obligated to comply with every provision. Improper access, use, or disclosure can lead to organizational discipline, professional consequences, civil enforcement, and in some cases criminal penalties.

HIPAA — Four Core Purposes
  1. Improve efficiency and effectiveness of the healthcare system
  2. Establish standards for protecting patient privacy and information security
  3. Ensure portability of health insurance coverage (patient can keep coverage between jobs)
  4. Protect patient rights regarding their healthcare information
Who HIPAA Applies To

HIPAA directly regulates covered entities and business associates; workforce members must follow their organization’s HIPAA policies and safeguards — including respiratory therapists, nurses, physicians, administrative staff, and any business associate handling patient information. There are no exceptions based on job title or years of experience.

Your responsibility: HIPAA compliance is not optional. Ignorance of the law does not protect you from prosecution. Training is mandatory.

Protected Health Information (PHI) — Know It When You See It

PHI is any information in a medical record that (1) can be used to identify a patient AND (2) relates to their past, present, or future medical condition, treatment, or payment. The identifying link is what makes it protected — information is de-identified only when it meets an approved HIPAA de-identification method; removing a name alone is not enough.

HIPAA Rules and Administrative Simplification Standards

HIPAA includes the Privacy, Security, Breach Notification, and Enforcement Rules, along with Administrative Simplification standards for transactions, code sets, and identifiers, each addressing a different aspect of patient information protection. Knowing which rule governs which requirement helps you understand HIPAA as a framework rather than just a list of prohibitions.

Privacy Rule

Establishes patient RIGHTS regarding their medical information and sets standards for use and disclosure.

  • Patient has right to access their own records
  • Patient can request corrections to records
  • Patient has right to know who accessed their information
  • Electronic chart audit trails track every access
  • Use and disclosure must have a permitted purpose. Treatment communication among involved providers is permitted; other uses may require authorization or another legal basis.
Notice of Privacy Practices must be provided as required by the covered entity’s workflow and federal notice requirements — explains their rights and how their information will be used. Patient should sign acknowledging receipt.

Security Rule

Protects electronic PHI (ePHI) specifically — ensuring confidentiality, integrity, and availability of electronic patient data.

Three categories of security safeguards (covered in detail in the next section):

  • Administrative: Policies, training, Privacy Officer
  • Physical: Locked records, shredding, badge access
  • Technical: Encryption, passwords, audit logs
On open networks (internet): HIPAA requires encryption, sign-on codes, passwords, and authentication.

Transactions and Code Set Standards

Standardizes electronic coding for healthcare transactions to ensure consistent, accurate data transfer across healthcare systems.

ICD-10-CM (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification): standardized diagnosis codes used in all healthcare documentation and billing.

  • Every diagnosis has a specific ICD-10 code
  • Enables smooth, accurate electronic data transfer
  • Ensures consistency across all healthcare systems
  • Required for insurance billing and claims

The Three Types of Security Safeguards

The HIPAA Security Rule requires three categories of safeguards to protect electronic PHI. Understanding which type of safeguard addresses which type of threat helps you recognize compliance gaps and apply the right protective measure.

Administrative Safeguards

Policies, procedures, and workforce management. Usually handled by the hospital's Privacy Officer. Addresses HOW the organization manages access and compliance.

  • Designated Privacy Officer
  • Mandatory employee HIPAA training
  • Access control policies (who can see what)
  • Incident response procedures
  • Employee discipline for violations
  • Business Associate Agreements (BAAs)
Physical Safeguards

Physical security of devices and facilities containing PHI. Addresses the physical environment — who can get in, what can be seen, and how information is stored and destroyed.

  • Locked storage of paper medical records
  • Secure document shredding
  • Badge access to restricted areas
  • Security cameras in sensitive areas
  • Restricted access to server rooms
  • Data destruction from retired computers
Technical Safeguards

Technology-based protections for electronic data. Addresses digital security — encrypting data, controlling access, and tracking who sees what electronically.

  • Data encryption (especially on open networks)
  • Password authentication and sign-on codes
  • Biometric authentication (fingerprint, retina)
  • Firewalls
  • Audit logs tracking all electronic access
  • Automatic log-off after inactivity
  • Data backup systems

Administrative, Physical, or Technical Safeguard?

The hospital's EHR system automatically logs out after 5 minutes of inactivity and requires password re-entry to resume.
Paper medical records are stored in a locked room accessed only by authorized staff with ID badge swipe access. Documents are shredded in cross-cut shredders.
The hospital requires all new employees to complete a 4-hour HIPAA compliance training course before accessing any patient records. Violations result in immediate termination.
All patient data transmitted over the hospital's wireless network is encrypted using 256-bit AES encryption. Unencrypted transmission triggers an automatic block.

HIPAA Violations — Consequences That End Careers

HIPAA violations are investigated and prosecuted by the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) Office for Civil Rights. Violations can be civil (fines) or criminal (prison). Both intentional and unintentional violations carry serious penalties.

Mandatory Exceptions — When Disclosure Is Required

HIPAA protects patient information — but certain uses or disclosures are permitted or required by law. The exact duty, recipient, and information disclosed depend on applicable law and facility policy. Failing to report in these situations can itself result in legal consequences.

Abuse and Harm

Child abuse and some other safety concerns may be reportable under applicable law. Adult abuse and domestic-violence reporting requirements vary by jurisdiction and circumstance — even without patient consent. Healthcare workers are mandatory reporters.

Workers' Compensation Cases

Workers’ compensation disclosures must follow the applicable workers’ compensation law and approved organizational process for the workers' compensation claim. Report to the workers' compensation authority as required by law.

Communicable Disease Reporting

Reportable infectious diseases (tuberculosis, HIV in some states, measles, pertussis, hepatitis) must be reported to public health authorities for disease tracking and outbreak prevention. No patient consent required.

Adverse Drug/Device Reactions

Serious medication or device events should be handled through the approved safety and reporting process; the organization determines required external reporting. This enables post-market safety monitoring and protects future patients.

Court Subpoena

Court orders and subpoenas are not interchangeable. Route legal requests through health-information management or legal counsel and disclose only as authorized. Patient records become part of legal proceedings. The facility's legal team handles specifics, but records must be produced.

Vital Statistics

Birth and death certificates, public health authority requirements. Mandatory reporting to state vital statistics agencies for population health tracking.

Must Disclose or Confidential?

Four Compliance Strategies — Protecting PHI Every Shift

HIPAA compliance is not a one-time training event — it is a daily practice. These four strategies translate HIPAA regulations into specific behaviors you can apply from your first clinical day.

1
Control Information Sharing

Share patient information ONLY with those directly involved in that patient's care — "need to know" basis. This means: not with other patients, not with visitors, not with curious colleagues, not with family members unless authorized by the patient, and not with your own family members at home.

Test: Before sharing any patient information, ask: "Does this person need this information to provide care to THIS patient right now?" If the answer is no, do not share.
2
Protect Physical and Verbal Privacy

Never discuss patients in public spaces: elevators, cafeteria, hallways, waiting rooms, parking lots. Keep your voice low when giving or receiving report — other patients and visitors may overhear. Use private rooms for shift-to-shift handoff reports when possible.

Remember: The elevator is the most common place for HIPAA violations. Even using a patient's room number and a vague description ("the patient in 412 who's struggling") can constitute a violation if overhead by the wrong person.
3
Safeguard Physical Documents and Digital Screens

Paper: Never leave charts open for public view. Turn papers over or take them with you if you leave your station. Lock charts in designated areas. Shred documents properly — never dispose of in regular trash. Printers: retrieve printouts immediately.

Computers: Log off EVERY time you leave — even for 30 seconds. Position monitors away from public view. Never share passwords or leave them visible. Lock the screen if you must step away without logging off fully.

Audit trail fact: Every computer access is logged with your credentials, timestamp, and computer location. If you leave a computer logged in and someone else accesses PHI, YOUR credentials are in the log.
4
Control Chart Access — "Need to Know" Only

NEVER access patient records unless you are assigned to care for that patient. Do not look up: former patients out of curiosity, celebrities or VIPs at your facility, family members or friends, or your own records. Do not look up colleagues' records.

Celebrity violation: Accessing a famous person's record is a willful violation — you KNOW you are not their assigned provider. Healthcare workers have been terminated, lost licenses, and faced federal prosecution for this specific violation. Every access is logged and audited.

Social Media & Technology — A Modern HIPAA Minefield

Social media represents the fastest-growing category of HIPAA violations in healthcare. The mistaken belief that removing a patient's name makes a post acceptable has ended many careers. Context, timing, and institutional connection are enough to link a post back to a patient.

Social Media Violations

NEVER post patient information on social media — even without identifying the patient by name, even if you think no one will recognize them, even if you change details:

  • "My patient today was so rude to me…"
  • Photo of hospital room or equipment "at work today"
  • "Just had the craziest shift in the ICU"
  • Any post with #hospital #pneumonia #ICU + patient context
Why it's a violation: Other healthcare workers can identify patients from context. Timing, location, and clinical details can link back to specific patients. Intent does NOT matter — the disclosure is the violation.

Photography Rules

Patient photography is allowed ONLY when:

  • Using the official hospital camera system
  • For legitimate documentation in the medical record
  • With explicit written patient consent
  • For an authorized legitimate healthcare purpose
NEVER permitted: Personal phone photos of patients or patient areas, photos for social media, photos without written consent. A wound photo on your personal phone is a HIPAA violation — regardless of whether you share it.

Decision 4 — Assessment vs. Treatment Form

At 2 PM, you are called to re-evaluate a patient whose status has significantly changed since admission. She was initially stable but now has new bilateral crackles, HR 112, SpO₂ dropped to 88% from 95%, and a new chest X-ray shows bilateral infiltrates. You perform an IPV treatment and document the results. By end of shift you have: completed the initial evaluation, performed the IPV treatment, and given two separate nebulizer treatments. How many documentation forms are required?

Case Summary — Four Documentation and Compliance Decisions

Decision 1 — Adverse Reaction: 88 × 1.3 = 114.4. Post HR 115 > 114.4 = adverse reaction. Document the calculation, the threshold, the actual HR, and the specific reaction (tachycardia). Notify physician. Effective treatment ≠ no adverse reaction.
Decision 2 — Break Room Violation: Politely decline and redirect. Discussing patient diagnosis, identity, family, and prognosis in a break room with non-assigned staff = active HIPAA violations. Patient's local celebrity status makes recognition easier — and heightens the violation.
Decision 3 — Mandatory Exception: Domestic violence is a mandatory reporting exception to HIPAA. Document objective findings accurately. Notify physician and social work. Patient's refusal cannot override mandatory reporting law. Compassionate care continues alongside the report.
Decision 4 — Form Count: 2 Assessment Forms (initial + significant status change re-evaluation) + 3 Treatment Forms (one per treatment session) = 5 total forms. Treatment Forms cannot be combined. Each session requires its own documentation completed before leaving the patient area.

FAQ

Documentation & HIPAA — Common Questions

Why is the legal principle "If it's not documented, it didn't happen" so important for RTs?

In a legal dispute, the medical record is the primary evidence of what care was provided. If the RT gave a nebulizer treatment, performed a thorough assessment, and identified an adverse reaction — but none of it is documented — there is no legal evidence that it happened. From the court's perspective, the care did not occur. Conversely, if the documentation is thorough, the RT is legally protected: the record shows the assessment was done, the treatment was given, the adverse reaction was caught and reported, and the appropriate clinical action was taken. Thorough documentation is not bureaucracy — it is professional protection and evidence of quality care.

Can an RT discuss patient information with a family member who is asking about their loved one?

Only with explicit patient authorization. Under HIPAA, patients have the right to designate who may receive information about their care. This authorization is typically documented in writing at admission (a HIPAA release form). Without this authorization, family members — including spouses, parents, and adult children — cannot receive PHI even if they are the primary caregiver. The RT should direct family questions to the nurse or physician assigned to the patient. If a patient is incapacitated and has an authorized healthcare proxy or power of attorney, that designee can receive information. Even with a designated proxy, information shared should be limited to what is necessary for care decisions.

What happens if I accidentally leave a computer logged in and a visitor sees patient information?

This is an unintentional HIPAA violation and should be reported to your facility's Privacy Officer or compliance department. Penalties for unintentional violations range from $100-$50,000 per violation — but self-reporting and demonstrating corrective action typically results in lower penalties and a corrective action plan rather than maximum fines. The important steps are: (1) Secure the computer immediately. (2) Document what happened, when, and what information may have been visible. (3) Report to the Privacy Officer. (4) Cooperate with any investigation. Hiding an accidental breach can convert an unintentional violation into a negligent or willful one when discovered, dramatically increasing penalties.

Why must mid-treatment data be collected only if the treatment lasts more than 15-30 minutes?

Mid-treatment monitoring is designed to catch adverse reactions in real time during long treatments. A 5-minute nebulizer treatment has negligible time for a significant adverse reaction to develop before the post-treatment check. But a 30-minute IPV treatment or an extended oxygen therapy session gives enough time for tachycardia, bronchospasm, fatigue, or other adverse responses to develop and escalate before the post-treatment assessment. The mid-point check allows the RT to intervene during the treatment if needed — stopping or modifying the therapy before the session ends if adverse signs are detected.

Are the HIPAA exceptions (mandatory disclosures) the same in every state?

The federal HIPAA mandatory exceptions are a minimum baseline — states can add additional mandatory reporting requirements beyond what federal HIPAA requires. For example, mandatory reporting of domestic violence is required in some states but not others under state law. HIV reporting requirements vary by state. Certain professions (social workers, teachers) may be mandatory reporters under state law for abuse even in situations where healthcare workers might have discretion. The important principle: HIPAA provides the federal floor. State law may require MORE disclosure in certain circumstances. When in doubt, your facility's compliance team or the supervising physician will guide the specific reporting requirements in your jurisdiction.

Summary — Documentation and HIPAA Quick Reference

Documentation Forms: Assessment Form = once per encounter (10 components: demographics, complaint, HPI, PMH, vitals, exam, diagnostics, O₂, orders, plan). Treatment Form = once per treatment session (pre + post required; mid only if >15-30 min). Never leave fields blank — use N/A.
HR Adverse Reaction: Pre-HR × 1.3 = threshold. If post-HR exceeds threshold OR exceeds 125 bpm → adverse reaction. Document the calculation, result, and action taken. Notify physician.
HIPAA Rules: Privacy Rule = patient rights and record access. Security Rule = ePHI protection (Administrative / Physical / Technical safeguards). Transactions and Code Set Standards = ICD-10-CM standardization. Notice of Privacy Practices at first visit.
Violations and Exceptions: Penalties: $100-$250,000 + up to 10 years prison. Mandatory disclosures: abuse, workers' comp, communicable disease, adverse reactions, subpoena, vital statistics. Four compliance strategies: control sharing, protect verbal/physical privacy, safeguard documents/screens, control chart access.
