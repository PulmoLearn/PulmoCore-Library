Physical Assessment & Disease Differentiation

This lesson introduces bedside physical assessment skills respiratory therapists use to recognize normal findings, identify abnormal findings, and begin differentiating common cardiopulmonary disease patterns. No prior clinical experience is assumed.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners should be able to perform a beginner-level physical assessment sequence, explain what normal and abnormal findings mean, and match common disease patterns to assessment clues.

1. Describe the sequence
Explain inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation in patient-friendly language.
2. Recognize normal findings
Identify normal chest movement, resonant percussion, midline trachea, and normal breath sounds.
3. Interpret abnormal findings
Connect dullness, hyperresonance, decreased fremitus, tracheal shift, wheezes, crackles, and diminished sounds to possible causes.
4. Differentiate patterns
Use finding clusters to distinguish common diseases such as asthma, pleural effusion, pneumothorax, consolidation, atelectasis, and pulmonary edema.
Video Overview

2-Minute Physical Assessment Overview

Why Physical Assessment Matters

A physical assessment helps the RT move from “the patient says they are short of breath” to “what signs can I observe, feel, tap, and hear that help explain the problem?” Early in training, the goal is not to diagnose independently. The goal is to recognize patterns and communicate findings clearly.

RT Clinical Connection

Assessment findings usually make the most sense when they are viewed together. One finding may be nonspecific, but a cluster of findings can point toward obstruction, fluid, collapse, consolidation, or air in the pleural space.

Quick Pre-Check

A patient has very decreased breath sounds on the right side and the right chest sounds hyperresonant to percussion. What does this finding cluster suggest?

The Bedside Assessment Sequence

Physical assessment is easiest to learn as a step-by-step sequence. Start by looking, then use your hands, then tap the chest, then listen with a stethoscope.

StepPlain LanguageWhat the RT Learns
InspectionLook at the patient.Breathing effort, posture, symmetry, skin color, and distress.
PalpationUse your hands to feel.Chest expansion, tenderness, crepitus, tracheal position, and vibration.
PercussionTap over lung areas.Whether the area seems air-filled, fluid-filled, dense, or overinflated.
AuscultationListen with a stethoscope.Air movement and abnormal sounds such as wheezes or crackles.

Inspection: What You See First

Inspection means looking carefully before touching the patient. The RT notices whether breathing appears comfortable or difficult. Always compare both sides of the chest.

Normal clues
Relaxed breathing, symmetric chest rise, normal skin color, calm appearance, able to speak comfortably.
Abnormal clues
Accessory muscle use, retractions, tripod posture, cyanosis, anxiety, fatigue, unequal chest movement.

Palpation: What You Feel

Palpation means using your hands to gather information. In pulmonary assessment, palpation helps evaluate chest expansion, tracheal position, tenderness, crepitus, and tactile fremitus.

Palpation FindingWhat It MeansWhy RTs Care
Symmetric chest expansionBoth sides rise similarly.Suggests similar ventilation to both lungs.
Asymmetric expansionOne side moves less.May suggest pneumothorax, atelectasis, pain, obstruction, or effusion.
Tactile fremitusVibration felt when the patient speaks.Increased with dense/consolidated lung; decreased when air or fluid separates the lung from the chest wall.
Tracheal deviationThe trachea is not centered in the neck.A shift can be a red flag. Pressure processes may push the trachea away; volume loss can pull it toward the affected side.
CrepitusCrackling under the skin.May suggest subcutaneous air after trauma, chest tube issues, or air leak.

Board Pearl

Increased fremitus often points toward consolidation because sound vibration travels better through dense tissue. Decreased fremitus can occur when air or fluid blocks vibration transmission. Tracheal position is a separate clue: a new shift away from midline is a red flag and should not be ignored.

Tracheal Deviation: A Palpation Red Flag

To check tracheal position, gently palpate near the suprasternal notch and compare whether the trachea feels centered. A new shift away from midline can suggest a serious pressure problem, while a shift toward one side may occur with volume loss. Early learners do not need to diagnose the cause yet — the key is to recognize that a new tracheal shift should be communicated promptly.

DirectionClinical MeaningExamples to Consider
MidlineExpected normal position.Normal or less severe/asymmetric findings.
Shift away from affected sideSomething may be pushing structures away.Large pneumothorax under pressure or large pleural effusion.
Pull toward affected sideSomething may be pulling structures toward volume loss.Atelectasis or significant lung volume loss.

Voice Transmission Note

Vocal fremitus is felt during palpation, while bronchophony, egophony, and whispered pectoriloquy are auscultated voice-transmission findings. The audio practice for those voice sounds has been moved into the auscultation section so all listenable sounds are grouped together.

Auscultation: Listening for Air Movement and Voice Transmission

Auscultation means listening with a stethoscope. The RT compares side-to-side, listens over multiple lung regions, and asks the patient to take slow deep breaths through the mouth when possible. This lesson now includes normal breath sounds, abnormal continuous and discontinuous breath sounds, and voice-transmission sounds.

Disease Pattern Recognition

Common cardiopulmonary diseases often create recognizable clusters of findings. This does not mean one finding diagnoses the patient. It means the RT uses the pattern to decide what to report, reassess, or investigate next.

How to Think About Patterns

Ask: Is there too much air, not enough air, fluid, dense tissue, collapse, or airway narrowing? Then match the physical findings to the likely process.

ProcessCommon Finding Logic
Airway narrowingWheezing, prolonged exhalation, sometimes hyperresonance from air trapping.
Air in pleural spaceHyperresonance, decreased fremitus, decreased or absent breath sounds.
Fluid in pleural spaceDull percussion, decreased fremitus, decreased or absent breath sounds.
Dense lung tissue/consolidationDull percussion, increased fremitus, bronchial breath sounds or crackles.
Fluid in alveoli/interstitiumCrackles, possible dullness at bases, increased work of breathing.
Pressure shift / tracheal deviationTrachea may shift away from a large pressure process and should be treated as a red flag when new or associated with distress.

FAQ

Common Student Questions

Do I need to diagnose the disease from physical assessment alone?

No. Early learners should focus on recognizing patterns and reporting findings clearly. Diagnosis requires the full clinical picture, provider assessment, and diagnostic data.

Why do we compare side-to-side?

Comparing right and left lung fields helps reveal asymmetry. One-sided findings are often more concerning than findings that are mild and equal on both sides.

What is the easiest way to remember percussion tones?

Resonant is normal air-filled lung. Dull suggests fluid/density/collapse. Hyperresonant suggests too much air.

What if I cannot hear breath sounds well?

Check stethoscope position, listen on skin instead of clothing, ask for slow deep breaths if appropriate, reduce background noise, and compare side-to-side.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Physical assessment helps RTs identify how the patient looks, feels, sounds, and responds. The key is not memorizing one isolated clue; it is recognizing clusters of findings and understanding what those clusters suggest.

Must know:
Inspect, palpate, percuss, then auscultate.
Pattern pearl:
Dullness suggests density/fluid/collapse; hyperresonance suggests extra air.
RT pearl:
Compare side-to-side and report major asymmetry promptly.
Red flag:
Sudden one-sided absent breath sounds with distress or falling SpO₂ is concerning.
