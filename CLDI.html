Chronic Lung Disease of Infancy

Chronic lung disease of infancy, often called bronchopulmonary dysplasia, is a chronic respiratory disorder most often seen in premature infants who required prolonged oxygen, positive pressure, or mechanical ventilation. This module focuses on RT-level recognition, gas exchange support, ventilator strategy, oxygen safety, nutrition-growth connections, and escalation when respiratory reserve is limited.

Disease Snapshot

Chronic lung disease of infancy develops when immature lungs are exposed to injury during a critical period of growth. The result is reduced pulmonary reserve, abnormal alveolar development, increased work of breathing, and prolonged need for oxygen or respiratory support.

What it is:
Chronic respiratory disease of prematurity with persistent oxygen or support needs.
Primary problem:
Immature lungs plus inflammation, oxygen exposure, and pressure or volume injury.
Classic signs:
Tachypnea, retractions, oxygen dependence, feeding difficulty, poor growth, and episodic desaturation.
Diagnostic clue:
Prematurity history with persistent oxygen requirement and chronic changes on chest imaging.
Acute danger:
Apnea, bradycardia, rising CO₂, worsening FiO₂ requirement, or pulmonary hypertension signs.
RT priority:
Balance adequate oxygenation with lung-protective support and careful monitoring.

RT Clinical Connection

For RTs, chronic lung disease of infancy is a reserve problem. These infants may look stable at baseline but deteriorate quickly with feeding fatigue, viral infection, airway obstruction, pulmonary hypertension, or excessive ventilatory demand.

Board Prep Frame

Immediately connect chronic lung disease of infancy with prematurity, prolonged oxygen or ventilator exposure, impaired alveolar development, persistent oxygen need, careful saturation targeting, pulmonary hypertension risk, and slow support weaning.

Anatomy & Pathophysiology

CLDI occurs when immature lungs are injured before normal alveolar and vascular development is complete. Inflammation, oxygen exposure, ventilator pressure, infection, fluid overload, and poor growth can all worsen the disease process.

Prematurity → immature alveoli and capillaries → oxygen/pressure/inflammation exposure → disrupted alveolarization + vascular remodeling → decreased reserve → chronic oxygen need, tachypnea, retractions, feeding fatigue, and pulmonary hypertension risk.

What Changes in CLDI

Impaired alveolar growthFewer and larger simplified alveoli may develop.Less surface area for gas exchange.
InflammationAirways and lung tissue remain irritated.Increases oxygen need and work of breathing.
Vascular remodelingPulmonary vessels may develop abnormally.Raises pulmonary hypertension risk.
Airway reactivitySmall airways may narrow or obstruct.Can cause wheeze, air trapping, and episodic deterioration.
Poor respiratory reserveInfant has limited ability to tolerate stress.Feeding, infection, or agitation may cause desaturation.

Danger Sign

An infant with CLDI who has increasing oxygen requirement, apnea/bradycardia, rising PaCO₂, poor feeding, or worsening retractions needs urgent reassessment.

Etiology, Triggers & Risk Factors

Chronic lung disease of infancy is not a trigger-based asthma process. It develops when premature lungs are exposed to inflammation, oxygen, pressure, volume, infection, or fluid stress during a period when the alveoli and pulmonary blood vessels are still developing. These infants may have limited reserve even when they look stable.

Risk Factor or StressorWhy It MattersRT Teaching Focus
Extreme prematurityAlveoli and pulmonary capillaries are structurally immature.Expect reduced reserve and close monitoring needs.
Prolonged oxygen exposureHigh oxygen exposure can contribute to oxidative lung injury.Titrate oxygen to ordered saturation targets instead of using unnecessary hyperoxia.
Mechanical ventilationFragile lungs are vulnerable to pressure and volume injury.Use lung-protective support and monitor pressures, volumes, and clinical response.
Infection or sepsisInflammation increases oxygen demand and can worsen gas exchange.Watch for rising FiO₂ need, apnea, worsening retractions, or temperature instability.
PDA or fluid overloadExtra pulmonary blood flow or edema can worsen compliance and oxygenation.Trend breath sounds, oxygen requirement, chest imaging, and response to therapy.
Poor nutrition and growthBreathing requires energy; poor growth can worsen respiratory muscle endurance.Coordinate support around feeding and watch for feeding-related desaturation.
Viral respiratory illnessRSV and other viruses can overwhelm limited respiratory reserve.Monitor closely for apnea, bradycardia, and escalation of support.

Clinical Manifestations

Infants with CLDI cannot report increased work of breathing, dyspnea, or activity limitation the way older patients can. Assessment depends on observable signs: breathing pattern, oxygen trend, feeding tolerance, growth, alertness, and response to handling or support.

Finding TypeWhat the RT May Observe
Respiratory patternTachypnea, nasal flaring, head bobbing, grunting, subcostal/intercostal retractions, or prolonged expiratory phase.
Oxygenation cluesDesaturation during feeding, sleep, crying, handling, or routine care.
Breath soundsCoarse crackles, diminished aeration, wheezing from airway reactivity, or uneven air entry.
Feeding toleranceFatigue, sweating, tachypnea, cough, choking, or desaturation during feeds.
Growth concernsPoor weight gain because breathing effort increases caloric demand.
Ventilation cluesApnea, bradycardia, rising PaCO₂, decreasing alertness, or worsening respiratory acidosis.

Important Teaching Point

An infant who becomes quiet, fatigued, less responsive, or apneic may be worsening even if obvious retractions appear to decrease. Less effort can mean the infant is tiring, not improving.

Diagnostics & Assessment

Infants with CLDI are not assessed with oxygen support trend zones or routine spirometry. RT assessment focuses on oxygen requirement, work of breathing, ventilation status, growth, chest imaging, feeding tolerance, and cardiopulmonary complications.

Monitoring Tools

Assessment ToolWhy It Matters
Continuous SpO₂ monitoringTracks baseline oxygenation and detects desaturation during sleep, feeding, crying, or handling.
FiO₂ and flow trendA rising oxygen requirement may be an early sign of deterioration.
Blood gas analysisEvaluates ventilation, CO₂ retention, respiratory acidosis, and response to support.
Chest radiographMay show hyperinflation, atelectasis, diffuse chronic changes, or interstitial markings.
Growth monitoringPoor growth may reflect high work of breathing and increased caloric need.
Feeding assessmentIdentifies fatigue, aspiration risk, tachypnea, or desaturation during feeding.
EchocardiographyEvaluates pulmonary hypertension when oxygen need or clinical course is concerning.

Blood Gas Patterns

Chest Imaging

Chest x-ray findings vary by severity and timing. RTs should connect imaging with bedside status: oxygen requirement, work of breathing, breath sounds, blood gas trend, and response to respiratory support.

Severity & Red Flags

The key question is not whether the infant sounds “wheezy.” The key question is whether the infant has enough reserve to maintain oxygenation, ventilation, feeding, growth, and safe work of breathing.

Apnea or bradycardiaCan indicate severe instability, hypoxemia, or respiratory fatigue.
Increasing oxygen requirementSuggests worsening gas exchange or reduced reserve.
Rising PaCO₂Indicates worsening ventilation and possible fatigue.

Management & Treatment

CLDI management focuses on supporting oxygenation and ventilation while minimizing additional lung injury. Care is individualized and often includes respiratory support, nutrition, growth support, infection prevention, and monitoring for pulmonary hypertension.

Common Support Strategies

Therapy or StrategyPurposeRT Focus
Supplemental oxygenMaintains adequate tissue oxygenation.Titrate to ordered targets and avoid unnecessary hyperoxia.
Heated humidificationProtects fragile infant airways and secretion clearance.Confirm correct humidification for the device and flow.
HFNC or CPAPReduces work of breathing and supports lung recruitment.Monitor flow/pressure, FiO₂, skin integrity, and clinical response.
Mechanical ventilationSupports severe respiratory failure or exhaustion.Use lung-protective concepts and trend gases/pressures.
Diuretics when orderedMay reduce pulmonary edema and improve compliance in selected infants.Monitor response, electrolytes, hydration, and breath sounds with the team.
Bronchodilator trial when orderedMay help if airway reactivity is present.Assess objective response rather than assuming all infants benefit.
Nutritional supportImproves growth and respiratory muscle endurance.Coordinate care to reduce feeding fatigue and desaturation.

Escalation Concept

Escalation is based on the whole picture: oxygen requirement, respiratory effort, blood gas trend, apnea/bradycardia, feeding tolerance, alertness, and whether the infant can maintain stability during routine care.

CLDI RT Clinical Management Framework

Respiratory therapists manage chronic lung disease of infancy (CLDI) by assessing respiratory effort and oxygenation, identifying worsening fatigue or ventilation failure, supporting oxygen delivery and lung recruitment, reassessing response, and escalating support when respiratory reserve declines.

Respiratory Failure & Ventilator Considerations

Some infants with severe CLDI develop progressive respiratory failure requiring noninvasive or invasive support. RT management focuses on lung protection, adequate oxygenation, careful ventilation, and minimizing additional pressure, volume, and oxygen injury.

Watch for:
Apnea, bradycardia, rising PaCO₂, worsening acidosis, severe retractions, head bobbing, and reduced responsiveness.
Vent goal:
Support ventilation and oxygenation while avoiding excessive pressure, volume, and FiO₂.
Settings concept:
Use lung-protective support, adequate expiratory time, and close gas/pressure monitoring.
Ventilator GoalWhy It Matters
Avoid excessive tidal volumesReduces volutrauma risk.
Limit high pressuresReduces barotrauma risk in fragile lungs.
Provide adequate expiratory timeHelps reduce air trapping when airway reactivity is present.
Carefully titrate oxygenBalances hypoxemia risk with oxygen toxicity risk.
Trend blood gases and clinical statusDetects worsening ventilation before collapse.

Board Pearl

For infants with CLDI, ventilator decisions are not based on adult asthma labels. Watch the infant’s reserve: oxygen requirement, PaCO₂ trend, pH, apnea/bradycardia, work of breathing, and fatigue.

FAQ

Common Chronic Lung Disease of Infancy Questions

What is the simplest way to understand chronic lung disease of infancy?

CLDI is chronic respiratory disease in premature infants whose lungs were injured or stressed while still developing. The main problem is limited reserve: the infant may need oxygen or support for a long time and may worsen quickly with feeding, infection, or fatigue.

How is CLDI different from asthma?

Asthma is usually an episodic, trigger-driven airway disease with reversible bronchospasm. CLDI is rooted in prematurity, disrupted alveolar/vascular development, oxygen exposure, inflammation, and ventilator-related injury. Some infants may have airway reactivity, but the disease is not simply infant asthma.

Why is a rising PaCO₂ dangerous in CLDI?

A rising PaCO₂ can mean the infant is no longer ventilating well. When it appears with fatigue, apnea, bradycardia, or acidosis, it may signal impending respiratory failure.

Why does feeding matter so much?

Feeding increases oxygen demand and coordination work. A fragile infant may desaturate, sweat, become tachypneic, or fatigue during feeds, making feeding tolerance an important marker of reserve.

What does oxygen support tell us?

Oxygen support is a trend. A rising flow or FiO₂ requirement, more frequent desaturation, or inability to tolerate handling suggests worsening reserve and the need for reassessment.

When should pulmonary hypertension be considered?

Consider pulmonary hypertension when an infant with CLDI has persistent or worsening oxygen need, poor growth, recurrent desaturation, poor response to support, or signs of cardiopulmonary strain.

What is the RT’s biggest safety priority?

The RT should balance adequate oxygenation and ventilation with lung protection. That means monitoring oxygen targets, work of breathing, blood gases, device settings, humidification, and escalation signs.

Summary & Clinical Pearls

Chronic lung disease of infancy is a prematurity-related disorder marked by impaired lung development, chronic oxygen or support needs, reduced pulmonary reserve, and risk for decompensation during feeding, infection, agitation, or growth failure. RT care centers on oxygenation, ventilation, support devices, feeding tolerance, growth, and early recognition of deterioration.

Must know:
CLDI is rooted in prematurity and disrupted alveolar/vascular development, not adult asthma physiology.
Board pearl:
Increasing FiO₂ need, apnea/bradycardia, and rising PaCO₂ are high-risk findings.
RT pearl:
Trend oxygen requirement, work of breathing, feeding tolerance, growth, and blood gases.
Common mistake:
Do not use adult markers like speaking in full sentences, oxygen support trend, or oxygenation and ventilation trend to assess an infant with CLDI.
